# The CTL Sound Award: 10 Reviews

**CULTURETECHLENS · "Culture, Clearly Seen." · Black Cultural Intelligence**

*Ten albums from the 2026 shortlist, reviewed the way the internet reviews albums — scored out of 100, no punches pulled, every opinion owned. These scores are the reviewer's alone. They play no role in the CTL Sound Award's 40/30/30 scoring model, which draws only on published critical aggregates, documented cultural-impact signals, and CTL evidence depth. Think of this as the barbershop next door to the archive.*

## 1. Ezra Collective — *Here Because of Hope*

**Lane: Jazz · Reviewer's score: 92/100**

The highest Metacritic number on the shortlist is a 90, and it belongs to five Londoners who spent the decade dragging jazz back into rooms it had been politely escorted out of. There's a version of this album that is a victory lap — Mercury winners, guest list like a Black Atlantic group chat (Lila Iké, Leona Lewis, Libianca, Pa Salieu, Letitia Wright) — and to the band's credit, it never plays like one. It plays like an argument: that jazz was never the museum piece, you were just in the wrong wing.

The guest list could have been a compilation album wearing a band's name. Instead the features feel like weather systems the horns learn to navigate. This is the consensus pick that actually deserves the consensus, which almost never happens. The jazz lane rarely gets a consensus entry this strong in an American awards conversation, and the reason is usually that American awards conversations are grading jazz on a rubric built for something else. Here the rubric caught up.

**Verdict:** The frontrunner that earned it. December's to lose.

## 2. Genesis Owusu — *REDSTAR WU & THE WORLDWIDE SCOURGE*

**Lane: Hip-hop (alternative) · Reviewer's score: 90/100**

Metacritic 88, and the second-strongest verified signal on the board belongs to a Ghanaian-Australian artist making a record that refuses every container you try to put it in. The eligibility rule that includes this album — "the artist is Black, or the album's primary cultural contribution sits in a Black music tradition" — is doing exactly the work it was written to do. Black music was never a zip code.

Owusu raps, sings, contorts, and stages the whole thing like a transmission from a world with better lighting. The 81 on the aggregator's user side (5,583 ratings) tells you the audience found it; the 88 tells you the critics had to admit they found it too. Records like this are why "alternative" lanes exist — not as a consolation bracket, but because the center's vocabulary genuinely cannot describe what's happening here.

**Verdict:** The most *interesting* record on the shortlist, and interesting is winning a lot of Decembers lately.

## 3. Vince Staples — *Cry Baby*

**Lane: Hip-hop · Reviewer's score: 89/100**

Seventh album, first one independent after Def Jam, and it sounds like somebody exhaled for the first time in a decade. Metacritic 87 across 14 critics — universal acclaim, near-total consensus — for a record threading politically charged rap through rock and punk elements, recorded loose, like permission was no longer something you applied for.

Staples has always been the most quotable pessimist in rap; the trick of *Cry Baby* is that the pessimism got *playful*. The independence isn't just a business detail, it's the album's whole sonic posture — the sound of an artist who stopped writing for the building. Consequence gave it an A-. Consequence was being conservative.

**Verdict:** The "finally" album. Fourteen critics can't be wrong, but they can be late.

## 4. Isaiah Rashad — *It's Been Awful*

**Lane: Hip-hop · Reviewer's score: 87/100**

Teased with a trailer in April, carried through the summer on a Tiny Desk (August 25) and a tour with a great name (Lil Sunny's Awful Road Trip Tour), landed as one of the year's most complete rap records. Metacritic 83, nine critics, universal acclaim. The guest list — SZA, Dominic Fike, Julian Sintonia — maps exactly where Rashad sits: the Southern rap traditionalist with indie instincts and a fanbase that treats every release like a family reunion.

The title is doing a lot of heavy lifting and the album carries it. Rashad's whole career has been about making struggle sound *smooth*, which is a magic trick most rappers attempt once and retire from. The 7.8 from Pitchfork feels like they heard a different, slightly worse album. They didn't. This is the one.

**Verdict:** TDE's quietest star made the loudest complete statement.

## 5. J. Cole — *The Fall-Off*

**Lane: Hip-hop · Reviewer's score: 84/100**

The most mythologized rap album of the decade finally arrived and it is *a lot*: double disc, DISC29/DISC39, 24 tracks, 101 minutes, Future to Erykah Badu to Petey Pablo to Burna Boy. The audience showed up — an 80 user score across 17,112 ratings, one of the most-rated rap albums of the year — and the critical establishment responded by... not producing a citable aggregate. No Metacritic. chorus.reviews gave it a 70, which in context reads like a shrug with footnotes.

Here's the honest review: it's too long, it's occasionally exhausting, and it contains some of the best rapping anybody did this year. Both things are true. The bloat is real — 101 minutes is a miniseries — but the highs are high enough that the "too long" complaint feels like complaining about the size of the feast. Cole made the album his mythology demanded. Mythology is heavy. It shows.

**Verdict:** A cultural event that happens to contain a very good album. The December question — how much can impact alone carry? — is the program's most interesting one.

## 6. Denzel Curry & Kenny Beats — *ii*

**Lane: Hip-hop · Reviewer's score: 88/100**

Nine tracks. Twenty-four minutes. The sequel to 2020's *Unlocked*, and the shortlist's most beloved rap record by the numbers that exist: 84 user score across 12,389 ratings, 8/10 from Clash, 8/10 from Fantano, and a 9.5 from hiphophighsociety — which, in that publication's economy, is basically a marriage proposal. JPEGMAFIA, Westside Gunn, and Yebba in support.

The case for *ii* is efficiency, and efficiency is the whole review: no wasted minutes, no skippable stretches, a producer-rapper partnership six years into operating at full trust. In a hip-hop lane crowded with sprawl — *The Fall-Off* is 101 minutes; this is 24 — brevity isn't just an aesthetic, it's an argument. Every minute is load-bearing.

**Verdict:** The best 24 minutes of rap this year. Not the most — the best.

## 7. Yaya Bey — *Fidelity*

**Lane: R&B / Soul · Reviewer's score: 86/100**

Metacritic 83, tied for the fourth-strongest verified signal on the shortlist, earned without the major-label machinery behind most of its lane-mates. Bey has built one of the most respected independent catalogs in contemporary R&B, and *Fidelity* plays like the record that whole catalog was building toward — intimate, precisely written, completely uninterested in chasing the center.

The honest limitation is the impact pillar: independent R&B rarely generates the chart peaks or award nominations that make impact easy to document. But listen to the thing. This is the R&B lane's consensus pick because it's the R&B lane's *best* pick — the album that understands the assignment of the genre (say the true thing beautifully) and refuses every shortcut around it.

**Verdict:** The connoisseur's choice that doesn't need the word "connoisseur."

## 8. Thundercat — *Distracted*

**Lane: Jazz (jazz-funk crossover) · Reviewer's score: 83/100**

Six years after *It Is What It Is*, Thundercat returned with a record sprawling across every tradition he touches — jazz-funk, R&B, psychedelia, yacht rock — and a guest list to match: Channel Tres, Willow Smith, The Lemon Twigs, A$AP Rocky, Tame Impala, Lil Yachty, Flying Lotus, and a posthumous Mac Miller appearance on "She Knows Too Much." Metacritic 81, ten reviews, universal acclaim. Paste gave it a C+, which tells you more about Paste's relationship with joy than about the album.

Thundercat albums are like his bass playing: technically absurd, emotionally direct, and funny in a way that keeps getting mistaken for not-serious. The sprawl is the point and occasionally the problem — this is a buffet, and buffets don't have arcs. But the highs ("She Knows Too Much" alone) justify the wandering.

**Verdict:** A joyful mess from a genius who refuses to tidy up. Correct decision.

## 9. Jill Scott — *To Whom This May Concern*

**Lane: R&B / Soul · Reviewer's score: 85/100**

Eleven years after *Woman*. Topped the UK R&B Albums chart in week one. Eleven publications reviewed it, seven at 8.0 — one of the broadest review spreads of the year, which is what happens when a legacy artist makes a record nobody can dismiss and everybody hears slightly differently. The guest list reads like a syllabus: Ab-Soul, JID, Tierra Whack, Too $hort, Common, Lupe Fiasco.

What the scores describe is a veteran operating with total command — not chasing the current R&B sound but reminding it where it came from. The AnyDecentMusic 7.5 aggregate undersells the warmth of the individual notices; this is an 8.0 record wearing a 7.5 average because consensus is a blunt instrument. Eleven years was worth the wait, and the wait is part of the text.

**Verdict:** The grown-folks album of the year, and "grown-folks" is a compliment.

## 10. Nia Archives — *Emotional Junglist*

**Lane: Diaspora & electronic · Reviewer's score: 87/100**

The Bradford-born producer's debut full-length carries jungle — a Black British tradition — into the album format, with Sampha and Jorja Smith along for the ride. Metacritic 80, plus a Mercury Prize 2026 shortlist spot (winner decided October 22 in Newcastle). The dual recognition — critical aggregate *and* peer-judged shortlist — makes this the diaspora lane's most credentialed entry.

Jungle rarely appears in American album-of-the-year conversations at all, which is precisely the failure the diaspora lane exists to correct. Archives makes the tradition's case without making it a history lesson: this is a *debut* that sounds like an arrival, bassweight with songcraft, the underground learning to sing. The Mercury judges and CTL's lanes found the same record independently. When two different systems point at the same thing, pay attention.

**Verdict:** The future of the shortlist's past. Jungle's American coronation starts here.
