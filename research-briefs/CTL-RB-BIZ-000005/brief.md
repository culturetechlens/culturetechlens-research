### 01 Executive Summary

When a major community institution closes, the effects are not one thing but several, arriving on different clocks. Four Chicago cases with evidenced closures and aftereffects; every effect tagged **DEMONSTRATED** (sources trace the mechanism) or **PLAUSIBLE** (reasonably inferred).

1. **Chicago Public Schools, 2013.** On May 22, 2013 the Board of Education closed 49 elementary schools and one high school program, displacing nearly 12,000 students — the largest single-year U.S. school closure to that date. UChicago Consortium: displaced students stayed two months behind in math four years later; 88% were African-American. Ewing's ethnography (*Ghosts in the Schoolyard*, 2018) documents the loss of schools as Bronzeville gathering spaces.
2. **Michael Reese Hospital, Bronzeville (closed 2008).** Closure announced June 2008; Chapter 11 that September; demolished 2009. Its adult trauma center closed in 1991 (UChicago Hospital's ended 1988); the South Side went 27 years without a Level I adult trauma center. A 2013 AJPH study linked >5-mile trauma distance to greater mortality; a JAMA study found a 3.9% decrease in South Side firearm deaths after the May 1, 2018 Level I opening. The 48-acre campus sat vacant 15+ years; the city paid ~$13M/year in debt service on its $91M purchase.
3. **Walmart, four Chicago stores (closed April 16, 2023).** Chatham Supercenter (+ Health center, Academy), Kenwood, Lakeview, Little Village. Documented effects: a mayoral policy response (municipally owned grocery exploration, Sept 13, 2023), emergency food distribution at the shuttered Chatham store, USDA-documented food-access gaps. Spillovers to surrounding businesses: no source found — **UNRESOLVED**.
4. **Whole Foods, Englewood (closed 2022).** Opened 2016 on a $10M TIF and $1 land sale; closed 2022; replaced January 2023 by a Save-A-Lot over community objection; on July 25, 2026 all seven Yellow Banana Chicago stores closed, including Englewood — a documented double-loss cycle.

**WHAT THE EVIDENCE ESTABLISHES:** traced effects — academic harm, longer trauma transports with mortality consequences, emergency food-access gaps, failed replacements, public costs of vacancy. **WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUGGESTS:** communities experience closures as identity loss and civic abandonment; subsidized replacements chosen without community input fail faster. **WHAT REMAINS DISPUTED OR UNKNOWN:** the "corridor declines when the anchor closes" narrative — no case documented it with named businesses, dates, numbers; crime's true weight in grocery closures is contested. **WHAT CTL SHOULD RESEARCH NEXT:** trace the ~50 closed school buildings; test Chatham spillovers with business-license data; track the municipal-grocery experiment (§22).

---

### 02 Primary Research Question

What documented effects can follow the loss of a major community institution?

Sub-questions: (a) What kinds of effects are traceable? (b) On what time horizons? (c) Which are DEMONSTRATED vs. PLAUSIBLE? (d) Where does the evidence run out?

---

### 03 Why It Matters

Anchor losses — groceries, schools, hospitals — concentrate on Chicago's South and West Sides, overwhelmingly in Black neighborhoods. Before CTL-DR-ECON-001 theorizes the Black business ecosystem's cycles of building and loss, this pilot establishes what can be documented about what happens *after* an anchor closes.

---

### 04 Scope

- **In scope:** four Chicago closures with documented effects — public schools (2013), a safety-net hospital (2008), a big-box retailer (2023), a subsidized grocery anchor (2022, plus its 2026 replacement failure). One national comparison case where it illuminates mechanism.
- **Out of scope:** why anchors close (treated only where cause links to effect); full institutional histories; ECON-001's capital-flows treatment (deferred to §22).
- **Time bounds:** closures 2008–2026; effects traced through September 2026.
- **Deliberate exclusion:** bank-branch closures — no case found meeting this brief's rigor bar; deferred to §22.

---

### 05 Definitions

**Anchor (operational).** An institution counts as an anchor here if it meets at least two of: (a) provides a service/good with few nearby substitutes; (b) is a significant local employer or foot-traffic generator; (c) functions as a regular gathering or civic space. Adapted from the anchor-institution literature: place-bound organizations "tied to their surroundings by mission, invested capital, or relationships," whose scale produces "significant economic impact in their surrounding community" (Healthcare Anchor Network; Democracy Collaborative).

**DEMONSTRATED vs PLAUSIBLE.** DEMONSTRATED: a source names the closure and traces a specific effect to it. PLAUSIBLE: reasonably inferred, untraced. Every §08 effect claim is tagged **[D]** or **[P]**.

**Food desert.** USDA-based distance-to-grocery measure (>½ mile in the Chicago figures cited), not a diet or health claim.

---

### 06 Historical Context

These closures sit inside a longer Chicago pattern. Michael Reese withered from 1,100 beds (1998) to 150 (2007): slow decline preceding the sudden event. The South Side lost adult trauma capacity in stages (Michael Reese 1991; UChicago Hospital 1988), producing the "trauma desert" activists protested for 27 years until UChicago Medicine's Level I center opened May 1, 2018.

The 2013 closures were themselves an endpoint: late-1990s/early-2000s demolition of Bronzeville-area public housing (Ida B. Wells, Robert Taylor, Ickes, Stateway Gardens) displaced thousands of Black families; enrollment fell; the district declared buildings "underutilized." Ewing documents the community experiencing the vote as the latest in a sequence of state actions restructuring Black space.

Both grocery cases were failed anchor-*creation* strategies: Walmart Chatham (opened 2012 after an eight-year effort) and Whole Foods Englewood ($10M TIF) were deliberate public anchor-building. Their closures are directly relevant to the city's municipally owned grocery exploration.

---

### 07 Key Findings

1. **Academic harm from school closure is the best-documented effect.** UChicago Consortium (May 2018): displaced students scored below expectations; math never rebounded — two months behind four years later. Mechanism: two-month merger timelines, lost materials, 21% of welcoming-school students not returning. **[D]**
2. **Trauma-access loss has mortality consequences, documented in both directions.** 27-year South Side Level I gap (transports to 10 miles/30 minutes); Crandall et al. (2013) linked >5-mile distance to greater mortality; JAMA found a 3.9% firearm-death decrease after the 2018 UChicago opening. **[D]** for the 1991 trauma-service loss — distinct from the 2008 hospital closure (local ED effects **UNRESOLVED**).
3. **Grocery-anchor failures produced a documented double-loss cycle.** Whole Foods Englewood: $10M subsidy → 6 years → closure → community-opposed Save-A-Lot → second closure July 2026. Walmart: four closures → emergency food distribution + mayoral policy initiative. **[D]**; economic spillover to surrounding businesses **UNRESOLVED**.
4. **Vacancy and public cost are documented where institutions were publicly entangled:** Michael Reese's 48 acres vacant 15+ years; $91M city purchase; ~$13M/year debt service. **[D]**
5. **Civic-space loss is documented ethnographically, not statistically:** Ewing's schools as "like community centers"; Englewood residents took the Whole Foods closure "as a sign that the city had given up on the community." **SUPPORTED** testimony, unquantified.
6. **The most-repeated claim has the least local evidence:** "when the anchor closed, the corridor declined" — with named businesses, dates, numbers — was documented in no case. The brief's central caution.

### 08 Evidence

Full claim records in `claim-ledger.json`.

**Case A — Chicago Public Schools mass closure, 2013**

- **The closure.** May 22, 2013: the Board voted to close 49 elementary schools and one high school program, displacing nearly 12,000 students — "the largest mass school closure to date" (EdWeek, on the Consortium's May 2018 report). *Count variance:* Consortium 2015 says 47; CTU counts 50 (see §20). **[D]**
- **Academic effects.** Displaced students scored below prior-achievement expectations: reading dipped then recovered by year two; math did not — students remained two months behind four years later. Absences, suspensions, GPA: no significant change (Consortium via EdWeek 2018). **[D]**
- **Disruption mechanism.** ~95 affected schools had two months to pack, move, hire, and absorb students. Teachers reported lost supplies/materials and setup-eaten instructional time. Fall 2013: 21% of welcoming-school students did not return, concentrated in 14 welcoming schools themselves forced to relocate. **[D]**
- **Who was affected.** 88% African-American; majority free/reduced-price meals; one-third housed disability programs (Consortium Jan 2015, via EdWeek 2015). **[D]**
- **Safety infrastructure.** Safe Passage (37 schools in 2012) added 51 schools post-closure — the district anticipating commute risk. **[D]**
- **Civic-space loss.** Ewing documents Bronzeville schools as "like community centers" — known teachers, events, post-graduation gatherings; closures mourned as loss of "a stable institution." The Dyett fight (2013 threat → 2015 strike → 2016 reopening) shows the anchor's civic weight. **[D]** as ethnography; unquantified.
- **Building fates.** **UNRESOLVED**.

**Case B — Michael Reese Hospital, Bronzeville (closed 2008)**

- **The closure.** Plans announced 2008-06-05; Chapter 11 filing 2008-09-29/30 (Modern Healthcare); closed 2008; demolished 2009. Beds: 1,100 (1998) → 150 (2007). **[D]**
- **The trauma-desert mechanism — distinct from the 2008 closure.** Michael Reese's adult trauma center closed 1991 (UChicago Hospital's ended 1988): 27 years with no South Side Level I center; victims transported up to 10 miles / 30 minutes (Daily Southtown; JEMS). Crandall et al. (2013, AJPH): >5 miles from trauma care → greater mortality. UChicago Medicine opened its Level I center 2018-05-01; JAMA: 3.9% decrease in South Side firearm deaths (Chicago Maroon). **[D]** in both directions. Do not conflate the 1991 trauma loss with the 2008 hospital closure.
- **National mechanism evidence (comparison).** Lee et al. (2015): per 1% of ED volume predicted to redistribute after closures, nearby EDs grew 0.80% — only ~80% reappeared; the rest is forgone care. **[D]** (peer-reviewed). A Sept 2026 *Journal of the American College of Surgeons* study (Brown/Rhode Island Hospital; via Becker's) of a 294-bed urban hospital closed 2018-01-01: essential surgical volume +20.2% at a neighboring hospital; payer mix shifted toward public insurance in the closed hospital's lower-white-share ZIP codes; interrupted-time-series found no significant immediate jump. **[D]** as comparison; not Chicago.
- **Vacancy and public cost.** City bought the 48-acre campus for $91M (2009) for a failed 2016 Olympic bid; vacant 15+ years; ~$13M/year debt service (Urbanize via Daily Line). **[D]**
- **2008 closure's employment/ED-crowding effects.** **UNRESOLVED** in numbers; UIC's Mapping Care documents the loss in kind (an institution that trained and employed Black nurses).

**Case C — Walmart Chicago closures, April 2023**

- **The closure.** Announced 2023-04-11; closed to the public by 2023-04-16: Chatham Supercenter + Health center + Academy (8431 S. Stewart Ave.), and the Kenwood, Lakeview, and Little Village Neighborhood Markets (4720 S. Cottage Grove Ave.; 2844 N. Broadway St.; 2551 W. Cermak Rd.). Walmart: Chicago stores never profitable in ~17 years; "tens of millions" in annual losses, nearly doubled in five years; theft/crime not leading causes, per spokesperson. Employees paid through 2023-08-11; severance for non-transfers. **[D]**
- **What Chatham was.** Opened September 2012 after an eight-year effort: 157,000 sq ft, ~350 jobs, full grocery + pharmacy — a food-desert intervention. **[D]**
- **Policy response.** 2023-09-13: Mayor Johnson announced exploration of a municipally owned grocery store with the Economic Security Project (city press release). **[D]** as response. USDA food-access figures (63.5% of West Englewood / 52% of East Garfield Park >½ mile from grocery; 37% of Black / 29% of Latine/x food insecure vs. 19% overall) describe conditions, not closure-caused effects.
- **Community emergency response.** My Block My Hood My City ran food distribution at the shuttered Chatham Walmart and sought the Walmart Academy facility for community use (City Bureau). **[D]**
- **Spillover to surrounding businesses / foot traffic.** **UNRESOLVED** — no source found.
- **Contested rationale.** Marynia Kolak (UIUC, via NextCity): chains use theft as "blanket rationale" for lower-income-neighborhood closures; closures reflect corporate growth-model dynamics. **[D]** as documented critique; the brief takes no position on crime's weight.

**Case D — Whole Foods Englewood (closed 2022); Save-A-Lot failure (closed 2026)**

- **Subsidized opening.** September 2016 at 63rd & Halsted; $10M TIF approved 2014; city sold land for $1 (NextCity via DPD); ≥100 planned jobs (CEGU). **[D]**
- **Limited uptake (pre-closure).** A 2021 study found "no significant changes in food and beverage availability and marketing" in Englewood's small stores one and two years after the store opened; 2020 resident interviews: many shoppers were "teachers and cops from outside the neighborhood" — the store read as gentrification (CEGU *Expositions*, UChicago). **[P]**-leaning: single study, student publication.
- **Closure.** April 2022: one of six national closures; closed 2022. Most residents learned days beforehand; residents "took the closure as a sign that the city had given up on the community" (CEGU). **[D]**
- **Replacement over objection.** 2023-01-01: Yellow Banana (owned by Cleveland's 127 Wall Holdings) began its Save-A-Lot lease, selected by Whole Foods as sublessee (Tribune via Connect CRE). Ald. Stephanie Coleman: "My community does not want a Save-A-Lot grocery store… We deserve quality, affordable fresh produce just as any other community." Asiaha Butler (R.A.G.E.): residents "excluded from the decision-making process." **[D]**
- **Second failure.** City committed >$13M toward a $25.2M renovation of six Yellow Banana Save-A-Lot stores (DPD); all six were open by April 2025. 2026-07-25: all seven Yellow Banana-operated Chicago stores closed — including 832 W 63rd St — after Save A Lot ended its licensing agreement in late June, citing financial distress and a 26% YoY decline in SNAP/EBT transactions (Progressive Grocer; Fox32). Yellow Banana had closed 38 non-Chicago stores and faced $2M+ vendor suits and $70k+ in penalties (Sun-Times via TheStreet). The city filed liens against each property; the deal had required a 10-year term (Inc.). **[D]**
- **Chain.** One subsidized anchor, two closures in four years; the imposed replacement failed faster than the original. Employment effects attested, not quantified. **[D]** for the chain; **[P]** for employment magnitude.

### 09 Analysis

**Four clocks.** *Immediate disruption* (school mergers; emergency food distribution); *service-access shifts* (trauma transport, grocery distance); *vacancy* over years–decades (Michael Reese's 15-year campus); *policy aftershocks* (municipal grocery exploration; $3.8B Bronzeville Lakefront fourteen years post-demolition).

**Substitution decides severity.** Worst where substitutes were thinnest: South Side trauma care (none for 27 years), Englewood grocery (the substitute failed twice). Where substitution was engineered (welcoming schools, Safe Passage), academic harm still occurred — substitution blunted, didn't prevent. Working hypothesis for ECON-001: severity ≈ (substitutability)⁻¹ × (speed of loss) × (pre-existing disinvestment).

**Subsidy doesn't inoculate.** Both grocery anchors were public-policy builds — eight years of effort for Walmart Chatham; $10M TIF + $1 land for Whole Foods; $13M+ for Save-A-Lot renovations. All failed. Documented mechanism in the Whole Foods case: unaffordable prices, outside shoppers, a replacement chosen without community input. Subsidy bought buildings, not rootedness.

**Causation discipline.** No case documents the "anchor closes → corridor businesses close" cascade with names and dates — a data gap, not a finding of no effect. Where mechanisms *are* traced, they are specific, dated, attributable.

**Who bears it.** Effects concentrate in Black neighborhoods: 88% of displaced CPS students African-American; one-third of closed schools housed disability programs; the trauma gap mapped onto the South Side; grocery losses onto USDA-measured gaps. The Providence comparison found the nearest ZIP codes had significantly lower white shares. This distributional pattern is the brief's most consistent finding.

### 19 Competing Interpretations

1. **"Closures follow decline" vs. "closures cause decline."** The district (underutilization, $1B deficit) and Walmart (17 years unprofitable) frame closures as responses; communities (Ewing; Englewood residents) frame them as causes of further harm. Both partially true: enrollment fell before 2013 (housing demolition displaced families), yet post-closure academic harm is demonstrated. Position: require sources to show the mechanism in each direction.
2. **Theft/crime as cause.** Walmart said theft wasn't leading; Kolak calls it a "blanket rationale"; others argue crime drove the business case. The brief takes no position on crime's weight.
3. **"Welcoming schools worked" vs. "closures harmed."** CPS leadership (2015) cited better attendance/behavior in welcoming schools; the Consortium's 2018 longitudinal analysis found net academic harm for displaced students. The brief weights the longitudinal finding.
4. **Replacement optimism vs. skepticism.** Whole Foods→Save-A-Lot is a natural experiment: the downgraded replacement failed faster. Not proof replacements always fail — but evidence against "any grocery beats none"; the community's stated preference (quality produce) was overridden, then vindicated.

### 21 Limitations

- **Selection bias:** cases selected *because* effects were documented; silent closures are invisible — cannot estimate how often anchor loss has no measurable effect.
- **Source asymmetry:** strongest evidence clusters on schools/hospitals; grocery effects rest more on journalism and testimony. Grades reflect this.
- **No counterfactuals** (closest: pre/post JAMA trauma study).
- **Geography:** all primary cases are Chicago South/West Side Black neighborhoods — may not generalize.
- **Recency:** Save-A-Lot closures are two months old; long-run effects unknowable.
- **Missing voices:** frontline workers thin; women's perspectives present (Butler, Coleman, Ewing, Crandall) but worker-level testimony underrepresented — flagged for ECON-001.
- **Rights:** visual provenance verified per item in `visuals.md`.
