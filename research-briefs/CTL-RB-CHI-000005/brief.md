# Chicago's Great Migration Geography
## CTL Research Brief CTL-RB-CHI-000005

**CULTURETECHLENS** — *Culture, Clearly Seen.* — Black Cultural Intelligence
*Chicago, c. 1916–1970*

**Research question:** Where did Black migrants settle in Chicago, and what constrained or enabled that settlement?

**Status:** Draft 1.0 (2026-09-28). Accuracy over completeness; uncertainty is marked, not hidden.

---

## 01 — What "settlement" means in this brief

"Settlement" here has three distinct layers, and they are not interchangeable. *Initial lodging* is where a migrant first slept: a relative's floor, a rooming house, a YMCA dormitory bed. *Neighborhood clustering* is where Black households concentrated once they stayed — the census-tract geography of the Black Belt, then Bronzeville, then the expanded South and West Sides. *Tenure form* is how they held the roof: renting whole apartments, renting single rooms (lodging), buying on contract, or owning outright. Much of the literature blurs these layers; this brief keeps them separate, because the forces that shaped a first address (a train station, a church referral) differ from the forces that shaped a neighborhood (a covenant, a bomb, a housing project).

## 02 — Wave one: who came, when, from where (c. 1916–1930)

The first wave began around 1916 and continued through the 1920s. More than 50,000 Black southerners arrived in Chicago between 1916 and 1919 alone, and the city's Black population rose from 44,103 in 1910 to 109,458 in 1920 — a 148 percent increase — reaching 233,903 by 1930. Migrants came chiefly from Mississippi, Arkansas, and Alabama, traveling the Illinois Central Railroad north. They were pulled by wartime industrial jobs and pushed by the debt-peonage economy of the cotton South. The Depression then slowed the movement sharply; the 1930s were a pause, not an end.

## 03 — Wave two: who came, when, from where (1940s–1960s)

The second wave opened with World War II and ran through the 1960s, far larger than the first. Black Chicago grew from roughly 278,000 in 1940 to 492,265 in 1950 and about 813,000 in 1960, reaching 1,102,620 (nearly a third of the city) by 1970. A 1944 estimate put the population at 337,000. Southern mechanization of cotton agriculture in the 1950s displaced farm labor and pushed a new generation north, while war-industry and postwar factory jobs pulled them. This wave, unlike the first, could not be contained in the old Black Belt — and the forces that failed to contain it reshaped the city's racial geography.

## 04 — First addresses: the station, the relative's floor, the rooming house

Most migrants arrived at the Illinois Central station at 12th Street and Michigan Avenue and settled nearby, close to South Side job districts — the stockyards, steel mills, and factories. Chain migration structured the first address: families pooled money for a single ticket and sent one member ahead, so the first "home" was often a relative's already-crowded apartment. Others went to rooming houses, which concentrated along Federal and Dearborn Streets, or to institutional lodging at the Wabash Avenue YMCA. Initial lodging was thus a function of the rail line, kin networks, and a thin institutional safety net — not of a free housing market.

## 05 — The 1919 Black Belt: where the first wave was confined

By 1919, most Black Chicagoans lived in a narrow district that NAACP investigator Walter White described that October as bounded by the railroad on the west, 30th Street on the north, 40th Street on the south, and Ellis Avenue on the east. Through the 1920s the majority lived between State Street, 31st Street, Cottage Grove Avenue, and 47th Street, according to Drake and Cayton's *Black Metropolis*. By the 1940s the commonly cited bounds were Cermak Road, Lake Michigan, South Chicago Avenue, and LaSalle Street. These shifting descriptions trace a single fact: the first wave was compressed into a small, dense South Side corridor, and each decade's "Black Belt" was slightly larger than the last — pressure, not choice, set its edges.

## 06 — Tenure in the first wave: renting, rooming, lodging

Homeownership was rare in the first wave; renting dominated, and subdividing dominated renting. Sixty-two percent of 274 Black families sampled in the 1920s took in lodgers, according to data from the Chicago Commission on Race Relations. The rent burden was racially skewed: one study found 80.7 percent of white families spent less than 20 percent of income on rent, while over 80 percent of Black families spent more than 23 percent. A September 1923 *Chicago Defender* notice described two to four married couples crowded into single seven-to-eight-bedroom apartments. Subdivision was the tenure logic of confinement: landlords extracted premium rents from a captive market, and tenants survived by stacking households.

## 07 — Reception infrastructure: the church and the YMCA

Two institutions absorbed the shock of arrival. Olivet Baptist Church, under Rev. Lacy Kirk Williams from 1916, directed migrants toward housing, provided childcare and what became the city's first kindergarten, and was described by scholar Wallace Best as a national model of migrant ministry; the congregation bought its building at 3101 S. King Drive in 1918. The Wabash Avenue YMCA at 3763 S. Wabash — a Julius Rosenwald-funded branch opened in 1913 — gave new arrivals lodging, job training, and education; the Association for the Study of Negro Life and History was founded there in 1915. Churches and the Black YMCA were the first wave's settlement infrastructure: they turned arrival into address.

## 08 — The Defender, chain migration, and what actually caused the movement

The *Chicago Defender* encouraged migration with front-page articles, success stories, and practical notices, and it circulated through the South on Illinois Central trains. But the paper's causal weight is debated: labor demand, chain migration, and Southern conditions did at least as much work. Families pooling ticket money and following relatives' letters was the dominant mechanism; the Defender amplified a movement more than it authored one. This brief treats the paper as an enabler of arrival, not its engine.

## 09 — Violence as a settlement constraint: bombs and the 1919 riot

The Black Belt's boundaries were policed by terror. From July 1917 to March 1921, Chicago saw 58 racial bombings tied to housing — 32 of them within 41st–60th Streets between Cottage Grove and State, the densest Black settlement zone. Walter White counted at least 20 bombings outside the district between January 1918 and August 1919, with two arrests and zero convictions. The July 27–August 3, 1919 riot — sparked by the drowning of Eugene Williams at the 29th Street beach — killed 38 people (23 Black, 15 white), injured 537, and left 1,000 to 2,000 homeless, most of them Black. Violence was not background noise; it was a boundary-enforcement system.

## 10 — Restrictive covenants and *Hansberry v. Lee*

Legal exclusion did what bombs did by other means. Restrictive covenants barred Black buyers and renters from most of the city's residential property (one often-repeated estimate says 80 percent, but the coverage figure is poorly sourced and should be treated as provisional). In 1940, *Hansberry v. Lee* struck down covenant enforcement in a Chicago case involving a Woodlawn covenant scheme — a landmark, but covenants remained socially potent until *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) made them nationally unenforceable. The legal architecture of the first wave was simple: you could live where Black people already lived, and nowhere else.

## 11 — Bronzeville: the Black Metropolis at its height

Inside the confinement, Black Chicago built a city-within-a-city. Bronzeville — the Black Metropolis — at its peak held over 300,000 residents in a roughly seven-mile strip from about 22nd to 63rd Street, State to Cottage Grove, with its commercial and institutional spine along streets like State, Wabash, Michigan, and Grand Boulevard. By mid-century roughly three-quarters of Black Chicagoans lived in this South Side chain of neighborhoods. One source claims over 90 percent of Black Chicagoans lived in Bronzeville by the 1930s — a definitional tension (neighborhood name versus census tracts) that this brief flags rather than resolves. Density was both achievement and symptom: a thriving Black economy built under siege.

## 12 — Kitchenettes: the signature tenure form

The kitchenette — a mansion or apartment subdivided into single-room units, often three to five families per original unit — was the Black Belt's signature tenure form. It was simultaneously an enabler and a constraint: it gave new arrivals an affordable first foothold, and it locked them into exploitative, overcrowded housing at premium rents. Rooming houses along Federal and Dearborn worked the same way. Richard Wright, who lived this geography, set *Native Son* (1940) in the Black Belt and documented kitchenette life in *12 Million Black Voices* (1941). Rare counter-examples existed: the Rosenwald Michigan Boulevard Garden Apartments (4618 S. Michigan, built 1929 for about $3 million) offered quality housing, but at a scale far below the need.

## 13 — The Depression pause and the 1940s hinge

The 1930s slowed migration and froze the Black Belt's geography; the 1940s broke it open. War-industry jobs restarted the flow, the Black population jumped from about 278,000 in 1940 to 492,265 in 1950, and the old corridor could no longer hold the numbers. The legal hinge was *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948): with covenants unenforceable, the question was no longer whether Black Chicago could expand but how — and at what price. The second wave's settlement story is the story of that expansion and its costs.

## 14 — Second-wave expansion: southward and westward

Expansion moved in two directions. On the South Side, the color line marched south: across 63rd Street, then 75th, then beyond 90th, as oral histories describe it — pioneers like the first Black family in Park Manor and Chatham arrivals such as Joe LaRue (1960) marking each stage. By 1960 Chicago had 14 majority-Black neighborhoods. On the West Side, a second Black Chicago took shape. The mechanisms differed from the first wave's confinement: blockbusting real-estate tactics turned white panic into rapid turnover, while mortgage discrimination kept ordinary Black buyers out of the conventional market. Expansion was real, and it was monetized by speculators.

## 15 — The West Side: a second Black Chicago

By 1950, census maps show West Side Black concentrations in blocks bounded by 16th Street, Roosevelt Road, Western Avenue, and State Street, and by Kinzie Street, California Avenue, Madison Street, and Ashland Avenue. North Lawndale became the emblematic second-wave neighborhood — over 90 percent Black by 1960 — and the emblematic case of predatory inclusion. The West Side matters to this brief's question because it breaks the South-Side-only frame: second-wave settlement was a two-front geography, and the institutions, churches, and block organizations of the West Side remain under-researched relative to Bronzeville.

## 16 — Public housing and the Neighborhood Composition Rule

The state built segregation into the second wave's geography. Under the CHA's "Neighborhood Composition Rule," the public housing of the 1950s and 1960s — 168 high-rise buildings and roughly 19,700 units — went overwhelmingly into predominantly Black South Side areas, with some West Side development. Ida B. Wells Homes were designated for Black families; Altgeld Gardens was an early Black-occupied development. The projects housed thousands of second-wave migrants and their children, but they also concentrated poverty where the color line had already drawn it. Pre-1950 CHA Black occupancy figures remain a documented gap.

## 17 — Trumbull Park: integration met with violence

When Betty and Donald Howard, a Black family, moved into the previously all-white Trumbull Park Homes on July 30, 1953, white neighbors launched years of sustained violence — riots, bombings, and harassment that continued until Black residents could move freely around roughly 1963. The episode, documented by historian Arnold Hirsch and the *Encyclopedia of Chicago*, is the second wave's sharpest proof that the end of legal covenants did not end violent boundary enforcement. Public housing integration was settlement, and settlement was contested block by block.

## 18 — Blockbusting and the end of covenants

After *Shelley*, speculators replaced covenants as the engine of turnover. Blockbusting — panic-peddling white homeowners into selling low, then reselling high to Black buyers shut out of conventional mortgages — accelerated neighborhood racial transition. The *Chicago Daily News* documented the tactics in Chatham in 1959: door-to-door warnings, staged "sold" signs, inflated fear. Blockbusting enabled Black expansion while taxing it: buyers paid inflated prices in transitioning neighborhoods, and the same mechanism that opened the South and West Sides stripped wealth from the people moving into them.

## 19 — Contract selling and the Contract Buyers League

Where mortgages were denied, contracts filled the gap — at a price. In North Lawndale, speculators bought houses cheap and resold them to Black buyers "on contract" at steep markups, with forfeiture clauses that wiped out all equity on a single missed payment. Clyde Ross, denied a mortgage, bought on contract in 1958 for $27,000 a house the seller had bought months earlier for $12,000 (one account gives 1961 and $26,000 — the conflict is unresolved). In 1968 Ross and hundreds of others formed the Contract Buyers League, withheld payments into escrow, and sued the speculators. Contract selling was the second wave's kitchenette: a tenure form that housed people the market excluded, on terms that extracted their wealth.

## 20 — What enabled settlement across both waves

Five enablers recur. First, the Illinois Central Railroad and its 12th Street station, which fixed the geography of arrival. Second, chain migration — pooled tickets, relatives' floors — which converted arrival into address. Third, reception institutions: Olivet Baptist and the Wabash YMCA in the first wave, and a denser web of churches, block clubs, and the Urban League in the second. Fourth, the Black press, especially the *Defender*, which circulated information and encouragement. Fifth, after 1948, the collapse of covenant enforceability, which — however predatorily mediated — made expansion legally possible. None of these enablers was sufficient alone; together they explain how a million people found roofs.

## 21 — What constrained settlement across both waves

Five constraints recur. First, restrictive covenants, which legally confined the first wave. Second, organized violence — 58 bombings, the 1919 riot, Trumbull Park — which enforced the covenants' boundaries. Third, mortgage and lending discrimination, which pushed buyers toward kitchenettes, rooming houses, and contract sales. Fourth, predatory tenure forms themselves: subdivision rents in the first wave, contract markups in the second. Fifth, public policy that concentrated rather than dispersed Black settlement — the CHA's siting rules and the FHA's discriminatory underwriting. (On redlining maps specifically: Chicago Fed research shows HOLC's maps were drawn after most HOLC lending was complete; they formalized racialized risk rather than directly controlling HOLC loans. FHA underwriting was the more direct mechanism.) The constraint system adapted: when law changed, violence and markets took up the work.

## 22 — Population and geography at a glance: the numbers with their limits

The arc: 44,103 (1910) → 109,458 (1920) → 233,903 (1930) → ~278,000 (1940) → 492,265 (1950) → ~813,000 (1960) → 1,102,620 (1970). The Black share of the city rose from about 2 percent to nearly 33 percent. Every figure here is SUPPORTED, not VERIFIED: the values converge across independent secondary sources citing the decennial censuses, but exact Census table provenance has not yet been checked in this brief, and the 1920 figure carries a 50-person variant (109,458 vs. 109,408). Boundary descriptions (White 1919; Drake and Cayton via CULtivate; CULtivate's 1940s maps) are snapshots from different years and methods — read them as a moving edge, not a fixed line. Claims about shares ("75 percent in the Black Belt," "90 percent in Bronzeville") reflect different geographic definitions and should never be mixed.

## 25 — Verdicts

### WHAT THE EVIDENCE ESTABLISHES

The two waves were demographically and geographically distinct: the first (c. 1916–1930) was confined to a dense South Side Black Belt by covenants and terror; the second (1940s–1960s) expanded southward past 90th Street and westward into North Lawndale and beyond, mediated by blockbusting, contract selling, and public-housing siting. Settlement in both waves ran through three layers — first lodging (kin, rooming houses, YMCA), neighborhood clustering (the moving edge of the Black Belt), and tenure form (renting, lodging, kitchenettes, contracts) — and each layer had its own enablers and constraints. Organized violence enforced residential boundaries in both waves: 58 bombings (1917–1921) and the 1919 riot in the first; Trumbull Park (1953–1963) in the second.

### WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUGGESTS

Reception institutions — Olivet Baptist, the Wabash YMCA, the *Defender*, chain-migration networks — were the difference between arrival and homelessness for thousands, though their quantitative reach is undocumented. The kitchenette and the contract sale were structural twins: tenure forms that housed the excluded while extracting their wealth. The end of covenant enforceability in 1948 did not end exclusion; it transferred the work to markets (blockbusting, contract selling) and to state siting decisions (the CHA's Neighborhood Composition Rule).

### WHAT REMAINS DISPUTED OR UNKNOWN

Exact Census table provenance for all population figures; the "75 percent" versus "90 percent" Black Belt/Bronzeville share tension; the true coverage of restrictive covenants (the "80 percent" figure is provisional); Clyde Ross's purchase year and price; pre-1950 CHA Black occupancy; the quantitative reach of Olivet's and the YMCA's housing referrals; the causal weight of the *Defender* versus labor demand; and the Dan Ryan Expressway's alleged racial routing intent.

### WHAT CTL SHOULD RESEARCH NEXT

Pull exact decennial Census tables (1910–1970) for Chicago's Black population and tract-level maps; page-check the 1922 Commission report for the 58-bombing count and lodger data; mine Olivet Baptist and Wabash YMCA archives for referral records; build the West Side institutional map the literature lacks; center women and ordinary workers; and trace the contract-selling paper trail (CBL court records, Satter's sources) to quantify wealth extraction.
