# CTL-RB-MEDIA-000003 — Black Images on Chicago Television

**Research Brief v0.1 — Status: Research**
**Domain:** Media (television) · **Geography:** Chicago, Illinois · **Period:** 1947–present
**Primary question:** How did locally produced television representation of Black people change over time in Chicago?

---

## 01 · Executive Summary

Chicago did not merely watch the Black television revolution — it produced several of its decisive chapters. From a Defender-sponsored talent contest on WBBM in 1947, through the gospel institution of *Jubilee Showcase* (WLS, 1963–1984), to WTTW's *Our People* (1968) and *Bird of the Iron Feather* (1970, the first all-Black television soap opera), to Don Cornelius's *Soul Train* launching on WCIU in August 1970, Chicago's local stations generated a disproportionate share of nationally significant Black television.

The evidence supports a four-era arc:

1. **1947–1967 — Live and Local, Thinly Black.** Television is born in Chicago (WBKB, WNBQ, WGN-TV); Black people appear mainly as musical performers, talent-contest hopefuls, and Sunday-morning gospel singers. Newsrooms are all-white.
2. **1968–1979 — The Black Production Decade.** The assassination of Dr. King and the Kerner Commission catalyze a burst of Chicago-produced Black programming: *Our People*, *Bird of the Iron Feather*, *Soul Train*, plus the arrival of the first Black reporters and anchors (Carole Simpson, Harry Porterfield, Merri Dee).
3. **1980–1999 — The Anchor Desk and the Backlash.** Black anchors become fixtures (Warner Saunders co-anchoring WMAQ's 10 p.m. news), but power is contested: the 1985 demotion of Harry Porterfield triggers a Jesse Jackson–led boycott of WBBM. Chicago also gains its only Black-owned full-power station, WJYS (1991).
4. **2000–present — Consolidation and the Ownership Gap.** Ownership consolidates; on-camera representation normalizes while station ownership by Black Chicagoans remains essentially absent.

**Strongest findings:** (a) the 1968–1971 window produced *Our People*, *Bird of the Iron Feather*, and *Soul Train* — three nationally significant programs — from Chicago's local and public stations within roughly three years; (b) WTTW and the UHF independents (WCIU, WSNS), not the network affiliates, carried the early load; (c) the advertising dimension has a Chicago-specific institutional answer — Burrell Communications (1971) — with work now archived at the Library of Congress.

**Largest uncertainty:** the archive itself. Early local broadcasts were rarely preserved; claims about "firsts" rest on secondary accounts; and the Museum of Broadcast Communications' specific holdings on Black Chicago television have not been inventoried for this brief. Behind-the-camera employment data by race for Chicago stations is essentially unavailable in public sources.

## 02 · Primary Research Question

**How did locally produced television representation of Black people change over time in Chicago?**

This is a question about *production*, not reception: what Chicago stations and producers put on the air, who made it, who owned it, and how those images changed across eras. National network programming watched in Chicago (e.g., *Amos 'n' Andy*, *Julia*, *The Cosby Show*) is context, not subject — flagged for CTL-DR-MEDIA-001.

---

## 03 · Why It Matters

Television was the 20th century's dominant image-machine, and Chicago was one of its birthplaces — the "Chicago School of Television" pioneered live, low-budget local production at WBKB in the 1940s. Understanding how local stations rendered Black life — from Sunday gospel hours to the 10 p.m. anchor desk — is foundational to CTL's claim that Chicago built Black cultural infrastructure, not just consumed it. The story also corrects a common error: treating Black television history as a national-network story centered in New York and Los Angeles. Chicago's UHF independents and its public station were national incubators.

---

## 04 · Scope

**In scope:** locally produced television in the Chicago market, 1947–present: local programs, local news, local advertising production, public television (WTTW), and Chicago-produced content later syndicated nationally (*Soul Train*, *Jubilee Showcase*). Includes on-air talent, news subjects, creative control, station ownership, and advertising images.

**Out of scope:** national network entertainment programming merely *watched* in Chicago (→ CTL-DR-MEDIA-001); Black radio history (→ CTL-DR-BPM-003 cross-references only); cable networks founded elsewhere; film.

**Inclusion rule:** a program, person, or station is included if the production or employment decision was made in Chicago. A nationally aired show produced in Chicago counts; a nationally produced show with Black Chicagoan guest stars does not.

---

## 05 · Definitions

"Representation" is disambiguated into **five dimensions**, because conflating them is this topic's classic failure mode:

1. **On-air performance** — hosts, performers, and characters (e.g., the Staple Singers on *Jubilee Showcase*; Soul Train dancers).
2. **News presence** — reporters and anchors *and* the framing of Black people as news subjects (e.g., Carole Simpson covering the Chicago Seven trial; Harry Porterfield's *Someone You Should Know*).
3. **Creative control** — who produces, directs, writes, and owns programs (e.g., Don Cornelius owning *Soul Train* outright; Richard Durham creating *Bird of the Iron Feather*).
4. **Ownership** — who holds station licenses (e.g., Jovon Broadcasting's WJYS — the market's only Black-owned full-power commercial station).
5. **Advertising images** — how Black consumers are depicted in commercials (e.g., Burrell Communications' "positive realism").

A sixth cross-cutting question — **behind-the-camera employment** (camera operators, technicians, managers) — is tracked where evidence exists; the record is thin, and that thinness is itself a finding.

**Terminology:** "Black" is used throughout; period sources quoted may use "Negro" or "African American" and are quoted verbatim.

---

## 06 · Historical Context

Commercial television reached Chicago in the late 1940s with WBKB (Balaban & Katz), WNBQ (NBC, 1948; renamed WMAQ-TV in 1964), and WGN-TV (1948) — all broadcasting live, much of it locally produced, in the improvisational "Chicago School" style. WTTW (channel 11), the city's public station, signed on in 1955; independent UHF stations (WCIU, later WSNS) operated on thinner margins and proved more willing to program for Black audiences.

Three national forces shaped the local story: the civil-rights movement; the **Kerner Commission (1968)**, which indicted the news media's failure on race and urged newsroom integration; and the economics of UHF independents, which needed underserved audiences. Chicago-specific forces mattered equally: a large Black viewership on the South and West Sides; a militant Black press (the *Defender*); and Operation PUSH's media-accountability campaigns under Rev. Jesse Jackson.

---

## 07 · Key Findings

1. **A three-year burst (1968–1970) produced three nationally significant programs.** *Our People* (WTTW, premiered a week after Dr. King's assassination), *Bird of the Iron Feather* (WTTW/NET, January 1970 — the first all-Black TV soap opera), and *Soul Train* (WCIU, August 1970) all originated in Chicago within roughly 30 months. No other local market can claim a comparable cluster.
2. **Public and independent stations, not affiliates, carried the early load.** WTTW (public) and UHF independents WCIU and WSNS account for nearly every landmark before 1978. The network affiliates (WBBM, WMAQ, WLS, WGN) integrated their anchor desks later — and contentiously.
3. **The anchor desk was won, then contested.** Carole Simpson (first Black woman TV reporter in Chicago, WMAQ), Harry Porterfield (first Black weekday anchor, WBBM, 1978), Merri Dee (WGN, 1972), and Warner Saunders (WMAQ 10 p.m., from 1980) normalized Black authority in local news. But Porterfield's 1985 demotion — to make room for Bill Kurtis's return — triggered a Jesse Jackson–led boycott of WBBM, showing representation without power was unstable.
4. **Chicago produced the advertising answer too.** Burrell Communications, founded in Chicago in 1971 by Tom Burrell and Emmett McBain, built "positive realism" into national TV commercials — a Chicago-originated intervention in the *images* dimension, with work archived at the Library of Congress.
5. **Ownership is the missing dimension.** Across eight decades, the Chicago market has had exactly one Black-owned full-power commercial TV station: WJYS (channel 62, Jovon Broadcasting, Joseph and Yvonne Stroud), which signed on in 1991 after a landmark FCC comparative hearing. No major-network affiliate in Chicago has ever been Black-owned.
6. **The archive is the story's weakest link.** Most early local programming was broadcast live and never preserved; several landmark claims ("firsts") rest on secondary sources; and behind-the-camera employment data is unavailable. What survives does so largely through oral history (The HistoryMakers, headquartered in Chicago) and specialist archives.

## 09 · Analysis

**Why did Chicago produce so much?** Three structural reasons. First, the UHF independents (WCIU, WSNS) were economically marginal and hungry for audiences the affiliates ignored; WCIU hired Don Cornelius as a news and sports reporter in 1967 and let him build a soul-music show with no financial backing. Second, WTTW — as a public station — had a public-interest mandate that, after 1968, translated into *Our People* and *Bird of the Iron Feather*. Third, Chicago's Black cultural economy (gospel, soul, the *Defender*, WVON) supplied talent, audiences, and sponsors — including Sears and the Black-owned Johnson Products Company, whose Afro Sheen sponsorship carried *Soul Train* into national syndication.

**The host paradox.** *Jubilee Showcase* (1963–1984) — arguably Chicago's longest-running Black-music television institution — was created, produced, and hosted by Sid Ordower, a white civil-rights activist. It won a national Emmy as a "Pioneering Project in Television." This complicates any simple story of self-representation: Black musical genius was on screen for two decades before Black hosts controlled comparable local airtime.

**The newsroom-authority gap.** Black Chicagoans entered local television through music and public affairs before news. Entertainment representation (dancers, gospel singers, soul acts) was abundant by the early 1970s; anchor-desk authority arrived later and had to be defended (the 1985 WBBM boycott). The pattern suggests stations would show Black *culture* before trusting Black *journalists* — a distinction worth preserving in the knowledge graph.

**The national-launch trap.** Chicago incubated *Soul Train* but lost it: national syndication moved production to Los Angeles in 1971 (a local Chicago version with Clinton Ghent continued to 1976). Chicago's role as incubator-but-not-beneficiary is a recurring pattern worth flagging for CTL-DR-ECON-001.

## 11 · People

- **Don Cornelius** (1936–2012) — WCIU reporter; creator/host/producer/owner of *Soul Train*; launched it on WCIU Aug 17, 1970; moved it to national syndication 1971.
- **Richard Durham** (1917–1984) — *Chicago Defender* and *Muhammad Speaks* writer; creator of *Bird of the Iron Feather* (WTTW, 1970); earlier *Destination Freedom* (WMAQ radio, 1948).
- **Jim Tilmon** (1934–2021) — host of WTTW's *Our People* (1968); later WMAQ meteorologist and host of *Tilmon's Tempo*; commercial airline pilot.
- **Merri Dee** (Mary Francine Dorham, 1936–2022) — WBEE (1966), WCIU (1967), *The Merri Dee Show* (WSNS, 1970–71); survived 1971 kidnapping/shooting; WGN anchor from 1972; later WGN community-relations director.
- **Harry Porterfield** (1928–2023) — WBBM (1964–1985): news writer, reporter, first Black weekday anchor (1978); creator of *Someone You Should Know* (1977); WLS (1985–2009); returned to WBBM 2009–2015.
- **Carole Simpson** (b. 1940) — first woman to broadcast news in Chicago (WCFL, 1965); first Black woman TV reporter in Chicago (WMAQ); later ABC News national correspondent and the first woman/minority to moderate a U.S. presidential debate (1992).
- **Warner Saunders** (1935–2018) — WBBM community-affairs director, host of *Common Ground*; WMAQ (1980–2009), 10 p.m. co-anchor; MBC honoree.
- **Sid Ordower** — white civil-rights activist; creator/producer/host of *Jubilee Showcase* (WLS, 1963–1984).
- **Tom Burrell** (b. 1939) — first Black employee of a Chicago ad agency; co-founded Burrell Communications (1971); "Black people are not dark-skinned white people."
- **Joseph and Yvonne Stroud** — owners, Jovon Minority Broadcasting; won FCC comparative hearing for channel 62; launched WJYS (1991).
- **Bill Campbell** (1950–2021) — WLS Director of Community Services; host/producer of *Chicagoing* (from 1978).
- **Ben Holman** — preceded Porterfield as a Black on-air newscaster in Chicago (details unresolved; further research).
- **Charles Thomas** — WLS-TV political reporter from 1991 (HistoryMakers).
- **Clinton Ghent** — dancer on early *Soul Train*; hosted the local Chicago edition until 1976.
- **Rev. Jesse Jackson** — led the 1985 boycott of WBBM over Porterfield's demotion; appeared on *Our People* (1970).

## 19 · Competing Interpretations

1. **"Chicago led" vs. "Chicago incubated, others harvested."** The 1968–70 cluster is exceptional, but *Soul Train*'s move to Los Angeles and the affiliates' late integration show Chicago failing to retain what it created.
2. **Progress vs. containment.** One reading: steady expansion to the anchor desk. Another: stations admitted Black *culture* (music, dance) long before Black *authority* (anchors, owners). The *Jubilee Showcase* host paradox is the strongest exhibit for the containment reading.
3. **The boycott's meaning.** Defense of Black journalistic authority, or personality-driven activism with undocumented structural effects? The brief's sources cannot settle it.

## 21 · Limitations

- **Source ceiling:** no archival research was conducted in person (MBC, WTTW archives, *Defender* morgue); the brief rests on published sources, oral-history finding aids, and reputable journalism.
- **"Firsts" inflation:** several claims originate with interested parties (stations, obituaries, tribute resolutions); each is graded, and two are marked DISPUTED.
- **Survivorship bias:** programs that survived on tape (*Soul Train*, *Jubilee Showcase* highlights) dominate the visual record; live local programming that vanished leaves no trace.
- **Employment data:** effectively absent; the analysis leans on visible on-air roles.
- **Period coverage is uneven:** 1968–1985 is well documented; the 1940s–50s and the 1990s–present are thin.
