# CTL-RB-MUS-000004 — Black Radio as Music Infrastructure

**CULTURETECHLENS — "Culture, Clearly Seen." — Black Cultural Intelligence**

---

## 1. Executive Summary

How did radio connect performers, records, advertisers, and audiences? In Chicago — the city that invented the Black radio DJ as a cultural institution — the answer is that radio *was* the infrastructure: not a channel music passed through but the physical, economic, and political plant that made Black music a business and Black audiences a public.

This brief traces that infrastructure from Jack Cooper's "All-Negro Hour" on WGBS in 1929 — the first Black DJ format hour, built on race records because they were cheaper than live talent — through the 1940s block-programming era on WGES (Al Benson, Richard Durham's dramas, "Negro Newsfront"), the 1963 launch of WVON ("Voice of the Negro") by Chess Records' Leonard and Phil Chess, the 1967–94 Black-soul and Black-owned era on 950 AM (WGRT, then Johnson Publishing's WJPC), the 1962–64 all-Black WYNR experiment on 1390, the disco and urban-contemporary reign of WGCI-FM 107.5, and the Hot Mix 5's live house mixes on WBMX 102.7 in the 1980s.

The mechanism is a circuit, documented link by link: performers and labels supplied records and promotion (including payola, regulated after 1960); DJs and program directors turned records into programming; advertisers and sponsors bought access to the audience that programming assembled; and audiences converted listening into record purchases, venue attendance, requests, ratings, and civic action — which in turn gave stations and artists leverage over labels and sponsors. The circuit's most consequential output was political: in 1983, WVON commentator Lu Palmer's "We shall see in '83" campaign helped elect Harold Washington, Chicago's first Black mayor — during the station's *music* era, three years before its 1986 all-talk flip, a chronology this brief corrects against earlier CTL drafts.

**Verdict in one line:** The evidence establishes radio as Chicago's central Black music infrastructure — a two-way circuit of promotion, advertising, audience feedback, and political mobilization — while the exact magnitudes of its economic effects remain provisional.

## 2. Primary Research Question

**How did radio connect performers, records, advertisers, and audiences?**

Sub-questions: Who owned the stations, and how did ownership change what got played? What did advertisers buy, and what did sponsors cost stations? How did audiences talk back — through requests, record purchases, ratings, and votes? How did regulation (payola disclosure, minority-ownership policy) and technology (AM/FM, the transistor radio, the turntable) shape the circuit?

## 3. Why It Matters

Every streaming-era debate about playlist power, pay-for-play, and who controls Black music's distribution has a Chicago-radio precedent. WVON was bought by a record label (Chess), became the instrument of a mayoral campaign (Washington 1983), and ended Black-owned again (Midway Broadcasting) — three ownership regimes that changed the music. The Hot Mix 5 proved a radio show could invent a genre's distribution channel. Understanding this infrastructure is prerequisite to understanding Chicago house, Chicago soul, and the political economy of Black cultural production — and it is the historical baseline against which CTL should measure today's platforms.

## 4. Scope

- **Geography:** Chicago and its Illinois suburbs (Cicero, Oak Park, Harvey) — the FCC's Chicago radio market. National context (Motown, FCC policy, Soul Train syndication) only where it touches the Chicago circuit.
- **Period:** 1929 (Cooper's "All-Negro Hour") to 2014 (950 AM's conversion to Relevant Radio), with analytical weight on 1963–1994.
- **Inclusions:** Full-time and block-programmed Black-oriented stations; DJ careers that crossed stations; ownership changes; the advertising and promotion economy; the radio→electoral-politics link.
- **Exclusions:** White-appeal stations except where crossover listening is documented (1963); post-2014 WVON history in detail (its 1690 move is noted; full modern chronology is scheduled research); podcast/streaming successors.

## 5. Definitions

- **Black-oriented / Black-appeal radio:** Programming directed at Black audiences, whether or not the station was Black-owned. Ownership and orientation are tracked separately throughout this brief.
- **Block programming:** Leased time slots sold to individual DJs or sponsors (the 1940s–50s model); distinct from full-time format stations.
- **Race records:** Commercial recordings marketed to Black audiences (1920s–40s); crucially, exempt from the ASCAP performance ban, which made them the cheapest broadcast material available.
- **Payola:** Payment for airplay; after the 1960 Federal Communications Act amendments, undisclosed pay-for-play became illegal and labels routed promotion through independent promoters.
- **Shared-time operation:** Two licensees splitting broadcast hours on one frequency (WXOL/WCEV on 1450 from 1979).
- **The circuit:** This brief's analytical model — performers/labels → records & promotion → DJs/programmers → programming → advertisers/sponsors → audiences → purchases/attendance/requests/ratings/votes → renewed leverage for stations and artists.

## 6. Historical Context

Black Chicago radio began as an economic workaround. When Jack Cooper launched "The All-Negro Hour" on WGBS on November 3, 1929, he used recorded race records rather than live talent because records were cheaper — and because race records were exempt from the ASCAP ban on recorded music, they were legally the path of least resistance (Ellett 2013, via the McFarland *Encyclopedia of Black Radio*). By 1938 Cooper was buying afternoon time on WSBC and WHFC; at his 1947 peak he sold 40 hours a week across four stations, and his WAAF "Wardrobe Derby" was sponsored by National Credit Clothiers — the advertiser link present from the beginning.

The 1940s added a second layer: WGES's 1390 signal carried Al Benson's Sunday gospel program from 1945 (he went secular with blues, jazz, and R&B and became the top Black DJ of the late 1940s), while Richard Durham wrote Black radio drama — "Democracy–USA" (WBBM, 1946), "Here Comes Tomorrow" (WJJD, 1947, the first Black soap opera), "Destination Freedom" (WMAQ, 1948–50) — and Vernon Jarrett with Oscar Brown Jr. read "Negro Newsfront" on WJJD (1948–51), one of the country's first Black radio newscasts.

The postwar economics turned hostile to musicians and friendly to DJs: the 1942–44 recording ban and the rise of the 45 RPM single made records — not live bands — the station's cheapest content, and the transistor radio gave teenagers (Black and white) private listening. By 1963, as NPR Illinois later documented, white and Black Chicago teenagers were listening to the same stations — the crossover audience that made Black radio commercially powerful and politically threatening.

## 7. Key Findings

1. **The label bought the station.** On April 1, 1963, Chess Records owners Leonard and Phil Chess purchased 1450 AM (WHFC, Cicero) and launched WVON, "Voice of the Negro," with the "Good Guys" — Franklin McCarthy, E. Rodney Jones, Herb Kent, Wesley South, Pervis Spann — and GM Lucky Cordell. The performers→records→radio link was, at launch, a single corporate circuit (VERIFIED: three legislative sources).
2. **The 950 AM chain documents Black ownership economics.** WAAF → WGRT (1967, soul, $900,000) → WJPC (1973, Johnson Publishing, $1.8M, urban) → all-rap (1992) → sold 1994 ($8M). Daddy-O Daylie's shrinking morning jazz show on WGRT is a small, documented picture of format economics squeezing out jazz (SUPPORTED: Billboard, Jet, Ebony, Duncan's).
3. **The 1390 AM facility is a palimpsest of the whole story.** WGES block programming (Benson) → WYNR all-Black "Winner" (1962–64, with an FCC hearing over dropped foreign-language shows) → WNUS all-news (1964) → WVON (1975) → WGCI (1983) → a 1989–90 Black talk format → WGRB. The frequency carried every model in sequence (SUPPORTED: Broadcasting, Billboard, Chicago Tribune, FCC Call Sign History).
4. **1983 was a music-era political victory, not a talk-format one.** Lu Palmer's "Lu's Notebook"/"On Target" (10 p.m.–midnight on WVON 1450) coined "We shall see in '83," ran political-education clinics, and lost sponsors over his politics — and was, per two Congressional tributes and his Tribune obituary, the single most effective voice in electing Harold Washington. The station's Black talk format came in 1986, three years later. Earlier CTL drafts inverted this; the correction is load-bearing (VERIFIED: Palmer's 1989 Eyes on the Prize oral history, primary).
5. **The Hot Mix 5 industrialized club culture on FM.** From 1981, WBMX program director Lee Michaels put club DJs doing live house mixes on "Saturday Night Live, Ain't No Jive" — the City of Chicago's 2023 Warehouse Landmark Report confirms WBMX was among the first stations to broadcast live house mixes. When WGCI-FM poached three members for its "Jackmasters" in 1986, it proved the format's commercial value (VERIFIED for the broadcast fact; PROVISIONAL for roster and ratings details).
6. **The advertiser node cut both ways.** National Credit Clothiers sponsored Cooper in 1947; under Globetrotter, WVON cut community programming to sell more commercials (per Searcy); Palmer's sponsors withdrew over his politics. Advertising funded the infrastructure and disciplined it (SUPPORTED/PROVISIONAL).
7. **Regulation shaped the circuit at two joints.** The 1960 payola-disclosure amendments pushed label promotion into independent-promoter middlemen (Willie Dixon's autobiography: "no disc jockey was going to play your record then without you paying him"). The FCC's 1978 minority-ownership policy (Tax Certificate, Distress Sale) grew minority-owned stations from ~40 to ~323 by 1995 — the policy context for Midway Broadcasting's 1979 return to 1450 (SUPPORTED).

## 9. Analysis — How the Circuit Worked

**Performers/labels → records & promotion.** Chess owning WVON collapsed the distance between label and station to zero; Motown's reported first-listen arrangement (lore, PROVISIONAL) is the same link in softer form. Payola was the link's shadow economy: Dixon's testimony describes a market where airplay had a price, and the 1960 disclosure rule didn't end the market — it professionalized it into independent promotion.

**Records → DJs/programmers → programming.** The DJ was the circuit's valve. Cooper's economics (records cheaper than talent), Benson's gospel-to-R&B crossover, Kent's "dusties" oldies invention at WBEE, the Hot Mix 5's live mixing — each shows DJs adding value labels couldn't: curation, personality, and eventually real-time music production on air.

**Programming → advertisers/sponsors → audiences.** Advertisers bought the assembled audience: clothiers in 1947, the full spot-load economy by the 1970s. But sponsors also constrained: Globetrotter's commercialization cut community programming, and Palmer's sponsors fled his politics — evidence that the advertiser node could veto as well as fund.

**Audiences → feedback → leverage.** Audiences closed the circuit five ways: buying records (the WXOL blues-sales bump, PROVISIONAL but structurally plausible), attending venues (Spann's 1960 B.B. King/Junior Parker concert), requesting songs, registering in ratings (the missing Arbitron layer), and voting (Washington 1983). Each feedback channel gave stations leverage over labels and sponsors — and gave artists leverage over stations.

**Ownership as the master switch.** Every change of owner changed the music: Chess (label synergy), Globetrotter/Gannett (commercialization, the 1390 move, the 1983 call change), Midway (Black ownership returning to 1450, the blues format, the 1986 talk flip), Johnson Publishing (Black corporate ownership on 950). The FCC's 1978 minority-ownership policy is the reason the Midway chapter exists in the form it does.

**The missing link.** The brief documents the circuit's structure at every node but cannot yet quantify its economics: no station rate cards, no label promotion budgets, no Arbitron books were consulted. The circuit is proven; its magnitudes are not.

## 19. Competing Interpretations

- **Was WVON a label marketing arm or a community station?** The Chess purchase supports the marketing-arm reading; the civil-rights record (MLK interviews, the 1968 loudspeaker trucks, Palmer's clinics) supports the community reading. The evidence sustains both — the station was both, and the tension between the two is the story.
- **Did Black radio cause the 1983 Washington victory or merely channel it?** Palmer's own account claims causal primacy ("the single most effective voice"); the brief treats this as VERIFIED that radio was central and PROVISIONAL on the exact causal weight. The organizing existed independently of the station; the station amplified it.
- **Payola: corruption or market?** Dixon's testimony frames pay-for-play as extortion of artists; the post-1960 independent-promoter system frames it as a market in access. The brief presents both without resolving — the disclosure regime's actual effects on Black artists specifically are unresearched.
- **The Hot Mix 5's importance:** Participant accounts ("the real story") claim foundational status for house music's broadcast breakthrough; the City of Chicago's landmark report corroborates the broadcast fact but not the superlatives. Keep the fact, attribute the superlatives.

## 25. Final Verdict

**WHAT THE EVIDENCE ESTABLISHES:** That Chicago's Black-oriented radio — from Cooper's 1929 "All-Negro Hour" through WVON's 1963 launch by Chess Records, the Black-owned eras on 950 AM (Johnson Publishing, 1973–94) and 1450 AM (Midway, 1979–), the 1390 AM facility's succession of formats, WGCI-FM's urban-contemporary reign, and WBMX's Hot Mix 5 — functioned as a two-way infrastructure connecting performers, labels, records, DJs, advertisers, and audiences. That ownership changes changed the music. That in 1983 this infrastructure helped elect Harold Washington through Lu Palmer's music-era commentary, not through a talk format that did not yet exist. That regulation (1960 payola disclosure, 1978 minority-ownership policy) and technology (records, AM/FM, the transistor radio, the mixer) structured the circuit at every joint.

**SUGGESTS:** That airplay drove record sales in specific cases (WXOL's blues bump); that the Motown–WVON arrangement was real in some form; that the Hot Mix 5's broadcasts were commercially decisive for house music's spread; that sponsor pressure systematically constrained Black political speech on commercial radio. All suggestive, none established — each is graded PROVISIONAL and flagged for the next research pass.

**REMAINS DISPUTED OR UNKNOWN:** The 2022 claim that Spann and South bought WVON in 1963 (contradicted by three legislative sources — treated as false); Oscar Brown Jr.'s "WVON then WHFC" sequence (anachronistic as stated); the exact magnitudes of every audience and revenue figure in the brief; the full post-1990 WVON history; the labor and sales side of the stations; the complete WGES block-programming record.
