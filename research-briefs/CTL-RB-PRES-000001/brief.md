## 01 — Executive Summary

Which institutions maintain significant Black Chicago collections, and how are they accessible? The evidence establishes that Black Chicago is preserved by a **layered network of roughly two dozen institutions** — public-library research collections, museums, university special collections, government archives, community archives, and artist-led collections — connected since 2006 by the Black Metropolis Research Consortium (BMRC). No single institution holds "the" Black Chicago archive; preservation is distributed, unevenly funded, and unevenly accessible.

The strongest, best-documented repositories are the Vivian G. Harsh Research Collection at the Carter G. Woodson Regional Library (the Midwest's largest Black history collection, per Chicago Public Library), the DuSable Black History Museum and Education Center (founded 1961; the oldest independent Black museum in the United States), and the Chicago History Museum's Abakanowicz Research Center. A decisive finding: **Chicago's Black-press heritage has been displaced or left incomplete.** The Johnson Publishing Company (Ebony/Jet) photo archive was sold for $30 million at a 2019 bankruptcy auction and transferred in 2022 to the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture and the Getty Research Institute in Washington, DC — it left Chicago entirely. The Chicago Defender's founding-family papers remain at CPL only because Robert Sengstacke refused national bids in 2009, while the paper's own business archives after the 2019 print shutdown are unaccounted for.

The access picture is the brief's central argument: at the time of writing, **the Harsh Collection is temporarily closed for research due to building maintenance**, with CPL "targeting November" for reopening; the new Cook County Historic Archives and Records Office (established 2020) is still closed to the public; and several major repositories are appointment-only or carry documented backlogs. Preservation of Black Chicago exists — but it is fragile, under-capitalized, and in places physically unreachable right now.

---

## 02 — Primary Research Question

**Which institutions maintain significant Black Chicago collections and how are they accessible?**

The question is bounded: it asks for *institutions*, *collections*, and *access conditions* — not a history of Black Chicago itself. "Significant" means holdings documented in published finding aids, collection descriptions, or reputable secondary accounts that substantially document Black life, thought, or creativity in Chicago. Access is reported exactly as each institution states it in its own published policies, verified on 2026-09-28, with no inference.

---

## 03 — Why It Matters

Black Chicago — the Great Migration's destination, the Black press's headquarters, the Chicago Black Renaissance's home — has repeatedly faced archival loss: building decay, institutional neglect, bankruptcy auctions, the quiet attrition of unprocessed boxes. When the Johnson Publishing Company archive was auctioned in 2019, only an emergency $30 million foundation consortium kept it from private hands — and it left Chicago anyway. When Woodson Regional Library's building crumbled in the 2010s, the Midwest's largest Black collection was at physical risk. "Who preserves Black Chicago?" is an accountability question: whether institutional trust is backed by open doors, processing capacity, and digitization — or by good intentions alone.

---

## 04 — Scope

**Included:** Institutions with a documented institutional custodian whose published holdings substantially document Black Chicago, from the 19th century to the present, located in Chicago or its immediate region (Evanston's Shorefront included as a BMRC member documenting Chicagoland Black life).

**Excluded:** Private and family collections without a documented institutional custodian; individual items; oral-history projects without archival custody; institutions outside the Chicago region (the Smithsonian and Getty appear only as the JPC archive's current custodians — a loss documented, not a collection inventoried). Church and funeral-home holdings are included only where an institutional custodian exists in the evidence (Shorefront's church-basement archive, with founder and site documented by NPR, is in; unnamed church records are out).

**Verification standard:** claims graded VERIFIED (primary source: institution page, finding aid, or official record), SUPPORTED (reputable secondary source), PROVISIONAL (single-source or dated evidence), DISPUTED (conflicting evidence), UNRESOLVED (sought, not found). Evidence outranks elegance throughout.

---

## 05 — Definitions

- **Black Chicago:** the Black people, communities, institutions, and cultural production of Chicago and its region, 19th century–present. Used interchangeably with "African American" where institutions use that term (e.g., the Harsh Collection's formal name); "African diasporic" is the BMRC's wider frame.
- **Collection:** an intentionally assembled body of materials (manuscripts, photographs, books, audiovisual, artifacts) held by an institution.
- **Finding aid:** the published descriptive inventory that makes an archival collection discoverable; its absence usually means the collection is effectively inaccessible to outside researchers even when it physically exists.
- **Access:** what an institution *states* about who may use materials, when, and under what conditions. "Open" means walk-in hours; "appointment-only" means advance arrangement required.
- **Contested terminology (flagged first, per protocol):** "Negro," "Afro-American," "Black," "African American," "African diasporic" all appear in collection names and the evidence. They are historical artifacts of their naming moments, reproduced here only in titles and quotations — never as the brief's own descriptors. "Black metropolis" derives from Drake and Cayton's 1945 sociological study *Black Metropolis*, the BMRC's namesake.

## 06 — Historical Context

Black archival preservation in Chicago began inside Black institutions before white institutions collected Black life. Vivian G. Harsh, Chicago Public Library's first African American branch manager (January 18, 1932), built the "Special Negro Collection" at the George Cleveland Hall Branch from 1932 — modeled on the Schomburg, Fisk, and Atlanta University collections — gathering books, photographs, clippings, and donations from Bronzeville patrons including Richard Wright and Langston Hughes. Margaret Taylor-Burroughs and Charles Burroughs founded the Ebony Museum of Negro History in their living room in 1961, the seed of the DuSable. These were acts of self-documentation in a city whose mainstream institutions largely ignored Black life.

From the 1970s, Black collections entered public and university custody at scale: the Harsh Collection moved to Woodson Regional Library in 1975; DuSable moved to Washington Park in 1973. But the BMRC's own founding story (2003–2006) is that as late as the early 2000s, UChicago faculty found Black archival materials "scattered across Chicago" with no clear map of access. The consortium's survey found a "treasure trove of previously unknown historical material" — much of it unprocessed and inaccessible. Two decades later, the portal exists, but the backlogs and access gaps below show the work is unfinished.

---

## 07 — Key Findings

1. **Preservation is distributed across roughly two dozen institutions, coordinated by the BMRC.** Launched 2006 at UChicago, the consortium connects public libraries, museums, universities, community and government archives, with a single searchable Archives Portal. Its own video describes "the 26 archives, libraries and museums that are our members."
2. **The flagship public collection is currently closed.** The Harsh Collection — the Midwest's largest Black history and literature collection — is temporarily unavailable for research due to building maintenance, with CPL "targeting November" for reopening.
3. **Chicago's Black-press heritage has been displaced or left incomplete.** The Johnson Publishing Company (Ebony/Jet) photo archive was auctioned in 2019 for $30M and transferred in 2022 to NMAAHC and the Getty Research Institute — physically housed in Washington, DC. The Defender's founding-family papers remain at CPL only because Robert Sengstacke refused national bids in 2009; the paper's own business archives after 2019 remain unaccounted for.
4. **Government archives are new and not yet open.** The Cook County Historic Archives and Records Office (established 2020) is still "currently closed to the public," offering remote reference only.
5. **Community and artist-led archives carry weight the formal system doesn't.** Shorefront Legacy Center documented Black suburban life for nearly 30 years from a church basement; its records underpinned Evanston's reparations findings. Theaster Gates's Rebuild Foundation holds the JPC books/periodicals, Frankie Knuckles's vinyl, the Edward J. Williams Collection, and 60,000+ lantern slides.
6. **Access honesty varies sharply.** Chicago History Museum's research center is free with no appointment required. UIC, DuSable, and SSAC are appointment-only; SSAC is mid-renovation (completion anticipated 2027); several CHM finding aids were offline at time of writing.

Founded 1932 at Hall Branch; renamed for Harsh 1970; moved to Woodson 1975; expanded 1998–99. Holdings: 70,000+ books (City landmark report); manuscripts of Wright, Hughes, Bontemps; photographs; rare books; Abbott-Sengstacke Family Papers (donated May 2009; ~83 boxes, ~4,000 photos, ~100 home movies); "Negro in Illinois" Papers (1936–1942, 53 boxes, largely digitized); Timuel D. Normal policy: by appointment (Tue–Thu 1–5 p.m.; 3rd Sat 1–4 p.m.); advance contact required for unprocessed collections.** [chipublib.org]

**DuSable Black History Museum and Education Center** (Washington Park). Founded 1961 by Margaret Taylor-Burroughs and Charles Burroughs as the Ebony Museum of Negro History; moved 1973; renamed for Jean Baptiste Point DuSable. Oldest independent Black museum in the U.S. Collection: 13,000 artifacts (Wikipedia) vs. 15,000+ items (Wintrust) — discrepancy unresolved. Holdings: Charles Dawson Papers, Ida B. Wells's desk, Paul Laurence Dunbar's violin, Harold Washington Wing materials, slavery-era relics, art holdings. Smithsonian Affiliate (2016). **Access: archives by appointment** — CEO Perri Irmer (Feb 2025): "Anyone can get access with an appointment to our archives"; the Hamilton Institute Reading Room is appointment-only, Tue–Sat. Five-year archives plan: full inventory, storage renovation; "A Call for Artifacts" launched Sept 2026.

**Chicago History Museum — Abakanowicz Research Center.** Research collections: printed/published materials, archives and manuscripts, prints and photographs, architectural drawings, ephemera; catalog ARCHIE (migrated 2026 to CARLI). **Access: free; no appointment required; Fall 2026 hours Wed–Fri 12–4 p.m., Sat 10 a.m.–4 p.m.; masks required; materials not paged after 3 p.m. Note: "Some finding aids are temporarily unavailable due to a technical issue" (2026-09-28).**

**Black Metropolis Research Consortium.** Launched 2006 at UChicago (host); from 2003 faculty–archivist discussions (Danielle Allen, Jacqueline Goldsby); predecessors Mapping the Stacks, UNCAP. Mission: expand access to members' holdings documenting African American and African diasporic history, Chicago focus. 20+ members (own video: 26 archives, libraries, museums); searchable Archives Portal; 95 summer fellowships since 2008 (UChicago News, ca. 2019). Associate members: Media Burn (10,000+ videos), Chicago Black Social Culture Map.

**Newberry Library.** Independent research library. Black Chicago strengths: Pullman Company Records; Jack Conroy Papers (Chicago Black Renaissance correspondence); Mark J. Satter Papers (redlining/contract-selling); Great Migration lantern slides (1922–23, "most complete set known to survive"). Reading rooms free; free reader card issued at registration. [newberry.org]

**UChicago — Hanna Holborn Gray Special Collections Research Center.** Ida B. Wells Papers; Julius Rosenwald Papers (1905–1963, 44 linear feet, unrestricted); BMRC host; namesake context (*Black Metropolis*, 1945).

**Northwestern University Libraries.** BMRC member; BMRC board seat. Collection-level Black Chicago descriptions not verified — portal-level coverage only.

**UIC — Special Collections and University Archives.** Black strengths: Chicago Urban League Records, Hyde Park Neighborhood Club Records, Maxwell Street Exhibition Photographs. **Access: by appointment only; Mon–Wed/Fri 10 a.m.–4 p.m., Thu 11 a.m.–4 p.m.; 24-hour advance registration.**

**Chicago State University — Gwendolyn Brooks Library.** Focus: African American history, literature, politics; Chicago neighborhood history. Collections: Gwendolyn Brooks Black Writers' Conference AV (1991–); Lerone Bennett Jr. Papers (Ebony executive editor); Provident Hospital Collection; Maxwell Street Photographic Collection; Illinois Leadership Collection (Legislative Black Caucus); Thomas H. Wirth Collection of 18th/19th-century African-Americana; Pincham Collection.

**South Side Community Art Center** (Bronzeville). Founded 1940 (committee 1938); oldest African American art center in the U.S.; National Treasure (National Trust, 2017). Archives: 75 linear feet, 1938–2008, **by appointment only**. $15M renovation/expansion, completion anticipated 2027; admin archives temporarily at Columbia College Chicago during renovation.

**Cook County Historic Archives and Records Office.** Established by the 2020 appropriation; first Manager of Archives Skyla Hearn; mandate tied to the county's 200th anniversary (2031). **Currently closed to the public; remote reference only.** [cookcountyil.gov]

**Shorefront Legacy Center** (Evanston). Founded by Morris "Dino" Robinson; ~30 years documenting Black life on Chicago's North Shore; housed in the basement of Evanston's Lula B. Sherman Church; holdings: local NAACP minutes, photographs, clippings, discrimination-era correspondence. Records underpinned Evanston's reparations research (NPR, Sept 19, 2026).

**Rebuild Foundation — Stony Island Arts Bank** (Greater Grand Crossing). Artist-led nonprofit (501(c)(3) Dec 2010; founder Theaster Gates). Four collections: 60,000+ glass lantern slides; Johnson Publishing books/periodicals; Frankie Knuckles's vinyl; Edward J. Williams Collection (~4,000 artifacts). Plus Archive House (14,000 architecture books), Listening House (8,000 records from Dr. Wax). Mellon: $3.5M (2022) for collections infrastructure. Stated research-access policy not verified.

**Harold Washington Library Center — Special Collections** (9th floor). Harold Washington Archives & Collections: State Representative Records (1957–1976, 3.25 linear ft, open, offsite — 24-hr notice); Congressional Records (1970–1983, 57 linear ft); Mayoral videotapes (1969–1987, 342 tapes); Political Education Project Records (1963–1988, 42 linear ft).

**Columbia College Chicago.** Center for Black Music Research (BMRC founding-discussion participant); temporarily housed SSAC admin archives during renovation.

**Johnson Publishing Company archive — custodianship (verified; no longer in Chicago).** Founded 1942. Archive: 4M+ photographic images, 9,000+ A/V recordings, ~2.25 TB digital content, textual records. Bought at 2019 bankruptcy auction for $30M by the Ford, Getty Trust, MacArthur, Mellon foundations; formally transferred July 28, 2022 to joint NMAAHC + Getty Research Institute ownership; **physically housed at NMAAHC, Washington, DC**; Getty committed $30M to processing/digitization. [nmaahc.si.edu; Hyperallergic; The Art Newspaper]

**Chicago Defender archives — custodianship.** Print ended July 10, 2019 (continues online). Founding-family papers (Abbott-Sengstacke) at CPL/Harsh since 2009 (Smithsonian had bid to buy). Backfiles (1909–1975) via ProQuest Historical Newspapers. Obsidian Collection Archives (Angela Ford) digitizes Black newspaper archives incl. the Defender. **Custodianship of the paper's own business archives after 2019: UNRESOLVED.**
## 09 — Analysis

Three patterns emerge. First, **the custodial map and the access map are different maps.** BMRC's portal makes holdings discoverable, but discoverability is not accessibility: the flagship Harsh Collection is closed, Cook County is pre-open, and several major collections are appointment-only or backlog-bound. Second, **the economics of Black archives are precarious at both ends of the scale.** The JPC archive survived only through a $30 million philanthropic rescue that nonetheless removed it from Chicago; at the other end, Shorefront preserves a century of suburban Black life in a church basement. Third, **Black Chicago's preservation began as self-preservation** (Harsh 1932, DuSable 1961, SSAC 1940, Shorefront ~1990s, Rebuild 2010) and only later entered public/university custody. Any CTL preservation strategy must center the institutions Black Chicago built for itself, not merely the ones that later acquired its records.

## 19 — Competing Interpretations

- **"Black Chicago's archives are safe because so many institutions hold them."** Counter-evidence: distribution is not the same as preservation; the JPC archive left Chicago despite its significance, the flagship Harsh Collection is closed, and backlogs persist.
- **"Digitization has solved access."** Counter-evidence: most collections are not digitized (e.g., the JPC archive's is a multi-year, $30M undertaking still underway); several CHM finding aids were offline at time of writing.
- **"Community archives are less rigorous."** Counter-evidence: Shorefront's records underpinned a municipal reparations program; the BMRC treats community archives as full members; the distinction reflects resourcing, not rigor.
- **Ownership vs. stewardship:** the JPC transfer is framed by the foundations as rescue; an alternative reading is that Chicago lost physical custody of its own image archive — both can be true.

## 21 — Limitations

Verification prioritized the largest and most-cited repositories; a dozen BMRC members are covered only at portal level. Web-published policies were verified on 2026-09-28 and can change. Social-media-only announcements, unsourced aggregators, and AI summaries were excluded as evidence, which may omit poorly documented holdings (especially church and funeral-home records). No on-site visits were conducted; stated policies were not field-tested. English-language sources only.
