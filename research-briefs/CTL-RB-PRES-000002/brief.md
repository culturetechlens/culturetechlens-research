## 01 Executive Summary

Historically significant Black digital material — the websites, forums, music, video, writing, and social-media records through which Black life has been documented online since the 1990s — is disappearing through five documented mechanisms: (1) outright platform shutdowns, (2) acquisitions and migrations that strand content, (3) corporate platform governance (purges, suspensions, API closures), (4) link rot and format decay, and (5) creator-owned archives with no institutional backup.

MySpace confirmed in March 2019 that a server migration destroyed roughly 50 million songs from 14 million artists uploaded between 2003 and 2015. Yahoo shut GeoCities in 2009, deleting whatever owners had not rescued; Yahoo Groups had all posted content deleted in December 2019 and closed fully in December 2020. Twitter ended free academic API access in spring 2023, degrading twarc and Documenting the Now's Hydrator — tools built to archive Black Lives Matter. DatPiff, the defining mixtape platform of hip-hop's blog era, went dark in 2023; its announced Internet Archive rescue collection then vanished without explanation (status unresolved). The pioneering Black portals collapsed earlier: Africana.com (founded 1999 by Gates and Appiah, bought by Time Warner in 2000) closed January 31, 2005; NetNoir (1995) is defunct; BlackVoices.com was absorbed by AOL and then HuffPost. Domain checks on 2026-09-28 found blackvoices.com, africana.com, netnoir.com, and racialicious.com no longer resolving; clutchmagazine.com redirected to a GoDaddy for-sale page.

The honest finding: nobody knows how much Black material was in most of these losses, because platforms never counted it and no institution was archiving it at scale. That unmeasured gap is itself the core risk. The response — Archiving the Black Web ($2.5M Mellon grant, March 2023), Documenting the Now, the Mixtape Museum — is real but young, and postdates most of the losses.

## 02 Primary Research Question

**What risks threaten historically significant Black digital material?**

A risk-mapping question, not a loss-accounting question. Every risk requires a specific documented case, and each case is classified: confirmed lost / confirmed at-risk / migrated elsewhere / status unknown. Suspected-but-unconfirmed losses stay PROVISIONAL or UNRESOLVED — never VERIFIED.

---

## 03 Why It Matters

The Black web has no default institutional steward. Its material lives on corporate servers governed by terms of service, advertising markets, and acquisition strategies — none of which are preservation strategies. When a platform dies, the historical record of the communities that used it dies with it unless someone else was copying. Black users adopted these platforms at disproportionate rates (Vine's teen users were 31% Black non-Hispanic per Pew, cited by NPR) — so the losses concentrate in the Black historical record. Working premise: **platform-controlled Black cultural memory is Black cultural memory at risk.**

---

## 04 Scope

**In scope:** U.S.-based Black digital material, 1990s–present: defunct or declined Black web portals, forums, and community sites; platform shutdowns/data losses affecting Black digital culture; expired or abandoned domains of Black media outlets, organizations, and artists; API changes, suspensions, and purges with documented impact on Black archives or creators; link rot in Black digital scholarship and journalism; creator-owned material with no institutional backup. Chicago cases where documented.

**Out of scope:** physical archives and digitization (CTL-RB-PRES-000001); non-U.S. material; speculative future risks without documented precedent; private individuals' personal data (hard privacy constraint).

---

## 05 Definitions

**Historically significant Black digital material** (working definition): digital-born content — web pages, forums, social-media posts, audio, video, datasets, code — documenting Black life, culture, organizing, or expression in the United States, whose loss would impair future historical understanding.

**Contested terminology — flagged first:**
- *"The Black web"* is a research question, not a settled category. ATBW's Mellon-funded initiative explicitly aims at "mapping, describing, and producing scholarship about *the Black web*" — the boundaries are not yet agreed.
- *"Loss"* = confirmed destruction or irretrievability. Content that moved (The Undefeated → Andscape) is *migrated*, not lost. Content whose location is unknown is *status unknown*, not lost.
- *"Archive"* = a preservation copy under independent stewardship — not a platform's servers, not a search cache, and not an embedded YouTube video (§17).

## 06 Historical Context

The Black web predates the social web. In 1994–1995, **NetNoir** (E. David Ellington, Malcolm CasSelle) launched on AOL and the open web. **BlackVoices.com** launched in 1995 at the Orlando Sentinel Online with a $5 million Tribune investment, moved to Chicago in 1998, and passed a million registered users. **Africana.com** was founded in January 1999 by Henry Louis Gates Jr. and Kwame Anthony Appiah. **BlackPlanet** launched September 1, 1999 (Omar Wasow, Benjamin Sun) and reached a reported 20 million users at its peak.

This first generation died of capital starvation and consolidation. Charlton McIlwain (Marketplace, 2021): Black founders "did not have the kind of networks of capital and investment" of white peers; "the capital dried up," and promising companies were acquired and absorbed. Africana.com was bought by Time Warner in 2000 (reported ~$10M) and closed January 31, 2005, features folded into AOL BlackVoices. AOL bought BlackVoices from Tribune in February 2004. Radio One bought Community Connect (BlackPlanet) for $38 million in April 2008. BlackPlanet survived under Urban One but declined after 2011 as Facebook and Twitter absorbed its users.

The second wave was the social web: MySpace (2003), DatPiff (2005), FakeShoreDrive (2007), Vine (2013), and Black Twitter's role in documenting the 2014 Ferguson protests — which seeded Documenting the Now (2016). Each wave concentrated Black cultural production on infrastructure Black people did not own. Each wave's infrastructure has since failed, been sold, or been fenced off.

## 07 Key Findings

**F1. Platform shutdown is the best-documented loss mechanism, and it is total.** MySpace (March 2019): ~50M songs, 14M artists, plus photos and videos. GeoCities (Oct 2009), Yahoo Groups (Dec 2019–Dec 2020): same pattern — corporate decision, months' notice, whatever was not copied is gone.

**F2. The pioneering Black portals were lost to acquisition and capital starvation.** Africana.com ended in a corporate fold-in; NetNoir is defunct; BlackVoices.com was sold twice and its message boards — the community's actual archive — were removed under HuffPost. Survival under new ownership did not preserve the content that mattered.

**F3. Platform governance now deletes and fences Black archives faster than shutdowns.** Twitter's 2023 API paywall ($100/mo Basic; reported $42,000/mo Enterprise) "robbed" Documenting the Now's Catalog, Hydrator, and twarc "of much of their usefulness" (Walsh, 2023). Facebook's 2018 purges removed hundreds of pages including Black-themed ones. Pew (May 2024): nearly 1 in 5 tweets gone within months.

**F4. The DatPiff case is the sharpest unresolved loss.** The defining mixtape platform of hip-hop's blog era went dark in March 2023 after its 2019 MediaLab acquisition; the announced Internet Archive rescue collection (~366,420 mixtapes) then disappeared without explanation. UNRESOLVED — no official statement from any party.

**F5. Nobody measured the Black share of any general-platform loss.** Platforms never published demographic breakdowns of lost content; Black collecting institutions were not web-archiving at scale when the losses happened. Documented disproportionate Black *use* makes disproportionate Black *loss* the reasonable inference — stated as inference.

**F6. The rescue infrastructure is real but young and small.** ATBW ($2.5M Mellon, March 2023), Documenting the Now (2016), the Mixtape Museum — all postdate most of the losses they were built to prevent.

Clutch Magazine web edition: SUPPORTED (domain status only) — clutchmagazine.com redirected to a GoDaddy for-sale page 2026-09-28; content disposition UNRESOLVED. The Mixtape Museum (Regan Sommer McCoy): SUPPORTED — community mixtape archive, hip-hop priority.

No *content* loss evidenced — service and trust were the casualties. The near-miss matters: nearly every case in this brief depends on the Wayback Machine as the archive of last resort.

## 09 Analysis

**The pattern.** Losses cluster at corporate transitions — acquisition (Africana, BlackVoices, BlackPlanet, DatPiff), business-model failure (GeoCities, Yahoo Groups), governance shifts (Twitter API, Facebook purges, Vine's managed decline). Black digital material is rarely the target; it is *collateral*. That is worse for preservation planning: collateral damage generates no villain, no lawsuit, no press cycle demanding restoration.

**The measurement problem.** Platforms never published demographic breakdowns of lost content, and the web-archiving practice "developed mostly within… well-resourced, primarily white, academic institutions" (ATBW's premise). The Black share of every general-platform loss is therefore unknowable — a finding, not a research failure: it is the direct consequence of who was (not) archiving.

**The migration trap.** "The content moved" is the most common false comfort. Africana.com's features "moved to AOL BlackVoices" — but AOL BlackVoices itself dissolved into HuffPost, whose message boards were removed. Migration ≠ preservation.

**The governance turn.** The newest risk is enclosure, not shutdown: APIs repriced, archives made read-only then deleted (Vine), accounts purged under contested "inauthentic behavior" rules, archivists' accounts suspended for trying to preserve (Yahoo Groups). Preservation that depends on a platform's permission is not preservation.

**The single-point-of-failure economy.** The most significant current Black digital material — independent music blogs, mixtape archives, critic sites — is held by individuals or two-person teams. When the founder stops paying the hosting bill, the archive ends.

## 19 Competing Interpretations

1. **Wasow's BlackPlanet→MySpace claim.** Plausible, widely repeated, self-interested; carried by Wikipedia. PROVISIONAL.
2. **The DatPiff disappearance.** Copyright takedown? Technical removal? Write-down abandonment? No official statement; all live. UNRESOLVED.
3. **Facebook's 2018 purges.** Frame A: protective moderation (incl. removing the fake 700K-follower "Black Lives Matter" page). Frame B: a private company deleting Black political organizing spaces under opaque rules. Same events; framing DISPUTED.
4. **@Blacktivist in the DocNow archive.** Include (documents the information environment) or exclude (purifies the movement record)? DocNow's own white paper treats this as an open ethical problem. DISPUTED.
5. **"The content moved, so nothing was lost."** Fails on inspection: each migration (Africana→BlackVoices→HuffPost) shed the community layer that was the actual archive. Migration ≠ preservation.

## 21 Limitations

- **Snippet ceiling.** Several claims rest on search-result records from reputable outlets (SUPPORTED, not VERIFIED).
- **Wayback blocked.** HTTP 429 prevented snapshot verification of dead domains; those claims stay UNRESOLVED.
- **Selection bias.** Documented cases only; undocumented losses are invisible. General-platform losses are better documented than Black-specific ones — the evidence base reflects who had resources to write the history.
- **Survivorship bias.** BlackPlanet survived and is therefore over-represented relative to portals that vanished without a trace.
- **No Black-specific link-rot measurement exists** in the sources found.
- **Time-boxed.** Domain statuses and platform states as of 2026-09-28.
