## §5B. The Afterlife: Black Chicago Online, 2005–2020 (new in v0.2)

The institutions v0.1 watched go online did not stay still. The afterlife is the story of what the early web *became* for Black Chicago: a flagship newspaper surrendering print, a social network proving a market the industry doubted, a new generation founding a newsroom natively online, a music practice breaking through YouTube, and an access gap that refused to close.

### F8. The Defender ended print in 2019 after 114 years (SUPPORTED)

Real Times Media announced in July 2019 that the *Chicago Defender* — founded 1905 — would cease its print edition and continue as an online publication. The final print edition published that week; the paper had been weekly since 2008. Contemporary coverage is multi-sourced and independent: NPR's *Code Switch* (July 10, 2019), WBEZ ("After 114 Year Run," July 10, 2019), and the *Chicago Tribune* (July 2019), which quoted Real Times Media CEO Hiram Jackson: "The numbers are evident that the best way to do that is through doubling down on our digital platform." Jackson told the *Tribune* the paper printed 16,000 weekly copies but reached 475,000 unique monthly visitors online. The *Tribune*'s editorial board marked the 114-year run explicitly.

This closes the arc v0.1 opened: the "web holdout" of December 2004 became, fifteen years later, an online-only paper — the late adopter outliving its own print form.

- **Grade:** SUPPORTED (multiple independent contemporary sources: NPR, WBEZ, Chicago Tribune).
- **Claim ID:** CL-020.
- **Source hunt (complete for this claim):** NPR Code Switch 2019-07-10; WBEZ 2019-07-10; Chicago Tribune via ND Insider 2019-07-07. Remaining: an archived copy of the final print edition's front page; Real Times Media's original announcement text.

### F9. BlackPlanet demonstrated the market investors doubted (PROVISIONAL)

The audit asserts BlackPlanet grew to about 15 million users despite investor skepticism that Black people were online at all. If true, this is the afterlife's market-structure finding: the audience the NTIA access gap described as disconnected was, in aggregate, a massive online community — the gap measured *households with home access*, not *people online*.

- **Grade:** PROVISIONAL. The ~15M figure and the investor-doubt framing are both unsourced assertions.
- **Claim ID:** CL-021.
- **Source hunt:** contemporary press profiles of BlackPlanet at peak (c. 2001–2008); founder interviews (Omar Wasow and co-founders) stating user counts; Comscore/Nielsen historical estimates; any named investor or contemporaneous quote expressing doubt about Black internet adoption. Do not cite the figure until one of these is recovered.

### F10. The TRiiBE founded a Black Chicago newsroom natively online (SUPPORTED)

*The TRiiBE* — an African-American online news and digital media company based in Chicago — was founded in 2017 by Morgan Elise Johnson and Tiffany Walden, with web developer David Elutilo. Corroboration is multi-sourced: Northwestern Magazine (February 2017 co-founding account), the Gateway Journalism Review (Field Foundation grant coverage, "launched in 2017"), Medill Reports ("Launched in 2017… exists exclusively online"), and the Local News Lab (Indiegogo campaign, $20,000+ from ~500 supporters; Field Foundation grant). The founders — Northwestern graduates and Alpha Kappa Alpha sorority sisters — built the outlet explicitly to counter mainstream coverage that reduced Black Chicago to crime narratives.

The TRiiBE is the generational inversion of the Defender story: where the legacy paper spent a decade resisting the web, the TRiiBE never had a print edition to surrender. It is also the answer to v0.1's §19 (missing women operators): a Black-woman-founded, Black-woman-led digital newsroom.

- **Grade:** SUPPORTED (multiple independent sources).
- **Claim ID:** CL-022.
- **Source hunt (substantially complete):** Wikipedia entry (founders, 2017, Chicago); Northwestern Magazine; Gateway Journalism Review; Medill Reports; Local News Lab. Remaining: founding traffic/audience figures; the 2019 TRiiBE Guide print edition as evidence of print *returning* as a prestige object.

### F11. Drill broke through YouTube before the industry noticed (PROVISIONAL)

The audit asserts Chief Keef's "Bang" had more than 400,000 YouTube views before his 2011 arrest — the canonical drill-breakout anecdote: a Chicago-born hip-hop practice (drill) reaching a mass audience through a video platform while the legacy music press was still oriented to radio and print.

- **Grade:** PROVISIONAL. The 400K figure and its timing relative to the arrest are unsourced.
- **Claim ID:** CL-023.
- **Source hunt:** contemporary 2011–2012 profiles of Keef's rise (Chicago Tribune, Complex, Pitchfork, Fakeshoredrive) stating view counts; arrest-date confirmation (2011); any archived YouTube view-count snapshot. Treat the figure as lore until a contemporary source is recovered.

### F12–F13. The access gap persisted into the broadband era (PROVISIONAL × 2)

The audit asserts: in the least-connected South and West Side neighborhoods, up to 40% of households have no internet (CL-024); the 10 least-connected areas are 72% Black (CL-025). If verified, this is the afterlife's structural finding — the NTIA-era racial gap of v0.1 §9 did not close with broadband; it *geographized* into specific neighborhoods.

- **Grade:** PROVISIONAL (both). No source dataset named.
- **Source hunt:** identify the underlying study (candidate sources: University of Chicago/Internet Equity Initiative, Chicago Connected program data, Census ACS 5-year table B28002 cross-tabulated by race, or Microsoft broadband usage data). Needed: study name, year, methodology, and the exact "least-connected" definition. Do not publish the 40%/72% figures until the dataset is in hand.

## Red-team notes on the afterlife material (v0.2)

1. **Audit-source risk.** All six afterlife claims arrived from a single growth audit with no citations. Two were corroborated (F8, F10); four remain PROVISIONAL. The brief must never let the corroborated two lend credibility to the uncorroborated four — they are graded independently.
2. **The "Bang" number is lore-shaped.** 400,000 is a suspiciously round figure typical of retrospective myth-making. Even if a contemporary source states it, treat as one data point, not a measured fact.
3. **The 40%/72% figures are the most dangerous in the brief.** Precise percentages without a named dataset invite citation laundering — the numbers will travel stripped of their PROVISIONAL grade. Do not publish, pitch, or brief these figures until the dataset is recovered. If asked, say: "an unsourced audit assertion currently being verified."
4. **TRiiBE vs Defender framing.** "Generational inversion" is the brief's interpretation, not a sourced claim. Keep the facts (dates, founders) separate from the reading.
5. **No causation on the access gap.** As in v0.1 §5 of red-team.md: the brief does not claim the access gap *caused* any institutional outcome.

## §26. Why this record matters (the preservation imperative)

Black Chicago's online history is disappearing faster than it is being documented. The BBSs left no archives. The listservs left no archives. The church websites of 1998 are gone. The Defender's 114-year print run ended with a week's notice in July 2019 — and the paper's own digital continuity now depends on a website, a platform the paper resisted for a decade.

Every gap in v0.1 (§7, F7) is a small extinction: a community that was online and left no recoverable trace. The afterlife section exists because the pattern repeated — BlackPlanet's peak, drill's YouTube breakout, the TRiiBE's founding — each at risk of becoming lore instead of record.

This is the donor-facing reason the brief exists: **CultureTechLens documents Black Chicago's digital life with the same evidence grades it brings to everything else — before the record rots.** The archive is not nostalgic; it is infrastructural. A community that cannot prove it was online cannot defend what it built there.
