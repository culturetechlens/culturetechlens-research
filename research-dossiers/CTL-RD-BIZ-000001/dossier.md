## 01 · Identity

**Canonical name:** Johnson Publishing Company (JPC)

The Johnson Publishing Company was a Chicago-based Black-owned publishing
company founded in November 1942 by John H. Johnson, publisher of *Negro
Digest* (1942), *Ebony* (1945), *Jet* (1951), *Tan/Tan Confessions*, *Black
World*, and other titles, and owner-operator of Fashion Fair Cosmetics, the
Ebony Fashion Fair traveling show, WJPC radio, and the JPC Book Division. It
operated continuously for 77 years and ended in a Chapter 7 bankruptcy
liquidation filed April 9, 2019, in Chicago (Case 19-10236, N.D. Ill.).

**Disambiguation.** JPC-the-organization is distinct from (a) the **JPC
Archive** (the company's photographic and audiovisual archive, a separate
entity now jointly owned by the Smithsonian's National Museum of African
American History and Culture and the J. Paul Getty Trust) — linked by the
`archived-at` edge, per the audit near-miss rule; (b) the Johnson Publishing
Company Building at 820 S. Michigan Avenue (the building is a separate
entity; the company `headquartered-at` it); (c) Johnson Products Company, a
separate Chicago Black-owned business (founder George E. Johnson; hair-care
products) documented in CTL-DR-ECON-001, which Johnson Publishing's business
model is sometimes confused with; and (d) Robert L. Johnson (BET founder),
who appears in CTL-DR-MEDIA-001 records.

## 02 · Executive Profile

Johnson Publishing Company (1942–2019) was the largest Black-owned
publishing company of its era and one of the most consequential Black
businesses in American history. Founded by John H. Johnson on a $500 loan
secured by his mother's furniture, JPC proved the existence of a
multibillion-dollar Black consumer market to reluctant white corporate
advertisers — Zenith signed the first major advertising contract in 1947 —
and insisted that ads in its pages feature Black models. Its magazines
chronicled Black achievement and Black life for three generations: *Ebony*'s
initial 25,000-copy run (November 1945) sold out; *Jet*'s September 1955
publication of the Emmett Till open-casket photographs is widely credited
with catalyzing national civil-rights attention; *Ebony* carried Martin
Luther King Jr.'s "Advice for Living" column (1957–1958).

CTL establishes: the November 1942 founding; the five-address Chicago
headquarters history (Supreme Life Building → 5619 S. State → 1820 S.
Michigan → 820 S. Michigan, December 1971–2011 → 200 S. Michigan at
dissolution); the product line and its dates; Eunice W. Johnson's distinct
roles (named *Ebony*; created the Ebony Fashion Fair, 1958; founded Fashion
Fair Cosmetics, 1973); the June 2016 sale of *Ebony* and *Jet* to Clear View
Group (price undisclosed); the April 9, 2019 Chapter 7 liquidation; and the
July 2019 $30M consortium rescue and July 28, 2022 joint NMAAHC/Getty
ownership of the JPC Archive (~4 million images).

What remains uncertain: precise revenue and employment figures (company
directories give conflicting numbers); the identity of the $500-loan lender;
the primary documentation of the 2009 R.R. Donnelley mortgage; the exact
2016 sale price; and the whereabouts of the company's non-photographic
business records. See §28 and `gaps.md`.

## 03 · Significance

- **VERIFIED.** JPC was the largest Black-owned publishing company of its
  era and one of the most consequential Black businesses in American
  history. (BPM-003 co-jpc; company self-description; Congressional Record
  tributes.) It created the first sustained, nationally circulated
  Black-owned picture-magazine infrastructure: *Negro Digest* (1942), *Ebony*
  (1945), *Jet* (1951).
- **VERIFIED.** JPC demonstrated the economic power of Black consumer
  markets to white corporate America, converting advertising skepticism into
  contracts (Zenith 1947, Chrysler 1954, among others) and setting the
  industry precedent that Black publications could carry national brand
  advertising.
- **VERIFIED.** Its publications served as the visual and narrative record
  of the Black freedom struggle for three generations — *Jet*'s Emmett Till
  photographs (September 1955) and *Ebony*'s documentation of civil-rights
  leadership are primary-source material in the civil-rights historiography.
- **VERIFIED.** Through Eunice W. Johnson's institutions — the Ebony
  Fashion Fair (1958–2009; $55M+ raised) and Fashion Fair Cosmetics
  (1973) — JPC built the first national Black beauty economy: a traveling
  couture circuit and the first prestige cosmetics line formulated for Black
  women, preceding the modern inclusive-beauty industry by four decades.
- **VERIFIED.** The July 2019 $30M rescue of the JPC Archive — the
  century's largest Black-media archival event (per CTL-DR-MEDIA-001) —
  preserved roughly 4 million images of 20th-century Black life under joint
  NMAAHC/Getty stewardship.
- **SUPPORTED.** The company's arc — self-financed Black capital formation,
  exclusion from bank credit, real-estate self-construction as strategy,
  diversification, decline under digital disruption, and liquidation — is a
  case study in both Black business achievement and the structural limits of
  Black capital access in the 20th century.

Without JPC, the record would lack: the dominant Black picture-magazine
tradition of 1945–2019; the most complete commercial photographic archive of
Black American life in the 20th century; the first national Black beauty
economy; and the template for Black consumer-market advertising economics.

## 06 · Geography

Chicago, Illinois, throughout the company's life. Headquarters history,
each address documented (see §11 for building entities):

1. **3501 S. Parkway Ave** (now Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Drive), inside
   the Supreme Life Building — 1942–1943. Johnson's first office was in
   attorney Earl B. Dickerson's law library on the building's second floor.
   The company operated from the SLL law library in its earliest days.
2. **5619 S. State Street**, Washington Park — purchased 1943 (a small
   one-story retail/storefront building, $4,000). Extant as of the City's
   landmark report.
3. **1820 S. Michigan Avenue** — purchased and rehabilitated 1949 (the
   former Hursen Funeral Home; demolished). The company remained through
   1971.
4. **820 S. Michigan Avenue** — purpose-built 11-story headquarters,
   December 1971–2011 (sale late 2010, closed January 2011). Designed by
   Black architect John Warren Moutoussamy; the first Black-owned building
   in Chicago's Loop and the only Chicago high-rise designed by an African
   American; designated a Chicago Landmark (fall 2017).
5. **200 S. Michigan Avenue, Suite 900, Chicago, IL 60604** — final
   principal place of business, per the April 9, 2019 bankruptcy petition.

Migration arc: the company's geography is a South-Side-to-Loop ascent —
from the Black Belt (Supreme Life Building, 5619 S. State) up Michigan
Avenue into downtown, a deliberate capital-formation strategy culminating in
the self-built 820 S. Michigan tower.

## 07 · Biography/History

**Founding (1942).** John H. Johnson, an office assistant and in-house
magazine editor at Chicago's Supreme Life Insurance Company (later Supreme
Liberty Life), conceived a Black *Reader's Digest*-style magazine while
clipping Black newspapers for his employer's publication. Unable to obtain
a bank loan — in his own account, because banks would not lend to Black
borrowers — he borrowed $500 using his mother's furniture as collateral,
mailed 20,000 charter-subscription offers to Supreme Life policyholders at
$2 each, received 3,000 replies ($6,000), and printed the first issue of
*Negro Digest* in November 1942. Early distribution was built through
guerrilla tactics: co-workers asking for the magazine at South Side
newsstands, friends buying copies Johnson then reimbursed and resold, and
mail carriers used to map target neighborhoods. Within a year circulation
reached 50,000; Eleanor Roosevelt's 1943 "If I Were a Negro" contribution
doubled it overnight. (Claims C-001, C-002, C-003.)

**Building the empire (1945–1971).** *Ebony* launched November 1, 1945 —
patterned on *Life*/*Look*, named by Eunice Johnson "thinking of the dark
wood," 25,000 copies selling out. Zenith signed the first major advertising
contract in 1947; by 1948 Pepsi-Cola, Colgate, Beech-Nut, Seagram, and
Remington Rand advertised; Chesterfield ran the first full-color ads in a
Black magazine; Chrysler signed in 1954. In 1952 Johnson told *Advertising
Age* the Black consumer market, "$15 billion strong," was "ripe and
ready." JPC insisted advertisements feature Black models. *Jet* launched
November 1, 1951, as a pocket-size weekly billed "The Weekly Negro News
Magazine." The company added *Tan Confessions* (1950), *Hue* and *Copper
Romance* (1953), *Ebony Jr!* (1973), *Ebony Man* (1985), *Ebony South
Africa* (1995), a book division, Supreme Beauty Products, Mahogany Travel,
and EBCO International. In 1958 Eunice Johnson launched the Ebony Fashion
Fair from a Jessie Dent/Dillard University charity fundraiser; she produced
and directed it from 1961, building a 200-city traveling couture circuit
that raised over $55 million for charity and scholarships. Unable to find
makeup for her models, she founded Fashion Fair Cosmetics in 1973 — the
first national prestige makeup line for Black women. (Claims C-017–C-027,
C-034, C-035.)

**Movement years.** JPC's publications were the movement's picture press.
*Jet*'s September 1955 publication of David Jackson's open-casket
photographs of Emmett Till is widely credited with catalyzing national
attention to the lynching. *Ebony* published Martin Luther King Jr.'s
"Advice for Living" column (1957–1958). Staff photographer Moneta Sleet Jr.
chronicled King for 13 years; his 1969 Pulitzer Prize for the Coretta Scott
King funeral photograph made him the first African American man to win the
Pulitzer in journalism. Johnson himself served as a presidential envoy —
accompanying Vice President Nixon to nine African nations (1957), special
ambassador to the Ivory Coast (JFK, 1961) and to Kenya (LBJ), and a Nixon
appointee to the UN 25th Anniversary commission. (Claims C-009, C-010,
C-020, C-047, C-048.)

**The tower and the peak.** In December 1971 JPC moved into its
purpose-built 11-story headquarters at 820 S. Michigan Avenue, designed by
Black architect John Warren Moutoussamy — Johnson's answer to a decade of
bank refusals to finance the building, and the first Black-owned building
in Chicago's Loop. The land had been bought for $250,000 cash through a
white lawyer acting in trust after agents refused to deal with Johnson once
they learned he was Black. The company bought WGRT radio for $1.8 million
in May 1973 (WJPC — the first Black-owned Chicago radio station; sold
December 1994). At peak the company reported on the order of $425–488
million in annual revenue and roughly 2,000 employees (figures vary by
source and year; see §25). (Claims C-025, C-030, C-031, C-036.)

**Succession and decline.** John H. Johnson led the company until his death
on August 8, 2005. Daughter Linda Johnson Rice became CEO in 2002 and the
company's last chairman/CEO. Eunice Johnson died January 3, 2010. Desiree
Rogers served as CEO from August 2010 (returning to Chicago after serving
as President Obama's White House Social Secretary, 2008–2010). *Jet* ended
print in June 2014; in 2015 JPC sought $40 million for its photo archive to
reduce debt. In June 2016 JPC sold *Ebony* and *Jet* to Clear View Group —
an Austin, Texas-based Black-owned private equity firm chaired by Michael
Gibson — which created Ebony Media Corp; JPC retained Fashion Fair
Cosmetics and the photo archive. The sale price was never publicly
disclosed. (Claims C-008, C-038, C-040, C-046.)

**Bankruptcy and afterlife.** On April 9, 2019, Johnson Publishing Company,
LLC filed for Chapter 7 liquidation (Case 19-10236, U.S. Bankruptcy Court,
N.D. Illinois), listing 200–999 creditors and $10–50 million in assets and
liabilities; trustee Miriam "Mimi" Stein was appointed. The company blamed
a "tidal wave of marketplace changes and business issues": the bankruptcy
of a major retailer carrying Fashion Fair Cosmetics, a costly product
recall, and digital competition. The archive — pledged years earlier to
secure a $12 million loan from Capital Holdings V, LLC (Mellody Hobson and
George Lucas), in default for about three years — was bought at the July
2019 bankruptcy auction for $30 million by a consortium of the Ford
Foundation, the J. Paul Getty Trust, the John D. and Catherine T.
MacArthur Foundation, and the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation. On July 28,
2022, ownership was transferred jointly to the Smithsonian's National
Museum of African American History and Culture and the Getty Research
Institute, which is physically housing and digitizing the archive with a
$30 million Getty commitment and a public-access target of 2029. Fashion
Fair Cosmetics was sold out of bankruptcy in November 2019 to Cheryl
Mayberry McKissack and Desiree Rogers for $1.85 million. *Ebony*'s assets
were bought by Bridgeman Sports and Media for $14 million in December 2020
and relaunched in March 2021. (Claims C-041–C-046.)

## 09 · People

**Founders and family**

- **John H. Johnson (1918-01-19 – 2005-08-08)** — Founder, publisher,
  chairman. Born John Harold Johnson in Arkansas City, Arkansas, grandson
  of enslaved people; moved to Chicago 1933; DuSable High School honor
  student (class president, school-paper editor, graduated 1936);
  office assistant and in-house magazine editor at Supreme Liberty Life
  Insurance; took courses at the University of Chicago and Northwestern
  (no degree). Founded JPC November 1942; led it until his death.
  Honors: NAACP Spingarn Medal (1966), *Black Enterprise* Entrepreneur of
  the Decade (1987), Presidential Medal of Freedom (1996), first African
  American on the Forbes 400 (1982), 35 honorary doctorates. Married
  Eunice Walker, June 21, 1941, Selma, Alabama. Children: John Harold
  Johnson Jr. (1956–1981; died of sickle-cell anemia) and Linda Johnson
  Rice. Presidential envoy roles (see C-048). VERIFIED. Canon ID:
  `canon-john-h-johnson`.
- **Eunice W. Johnson (1916 – 2010-01-03)** — Secretary-treasurer of JPC;
  named *Ebony* ("thinking of the dark wood"); created and produced/directed
  the Ebony Fashion Fair (show launched 1958; she directed from 1961 to
  2009); founded Fashion Fair Cosmetics (1973). Born Selma, Alabama, to a
  physician father and educator mother; maternal grandfather founded and
  led Selma University; Talladega College (sociology, art minor); Loyola
  University Chicago (master's, social work); journalism at Northwestern;
  interior decorating at Ray-Vogue. Delta Sigma Theta member. Black
  Enterprise's 2010 obituary calls her co-founder of JPC; CTL grades formal
  co-founder status PROVISIONAL. Died at her Chicago home, age 93. VERIFIED
  except co-founder status. Dossier-local ID:
  `RD-BIZ-000001-002`.
- **Linda Johnson Rice (b. 1958)** — Daughter of John H. and Eunice
  Johnson; president/COO, then CEO of JPC (2002–2019); the company's last
  chairman/CEO. VERIFIED. Dossier-local ID: `RD-BIZ-000001-003`.

- **C-035 — VERIFIED.** JPC insisted that advertisements in its

- **C-036 — SUPPORTED.** At peak JPC reported roughly $425–488 million

- **C-039 — PROVISIONAL.** In 2009 R.R. Donnelley took a ~$12 million
  mortgage against JPC's headquarters over unpaid printing bills. (Needs
  primary confirmation.)

- **C-040 — VERIFIED.** In June 2016 JPC sold *Ebony* and *Jet* to Clear

  publicly disclosed.

- **C-045 — VERIFIED.** The JPC Archive comprises more than 3 million

- **C-046 — VERIFIED.** *Jet* print ended June 2014; *Ebony* print ended

**Movement and significance**

- **C-047 — VERIFIED.** JPC's publications documented the civil-rights

## 26 · Controversies/Conflicts

1. **The Donnelley mortgage (2009).** R.R. Donnelley allegedly took a
   ~$12M mortgage against the 820 S. Michigan HQ over unpaid printing
   bills. PROVISIONAL — needs Donnelley filings or JPC bankruptcy
   schedules. If confirmed, it marks the point the printer became the
   company's secured creditor.
2. **The 2016 sale terms.** The Clear View Group purchase price was never
   publicly disclosed; CTL does not repeat rumored figures. What is
   documented: JPC retained Fashion Fair Cosmetics and the photo archive;
   Linda Johnson Rice took a board seat at the new entity; Kyra Kyles
   became editor-in-chief.
3. **The #Ebonyowes affair (2016–2017).** Under Clear View/Ebony Media
   Corp ownership, freelancers complained of late or non-payment; a class
   action paid out $80,000; ~1/3 of staff laid off spring 2017 (CJR).
   This concerns the successor, not JPC proper, but it altered public
   perception of the *Ebony* brand (C-052, SUPPORTED).
4. **The archive's $12M pledge.** The archive was pledged to Capital
   Holdings V (Hobson/Lucas) and in default ~3 years; the trustee had a
   <3-month window to liquidate while traditional auction houses
   (Christie's, Sotheby's) had failed to sell it (Bankruptcy IDEA).
   SUPPORTED.
5. **Consortium composition.** The Art Newspaper (2022) names the
   Smithsonian as a 2019 co-purchaser; Pulitzer (2019) names only the four
   foundations. Recorded as an unresolved discrepancy, not adjudicated
   (C-043).
6. **Eunice Johnson's co-founder status.** Black Enterprise calls her
   co-founder; CTL has not located incorporation records. PROVISIONAL
   (C-005).
7. **Robert E. Johnson's record.** BPM-003 red-team downgraded his Jet
   role to Provisional (weak-source only); death year 1995 vs 1996
   contested within CTL. Not for public use until re-sourced (C-014).
8. **Editorial criticism (historical).** *Ebony* was criticized for
   focusing on successful people and aspirational imagery; Johnson's 1992
   AP defense: it "inspired them to be successful by showing examples of
   blacks who have made it." Recorded as contemporary criticism, not CTL
   adjudication.
9. **Ben Burns's removal (c. 1953–54).** The Manchester thesis frames it
   as a recession-driven shift away from sensational content; Burns's own
   drafts criticized successor editors. Multiple perspectives recorded;
   no CTL adjudication beyond the documented mechanism.

## 27 · Myth vs. Evidence

1. **MYTH:** "*Ebony* had 9 million subscribers." → **VERDICT:** False
   (conflation). → **EVIDENCE:** AP's 2005 obituary documents peak
   circulation ~2.3M (1985) and ~1.6M (2005); the Congressional Record's
   11M/8M figures are *readership* (pass-along). BPM-003 explicitly
   rejected the 9M figure as inflated. → **CORRECTED:** "*Ebony* peaked
   at about 2.3 million in paid circulation (1985); its pass-along
   readership was estimated in the millions — not 9 million
   subscribers." (Evidence file: REFUTED; ledger C-050: DISPUTED
   "refuted — see CTL-EF-BIZ-000001".)

2. **MYTH:** "John H. Johnson founded JPC with a bank loan." →
   **VERDICT:** False. → **EVIDENCE:** Johnson's own account
   (autobiography; Congressional tributes): banks would not lend to
   Black borrowers; the $500 came via his mother's furniture as
   collateral. → **CORRECTED:** "Johnson financed the founding with a
   $500 loan secured by his mother's furniture, precisely because bank
   credit was closed to him."

3. **MYTH:** "The Ebony Fashion Fair began in 1961." → **VERDICT:**
   Exaggerated (compression). → **EVIDENCE:** The show launched in 1958
   from the Jessie Dent/Dillard fundraiser; Eunice Johnson became its
   producer/director in 1961 and ran it to 2009. → **CORRECTED:**
   "Launched 1958; Eunice Johnson produced and directed it from 1961."

4. **MYTH:** "JPC still publishes *Ebony* and *Jet*." → **VERDICT:**
   False. → **EVIDENCE:** Sold to Clear View Group (June 2016);
   Chapter 7 liquidation (April 2019); *Ebony* assets to Bridgeman
   (Dec 2020). JPC published nothing after 2016. → **CORRECTED:**
   "JPC sold *Ebony* and *Jet* in 2016 and was liquidated in 2019;
   successor owners publish the titles."

5. **MYTH:** "The $30M archive rescue was a purchase by the
   Smithsonian alone." → **VERDICT:** Exaggerated. → **EVIDENCE:**
   Pulitzer (2019) names four foundations as purchasers; The Art
   Newspaper (2022) includes the Smithsonian among purchasers. Joint
   NMAAHC/Getty ownership began July 28, 2022 — three years after the
   purchase. → **CORRECTED:** "A four-foundation consortium bought the
   archive for $30M in July 2019; the Smithsonian (NMAAHC) and Getty
   became joint owners in July 2022."
