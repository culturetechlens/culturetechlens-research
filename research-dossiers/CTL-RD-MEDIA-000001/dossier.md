## 01 · Identity

**Canonical name:** WVON (1450 AM / 1390 AM / 1450 AM / 1690 AM — call sign continuous since 1963)
**Dossier ID:** CTL-RD-MEDIA-000001
**Entity type:** Radio station (AM broadcast; Black-oriented music 1963–1986; Black-oriented talk 1986–present)
**One-sentence identification:** WVON is Chicago's heritage Black radio station — launched April 1, 1963 by Chess Records owners Leonard and Phil Chess as "The Voice of the Negro," now "The Voice of the Nation," a Black-owned talk station operated by Midway Broadcasting Corporation on 1690 AM.
**Disambiguation:** This dossier covers the WVON *call sign and station lineage* across its frequency migrations (1450 → 1390 → 1450 → 1690). It is not the 1390 AM facility now known as WGCI (a separate FCC facility whose calls were WVON 1975–1983), nor the 1450 AM facility's other time-share occupant WCEV. Facility-vs.-station distinction is documented in §05 and §26. Not to be confused with WJPC (`canon-wjpc`) or WGCI (`canon-wgci`), separate Chicago Black-radio entities.

## 02 · Executive Profile

WVON is the longest-running institutional voice in Chicago Black radio: a single call-sign lineage spanning 1963 to the present, through four ownership eras and three frequency moves. CTL establishes, from primary and strong secondary evidence, the complete ownership/frequency timeline: WHFC (1926–1963) → Chess brothers purchase and WVON launch (April 1, 1963) → Globetrotter Communications (sale consummated 1970, $9M) → 1450-to-1390 move (February 5, 1975) → Combined Communications/Gannett (1977–78) → Midway Broadcasting's 1450 license (WXOL, August 1979) → WVON calls restored to 1450 (March 28, 1984) → all-talk format (March 1986) → 1690 AM via LMA with Clear Channel/iHeartMedia (September 18, 2006). CTL's strongest findings: (1) the Chess-era station (1963–1969) functioned as both a Chess Records marketing engine and a civil-rights communications hub — the dual role is the subject of a 2012 Loyola PhD dissertation; (2) the widely repeated claim that WVON's *talk format* powered Harold Washington's 1983 mayoral campaign is temporally impossible and REFUTED — the music-era station backed Washington via journalist Lu Palmer's organizing, while the talk format (March 1986) served the 1987 reelection and later campaigns; (3) the station's Black ownership was restored in 1979 when former WVON personalities Pervis Spann and Wesley South formed Midway Broadcasting, and continues today under Spann's daughter Melody Spann Cooper. What remains uncertain: the exact date the "Voice of the Negro" branding became "Voice of the Nation"; the WNUS-AM-FM purchase price ($4.45M vs. $3.55M in competing sources); and advertiser-level economics of the Chess era, which survive only in fragmentary trade-press traces.

## 03 · Significance

- **CLAIM (VERIFIED):** WVON is the only Chicago Black radio station with a continuous call-sign lineage from the civil-rights era to the present, making it the primary broadcast witness to Black Chicago's post-1963 political and cultural history.
- **CLAIM (VERIFIED):** During the Chess era (1963–1969), WVON fused commercial R&B broadcasting with civil-rights journalism — interviewing movement leaders, broadcasting Operation Breadbasket meetings, and deploying DJs with loudspeaker trucks to calm the West Side after King's assassination — a dual commercial/movement function documented in a dedicated scholarly dissertation (Searcy 2012).
- **CLAIM (VERIFIED):** The March 1986 conversion to all-talk created Chicago's first Black talk-radio format, a programming innovation competitors (notably Gannett's WGCI-AM in January 1989) then imitated.
- **CLAIM (SUPPORTED):** WVON's rotation demonstrably moved national record markets in the 1960s — Motown routed premieres through the station first, and its airplay influenced other markets' programming.
- **CLAIM (VERIFIED):** The station's 1979 return to Black ownership (Midway Broadcasting) and its survival as a Black-owned talk outlet into the 2020s make it a rare case of Black media ownership persistence through the consolidation era.
- Without WVON, the record of how Black Chicago *heard itself* — the music, the movement news, the electoral mobilization — would rest almost entirely on print.

## 07 · Biography/History

WVON's lineage begins as **WHFC**, a 1926 Chicago station owned by Richard W. Hoffman that by the 1940s–1960s aired ethnic and African-American programming from Cicero-licensed 1450 AM, moving its studios and transmitter to 3350 S. Kedzie in the early 1950s (Ghrist 1996; Broadcasting 1948) [SUPPORTED]. In early 1963 the Chess brothers — Leonard and Phil Chess, owners of Chess Records — bought WHFC (with FM sister WEHS) for a reported $1 million (Broadcasting 1963-02-18; Billboard 1963-04-13) [VERIFIED/SUPPORTED], seeking both a promotional outlet for the label and a station that could, in their phrase, galvanize Black Chicago (Rush 2019; station's own history) [SUPPORTED].

On **April 1, 1963**, WVON — "The Voice of the Negro" — debuted with the hand-picked "Good Guys": Franklin McCarthy, E. Rodney Jones, Herb Kent (retained from WHFC's staff), Wesley South, and Pervis Spann, under GM Lucky Cordell (Davis 2013; HR0201) [VERIFIED]. Playing R&B and soul, it was by early 1964 the second most-listened Chicago station in evening hours (Billboard 1964-03-28) [SUPPORTED]. The Chess era fused commerce and movement: the station marketed Chess and national R&B product — Motown's Berry Gordy routed every premiere through WVON first (Davis 2013; Danois 2013) [SUPPORTED] — while its news operation interviewed Martin Luther King Jr. and Elijah Muhammad, carried Jesse Jackson's call reporting King's assassination, and sent DJs out with loudspeaker trucks to plead for calm during the post-assassination riots (Davis 2013; Rush 2019) [SUPPORTED]. Loyola historian Jennifer Searcy's 2012 dissertation documents this dual function as the station's defining 1960s role [SUPPORTED].

Leonard Chess died in October 1969; the family sold WVON to **Globetrotter Communications** — George N. Gillett Jr. and Potter Palmer — for $9 million, consummated 1970 (Billboard 1969-12-13; Broadcasting 1970-09-28) [VERIFIED]. Globetrotter bought WNUS-AM-FM from the McLendon interests and on February 5, 1975 moved WVON from 1450 to the 5,000-watt 1390 signal (Tribune 1975-02-06) [VERIFIED]; the WNUS price is reported as $4,450,000 (Tribune) against $3,550,000 in later station histories [DISPUTED]. The 1450 frequency went dormant, then carried WFMT as interim operator from August 1976 (Billboard 1976-09-11) [SUPPORTED]. Under Globetrotter the station cut news and community programming from 1974 and fired the on-air staff in 1977 (DePaul/Ramsay paper, citing Searcy and Benton) [SUPPORTED]. Combined Communications bought Globetrotter in 1977 and merged with Gannett in 1978 (Billboard 1977-05-14; Washington Post 1978-05-09) [VERIFIED].

In 1979 two former WVON personalities, **Pervis Spann and Wesley South**, formed **Midway Broadcasting Corporation** and won the 1450 license; their **WXOL** premiered in August 1979 with a blues format, time-sharing 1450 with Migala Communications' ethnic WCEV from October 1979, both transmitting from the old WVON tower at 3350 S. Kedzie (Davis 2013; Billboard 1979-08-04; FCC history cards; Broadcasting & Cable Yearbook 1981) [VERIFIED]. Gannett, meanwhile, changed the 1390 calls from WVON to WGCI in 1983; Midway reclaimed the heritage calls and on March 28, 1984 WVON returned to 1450 (FCC history cards; station histories) [SUPPORTED], with audience reportedly up 50% after the change [SUPPORTED, single source].

At co-owner Wesley South's urging, WVON dropped music for **all-talk in March 1986** — "changing to a talk format in March '86," per GM Hoyett Owens in a contemporary 1989 interview — creating Chicago's first Black talk-radio format (In These Times 1989-03-15; HR0201; Benton 2007) [VERIFIED]. The talk era's political function is precisely dated by the evidence: the *music-era* station had backed Harold Washington's 1983 campaign through journalist Lu Palmer's "Lou's Notebook" and his "We Shall See in '83" organizing (Davis 2013; HistoryMakers Palmer finding aid) [VERIFIED]; the *talk* format then served Washington's 1987 reelection, Carol Moseley Braun's Senate campaign, and Barack Obama's rise — Obama, as state senator, filled in for host Cliff Kelley in 2002 (In These Times 1989; Davis 2013; Reich 2013) [SUPPORTED]. The claim that the talk format elected Washington in 1983 is temporally impossible and REFUTED (see §27).

On September 18, 2006, WVON's calls and programming moved to **1690 AM** (Berwyn license, 10,000 watts day) under a local marketing agreement with Clear Channel (now iHeartMedia), which owns the facility; Midway Broadcasting operates the station (station histories; FCC call-change record) [VERIFIED]. Studios moved from 3350 S. Kedzie to Chatham's Soft Sheen building in 2007 and to the South Loop in late 2018 (Crusader 2018) [SUPPORTED]. Midway remains Black-owned under Chair/CEO **Melody Spann Cooper**, Pervis Spann's daughter, who began at WVON at age 15, became president/GM in 1994, and bought a controlling Midway interest in 1999 (HistoryMakers bio; Gift of Hope; House testimony 2024) [VERIFIED]. The station continues as "The Voice of the Nation" — when that rebrand replaced "Voice of the Negro" is undocumented [UNRESOLVED].

## 27 · Myth vs. Evidence

**MYTH-001 — "WVON's talk format powered Harold Washington's 1983 mayoral campaign."**
VERDICT: **False (anachronism).** EVIDENCE: Talk format began March 1986 per GM Hoyett Owens (In These Times, 1989); Washington was elected April 1983. What the sources support: the *music-era* station backed Washington through Lu Palmer's organizing ("We Shall See in '83"; HistoryMakers; Davis 2013); the *talk* format served Washington's 1987 reelection (Palmer: "In 1987 the station performed beyond and above the call of duty for Harold's cause"). CORRECTED WORDING: "WVON backed Harold Washington's 1983 campaign as a Black music station; its all-talk format, launched in 1986, then served subsequent campaigns including Washington's 1987 reelection."

**MYTH-002 — "Spann and South bought WVON in 1963 and created the 'Voice of the Negro.'"**
VERDICT: **False.** EVIDENCE: Leonard and Phil Chess bought WHFC and launched WVON April 1, 1963 (Billboard 1963; three legislative sources). Spann and South bought the *1450 license* in 1979 and reclaimed the WVON calls in 1984. The claim appears in an otherwise reliable 2022 Congressional tribute and is internally inconsistent. CORRECTED WORDING: "Spann and South were original 1963 WVON DJs who bought the 1450 frequency in 1979 and restored the WVON call letters in 1984."

**MYTH-003 — "WVON went all-talk in January 1989."**
VERDICT: **False (misattribution).** EVIDENCE: January 1989 is when *WGCI-AM* — Gannett's station on the former WVON 1390 facility — switched to Black talk to compete with WVON (In These Times, 1989). WVON's own switch was March 1986. This resolves the BMI-002 red-team's 1986-vs.-1989 dispute.

**MYTH-004 — "WVON stands for 'The Voice of the Nation' originally" / "Voice of a Nation."**
VERDICT: **Exaggerated/misremembered.** EVIDENCE: All legislative and contemporary sources give the 1963 branding as "Voice of the Negro"; "Voice of the Nation" is the current branding (Rush 2019; station materials), rename date unknown. CORRECTED WORDING: "WVON launched as the 'Voice of the Negro' (1963) and is now branded the 'Voice of the Nation.'"

**MYTH-005 — "The Motown-first arrangement."**
VERDICT: **Substantially true but single-threaded.** EVIDENCE: Davis 2013 tribute + Danois 2013 both report Gordy sent every premiere to WVON first; no contradictory evidence found, but no Motown-company or Gordy-primary confirmation in this pass. Keep attributed.
