## 01 · Identity

**Canonical name:** Chess Records
**Dossier ID:** CTL-RD-MUS-000001
**Entity type:** Business — independent record company (organization; defunct as an active label, 1975)
**One-sentence identification:** Chess Records was the Chicago independent record label founded in 1950 by Polish-Jewish immigrant brothers Leonard and Phil Chess, whose blues, R&B, and rock-and-roll recordings — by Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, Chuck Berry, Bo Diddley, Etta James, Willie Dixon, and others — defined the sound of Chicago blues and shaped rock and roll worldwide.

**Disambiguation:** Not to be confused with (a) the **2120 S. Michigan Avenue building** (a place; this dossier treats organization and building as separate entities per audit rule), (b) **Checker/Argo/Cadet** subsidiary labels (separate entities with their own edges), (c) **Aristocrat Records** (the 1947 predecessor), or (d) the current **UMG/Geffen reissue program** that trades on the Chess name. The Chicago bluesman **Willie Dixon never owned Chess Records** — his widow Marie bought the 2120 building in 1993 (see §27).

## 02 · Executive Profile

Chess Records (1950–1975) was the central institution of the Chicago blues recording industry and one of the most consequential independent labels in American music. Founded by brothers Leonard Chess (1917–1969) and Phil Chess (1921–2016), Polish-Jewish immigrants who entered the business through South Side nightclubs, the label grew from a trunk-distribution operation selling "race records" to Black retail stores into a vertically integrated company — pressing plant, studio, publishing, shipping, and label printing under one roof at 320 E. 21st Street — that put Chicago blues on national charts and, through British rock's embrace, around the world.

CTL establishes the following with confidence: the 1950 founding and the Aristocrat (1947) lineage; the founders' biographies; the address chronology (Cottage Grove storefronts → 2120 S. Michigan, May 1957–1967 → 320 E. 21st, late 1966 onward); the subsidiary and publishing structure (Checker 1952, Marterry/Argo/Cadet, Arc Music 1953); the independent-distribution business model, including Leonard's own trunk sales and a 1954 Louisiana production deal; the 1969 sale to General Recorded Tape for $6.5 million plus stock; and the catalog path through All Platinum to MCA and into Universal Music Group, which owns it today (Geffen/UMe manage reissues). The 1985-vs-1986 and All-Platinum-vs-Sugar-Hill details of the MCA transfer are genuinely disputed and recorded as such.

What remains uncertain: thin spots include Arc Music's founding documentation, the All Platinum wind-down's asset inventory, the 1975 dismantling (hundreds of thousands of records reportedly destroyed), and the exact founder set of Aristocrat. The credit and royalty disputes — Maybellene payola credits, the 1977 Dixon/Waters suits against Arc, the Led Zeppelin suits — are documented facts, not folklore; the "found guilty" framing of the Zeppelin cases is refuted. CTL's standing position holds: Chicago electrified and industrialized the blues; it did not invent the electric blues.

## 03 · Significance

- Chess Records was the recording institution that industrialized the Chicago blues — electrified Delta-derived music captured in a purpose-built studio system, distributed nationally, and marketed across racial lines. [VERIFIED]
- Its catalog is the primary recorded document of the 1950s–60s Chicago blues canon (Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, Little Walter, Willie Dixon) and of early rock and roll (Chuck Berry, Bo Diddley). [VERIFIED]
- The label's recordings were the study texts of the British blues revival: the Rolling Stones named themselves for a Chess single and recorded at 2120 S. Michigan in 1964; Chess masters underpin the rock canon from the Beatles to Led Zeppelin to the Voyager Golden Record ("Johnny B. Goode," 1977). [VERIFIED]
- Chess exemplifies the independent-label business model of the postwar era — self-distribution to Black retail, regional indie distributors, in-house production — and its limits: the 1969 sale and 1975 wind-down trace the consolidation of independents into the conglomerate system that became UMG. [VERIFIED]
- Its royalty and credit disputes (Dixon, Waters, Maybellene payola credits, the Zeppelin suits) are landmark cases in the exploitation-and-recovery history of Black music, directly seeding the Blues Heaven Foundation's royalty-recovery mission. [VERIFIED]
- The 2120 S. Michigan building (Chicago Landmark, 1990; Blues Heaven Foundation museum since 1997) is one of the most visited music-heritage sites in Chicago. [VERIFIED]

## 07 · Biography/History

**Origins (1928–1947).** Lejzor Szmuel Czyż (b. March 12, 1917, near Pinsk) and his brother Fiszel (b. 1921, Motol) were brought to Chicago in 1928 by their Polish-Jewish immigrant family and grew up on the West Side, working with their father in a junk shop at 2979 S. State Street. Leonard moved into liquor distribution and then taverns and nightclubs catering to a primarily Black South Side clientele; the largest was the Macomba Lounge, opened with Phil in 1946. The clubs' live jazz and blues gave the brothers their commercial education in Black music. [VERIFIED]

**Aristocrat (1947–1950).** In 1947 Leonard watched a Hollywood scout talk to Macomba house-band singer Andrew Tibbs; his response — "if he's good enough for Hollywood, I'll put him on record myself" — launched the record business. He bought time at Universal Recording (then in the Civic Opera building) and cut Tibbs's "Union Man Blues." Operating from 2300 E. 71st St., the sessions were issued on Aristocrat Records, owned by a partnership of Leonard, Phil, and investor Evelyn Aron. Leonard handled local distribution himself — records in his car trunk, rounds of record and variety stores, barber shops, beauty salons, and other Black-owned businesses — and acted as distributor for other small independents; he traveled to Detroit, the Midwest, and the South with a wire recorder scouting talent. In January 1950 Phil joined full-time, Aron withdrew to start her own distributing company, and on a Memphis presser's suggestion the label was renamed Chess Records. The first release, Gene Ammons's "My Foolish Heart" (June 1950, no. 1425 — the brothers' childhood apartment number), was a hit. "That was when Chess was really born." [VERIFIED]

**The 1950s: blues and rock and roll.** Building on Aristocrat's jazz/blues base, Chess recorded Delta-derived blues for the Great Migration market: Muddy Waters (recording for the brothers since 1947), Howlin' Wolf (via Sam Phillips's Memphis pipeline), Little Walter, Sonny Boy Williamson, Willie Mabon, J.B. Lenoir. Willie Dixon, employed from the early 1950s to coordinate recordings, became songwriter, arranger, session bassist, and producer — "right arm" to Leonard Chess — and the label became his laboratory for the Chicago blues sound. Checker Records (1952) gave the company a second imprint for radio play. The 1954 deal with Shreveport distributor Stan Lewis tapped the Louisiana Hayride circuit. In 1955 two unknowns — Bo Diddley (spotted street-performing by Dixon) and Chuck Berry of St. Louis ("Maybellene") — made Chess a rock-and-roll label; Berry's and Diddley's records crossed to the pop charts while the label dominated the R&B charts. [VERIFIED]

**2120 S. Michigan (1957–1967).** Purchased late 1956 and occupied in May 1957 after a $20,000 remodel, 2120 was Chess's first owned home: executive offices, shipping room, and a second-floor studio with a concrete-lined master vault — ending dependence on outside studios (a failed 1954 makeshift studio at 4750–52 S. Cottage Grove notwithstanding). The studio, first managed by the Sheldon subsidiary (designed by Jack Weiner), was taken over by Chess in 1958 and renamed Ter-Mar (for Terry and Marshall Chess); engineers Malcolm Chisholm (1958–59, ex-Universal Recording) and Ron Malo (from 1959; built the basement echo chambers) shaped the house sound, with A&R man Ralph Bass recreating the nightclub atmosphere on the floor. Berry cut four of his seven top-ten singles there; the roster ran from Muddy, Wolf, Little Walter, and Sonny Boy through Buddy Guy, Etta James (from 1960), and Koko Taylor to the Moonglows, the Dells, Ramsey Lewis, Ahmad Jamal, and the comedy albums of Moms Mabley and Pigmeat Markham. The Rolling Stones recorded there in June 1964, cutting the tribute "2120 South Michigan Avenue." [VERIFIED]

**The 1960s: expansion and radio.** Chess diversified into soul and R&B (Roquel "Billy" Davis as 1960s A&R/producer; the house session band including Maurice White, Louis Satterfield, Pete Cosey, Phil Upchurch), gospel, jazz, spoken word, and comedy. The brothers entered broadcasting as part-owners of WVON (1963) and acquired WSDM-FM. In 1966 they bought the eight-story factory at 320 E. 21st Street and moved in late 1966–early 1967, building a vertically integrated "music factory": eight-track recording, pressing plant, label printing, shipping — "from performance to record shop, in 24 hours." The move traded streetwise informality for corporate scale, diluting the creative spark even as the company chased the pop market. [VERIFIED]

**Sale and after (1969–).** Announced November 2, 1968 and closed January 1969, the brothers sold Chess to General Recorded Tape for $6.5 million plus 20,000 shares of GRT stock; both brothers and Marshall Chess stayed in management, with Marshall as president. Leonard — ill, feeling "over the hill" — died of a heart attack on October 16, 1969, aged 52. Phil left for WVON, retiring in 1972 to Arizona (d. 2016, age 95). GRT consolidated Chess into Janus by 1972, wound the label down by 1975, and sold the remains to All Platinum Records (Englewood, NJ); Ter-Mar Studios operated at 320 E. 21st until 1979. The catalog passed through the Robinson companies to MCA (1985/86 — year and seller disputed) and, via the MCA/PolyGram merger, into Universal Music Group, which owns it today with Geffen/UMe managing reissues. [VERIFIED/DISPUTED as noted]

**Afterlife.** The 1977 Dixon/Waters suits against Arc, the Arc-vs-Zeppelin suits (1972), and Dixon's personal 1987 Zeppelin settlement are the legal spine of the label's exploitation-and-recovery history — and the direct seed of Willie Dixon's Blues Heaven Foundation (1984), headquartered since 1997 at 2120 S. Michigan, the building his widow Marie bought in 1993 and the city landmarked in 1990. [VERIFIED]

## 24 · For the Reader

This dossier was built to answer one question: **how did a two-storefront Chicago record company, run by two immigrant brothers who started with records in a car trunk, become the institution that industrialized the Chicago blues?**

The short answer: by owning the whole pipeline. Chess didn't just record blues musicians — it scouted them (Leonard with a wire recorder in the South), recorded them in its own studio (2120, then 320), pressed and shipped the records itself, published the songs through its own BMI company, and sold them through its own network of Black retail and regional indie distributors. That integration is what "electrified and industrialized the blues" means: Chicago didn't invent the electric blues — Muddy Waters's Chess sides were, in Smithsonian's formulation, "basically identical" to his 1942 Lomax recordings "except for electricity" — but Chess turned that electricity into an industry.

The hard edge of that story is the money. The same system that made the records also produced payola co-credits, the Arc Music royalty suits of 1977, and the decades-long legal fight with Led Zeppelin that ended — twice — in settlements, never in a courtroom victory. Willie Dixon's revenge was institutional: the Blues Heaven Foundation, headquartered in the very building where the records were made. That's why this dossier treats the building and the company as separate entities — the music lives in one, the business in the other, and the story needs both.

If you're building with this dossier — an exhibit, a curriculum, a tour — start at 2120 S. Michigan, but tell the whole chain: Cottage Grove storefronts → 2120 → 320 E. 21st → the GRT sale → the catalog's long road to MCA and UMG. And remember: the catalog you hear today on Geffen/UMe reissues is the same one Leonard Chess sold for $6.5 million and some stock in January 1969.

## 25 · CTL Framing

- **Black Cultural Intelligence lens:** Chess Records is a case study in Black cultural production inside a non-Black-owned institution: the artists, the sound, the audience, and the retail network were Black; the ownership, publishing, and royalties were not. The dossier's royalty and credit findings (payola credits, Arc suits, Zeppelin settlements) are the institutional evidence of that asymmetry — documented, graded, and refutation-checked.
- **Myth correction as method:** Three refuted myths (guilt verdict, Dixon ownership, 1947 founding) are kept in the ledger precisely so the record shows what was tested and failed. CTL does not drop refuted claims; it grades them.
