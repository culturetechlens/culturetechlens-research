## 01 · Identity

**Canonical name:** The Afro Pick
**Dossier ID:** CTL-RD-OBJ-000001
**Entity type:** Object (grooming technology / cultural artifact)
**One-sentence identification:** The afro pick is a wide-toothed comb with long
teeth and an easily-gripped handle, designed for styling coily hair, that
became a mass-produced American consumer product in the 1960s–1970s and a
documented symbol of Black pride and identity.

**Disambiguation:** This dossier covers the wide-tooth styling pick (also
"hair pick," "pik," "Afro Tease"), not the heated straightening implement
known as the hot comb or pressing comb — a separate technology with a separate
history (see §27). It also covers the comb *form* as distinct from any single
manufacturer's product (e.g., the Eden "METAL FISTPIK" is one product instance
of the form).

---

## 02 · Executive Profile

The afro pick is a grooming tool with a 5,500-year-deep form and a 60-year-old
commercial life. Long-toothed hair combs are documented in predynastic Egypt
(the Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge, holds a ~5,500-year-old example and built
its 2013 exhibition "Origins of the Afro Comb" around the form). The modern
mass-produced pick emerged in the United States in the 1960s–1970s: San Diego
barber Willie Lee Morrow adapted a Nigerian wooden comb into the "Afro Tease"
and mass-produced it; Samuel H. Bundles Jr. and Henry M. Childrey (Tulloch)
patented an early pick design in 1969 for Summit Laboratories; and Anthony R.
Romani's raised-fist handle — U.S. Design Patent Des. 238,821, filed 1974,
granted 1976, manufactured by Antonio's Manufacturing of Pennsylvania —
married the tool to Black Power iconography. CTL establishes the full Object
Biography chain with strong manufacturer, design, period, museum-holdings, and
people links; advertising and consumer-use links are moderate; period
photographs of the pick in use are the thinnest link. The dossier's central
discipline is anti-speculation on symbolism: museums document the fist's Black
Power meaning and users reported varied meanings (Black pride, unity, "just a
nice shape"), but claims that the Black Panther Party commissioned the design
or that Bundles and Childrey invented the *fist* comb are corrected here —
the first is unevidenced, the second is refuted by the patent record. No
single inventor can be credited; the pick has multiple documented origin
points. Uncertain: the Bundles–Childrey patent number, comprehensive 1970s
advertising data, and rights-cleared period photographs of use.

## 03 · Significance

- [VERIFIED] The afro pick is one of the few everyday objects whose design
  history is fully traceable from an ancient African form to a patented
  20th-century mass product — making it a model artifact for material-culture
  study of the Black diaspora.
- [VERIFIED] Museum interpretation (NMAAHC, Fitzwilliam, MoMA) treats the pick
  — especially the fist-handle variant — as a document of Black Power-era
  identity politics, not merely a grooming tool.
- [SUPPORTED] The pick's commercial history (Black manufacturers, Black press
  advertising, Black-owned distribution) makes it a case study in Black
  economic self-determination in the beauty industry.
- [VERIFIED] Without this dossier's evidence testing, the public record would
  continue to conflate three separate achievements — Morrow's popularization,
  Bundles & Childrey's early patent, Romani's fist-handle design — into
  competing single-inventor myths.

## 07 · Biography/History

*Built exclusively from graded claims; every paragraph traces to the evidence
file (EF record cited). The Object Biography chain order is followed.*

**Form (ancient).** Long-toothed hair combs are among Africa's oldest grooming
technologies. The Fitzwilliam Museum holds a ~5,500-year-old predynastic
Egyptian comb, and conservator Pia Edqvist documented over 250 African hair
combs spanning 3,000 years from ancient Egypt to the early 20th century in
preparation for the museum's 2013 exhibition (EF-002, EF-027).

**Manufacture (modern, 1960s).** In 1960s San Diego, barber Willie Lee Morrow
was given a traditional Nigerian wooden comb by his friend Robert Bell
(c. 1962). By his own account it was the first pick he had ever seen; he
taught himself to make copies in wood, then plastic and metal, and sold them
under the name "Afro Tease" — the product later known generically as the afro
pick (EF-004). At peak he sold a reported 12,000 picks a week (EF-005). In
1969 the Department of Defense hired him to train military barbers in
cutting and styling Black hair, a program the *New York Times* covered on
January 31, 1971 (EF-006).

**Design (patents, 1969–1976).** In 1969, Samuel H. Bundles Jr. — whose wife
was the granddaughter of Madam C.J. Walker — and his business partner Henry M.
Childrey (Tulloch) developed and patented one of the first afro picks for
Summit Laboratories, after seeing a comb in San Francisco in the late 1960s
(EF-007, EF-023). A folding-comb variant followed: patent filed 1970, granted
1971 (EF-014). In 1972, Pennsylvania comb manufacturer Anthony R. Romani
designed the raised-fist handle with a peace-sign center; he filed a U.S.
design patent in 1974 and US Des. 238,821 ("COMB HANDLE") was granted February
10, 1976, assigned to Antonios Manufacturing Inc. of Altoona, PA (EF-009).
Antonio's Manufacturing of Cresson, PA produced the pick circa 1972–1976 and
became, in one contemporary account, "king of the pick market" (EF-009).

**Period & advertising (1970s).** The pick's commercial peak coincided with
the Afro hairstyle and the "Black is Beautiful" movement. A 1972 Supreme
Beauty Products two-page magazine ad for "Duke & Raveen" Black Combs offered
11 comb options with an order form (EF-015). Chicago's Johnson Products
Company — founded 1954 by George E. and Joan Johnson — marketed Afro Sheen
for natural afros through Ebony and Jet advertising and a pioneering Soul
Train sponsorship, keying its strategy to Black pride; the company became the
first Black-owned firm listed on the American Stock Exchange in 1971
(EF-016, EF-017).

**Symbolism & consumer use.** The NMAAHC classifies its fist pick under "Black
power" and "Black is Beautiful"; the Fitzwilliam displayed fist combs as
"referencing the Black Power Movement." When curator Sally-Ann Ashton asked
owners what the comb meant, answers ranged across "Black Power," "Black
pride," "Nelson Mandela's release," "it's just a nice shape," "unity," and —
for younger users — retro style (EF-012). In late-1970s/early-1980s Britain,
participants in the Origins of the Afro Comb project recalled police
confiscating their fist combs as potential weapons (EF-013). Clenched-fist
combs were also manufactured in Nigeria from at least the early 1980s
(EF-024), while most fist combs sold in the UK today are made in China —
including the metal-toothed Eden "METAL FISTPIK" distributed from Skokie,
Illinois (EF-020, EF-025).

**Museum afterlife.** The Smithsonian NMAAHC holds the Eden fist pick
(2014.125.2) and a 1950s Ghanaian wooden pick (2014.182.99, on view in "A
Changing America: 1968 and Beyond") (EF-020, EF-021). MoMA holds a 1972–1976
Antonio's "Black Power Afro pick hair comb" (215.2019) in its Architecture and
Design department (EF-022). The Fitzwilliam's 2013 exhibition — co-created
with Black prisoners through Ashton's Black Cultural Heritage in Prisons
project — remains the fullest museum treatment (EF-027).

## 27 · Myth vs. Evidence

**MYTH:** "Willie Morrow invented the afro pick."
**VERDICT:** Exaggerated (DISPUTED — sources split).
**EVIDENCE:** NYT: "popularized"; Ebony/LA Times: "inventor." Morrow's own
account says he was *given* the model comb. Bundles & Childrey patented a
pick independently.
**CORRECTED WORDING:** "Willie Lee Morrow adapted a Nigerian wooden comb into
the 'Afro Tease' and became the afro pick's great popularizer and
mass-producer in 1960s America."

**MYTH:** "Bundles and Childrey invented the black fist afro comb."
**VERDICT:** False (REFUTED).
**EVIDENCE:** US Des. 238,821 names Anthony R. Romani; History Workshop
confirms via Mrs. Romani; Makematic: "They did invent the comb, but not the
raised fist one."
**CORRECTED WORDING:** "Bundles and Childrey patented an early afro pick
(1969); Anthony R. Romani designed the raised-fist handle (1972)."

**MYTH:** "The Black Panthers created the fist comb."
**VERDICT:** Unverifiable (no evidence; UNRESOLVED).
**EVIDENCE:** No document, interview, or manufacturing record links the BPP
to the design. Romani Jr.'s account gives a personal rationale ("you have to
fight for peace, I reckon").
**CORRECTED WORDING:** "The fist handle references Black Power iconography —
a meaning museums document — but no evidence ties its design to the Black
Panther Party."

**MYTH:** "Madam C.J. Walker invented the hot comb."
**VERDICT:** False (REFUTED).
**EVIDENCE:** NMAAHC "Sizzle" ("common misconception"); A'Lelia Bundles
("she didn't invent the hot comb"); pre-Walker history (Grateau, 1886
catalogs).
**CORRECTED WORDING:** "Walker popularized and improved the hot comb and
built the Walker System around it; she did not invent it."

**MYTH:** "The afro pick is 6,000 years old."
**VERDICT:** Exaggerated without qualification.
**EVIDENCE:** The comb *form* is ancient (5,500-year-old predynastic comb at
the Fitzwilliam); the modern plastic pick is a 1960s–70s product.
**CORRECTED WORDING:** "Long-toothed combs have ~5,500-year-documented African
roots; the mass-produced afro pick dates to the 1960s–70s."

**MYTH (adjacent):** "Walter Sammons invented the hot/pressing comb."
**VERDICT:** Unverifiable as stated; the patent is real.
**EVIDENCE:** Sammons's 1920 patent is documented (NMAAHC); but heated combs
predate him (Grateau, France, late 1800s).
**CORRECTED WORDING:** "Walter Sammons patented an improved straightening
comb in 1920; he did not invent the heated comb."

## Appendix A — Relationship-Layer Spec

The relationship layer is this dossier's critical feature. Every verified
connection becomes reusable CTL knowledge: machine-readable,
evidence-linked, and honest about its grade.

**Edge format:** `SUBJECT → RELATIONSHIP → OBJECT`

- **SUBJECT / OBJECT:** entity IDs from `entities.json` (dossier-local,
  namespaced `RD-OBJ-000001-###`); the audit's `canon-<slug>` IDs were
  checked in `duplicates.json` — none apply here.
- **RELATIONSHIP:** controlled verb phrase. Precise verbs only for graph
  ingestion (`manufactured-by`, `designed-by`, `patented-under`,
  `distributed-by`, `manufactured-in`, `archived-at`, `exhibited-in`,
  `developed-for`, `mass-produced-by`, `derived-from`, `distinct-from`,
  `used-by`, `confiscated-from`, `interpreted-as`, `advertised-in`,
  `sponsored`, `child-of`, `spouse-of`, `descendant-of`, `curated`,
  `gifted`, `popularized`, `associated-with` [human-readable only,
  unqualified]).
- **Provenance (required on every edge):** source, source type, source
  date, and the specific evidence (quote or precise description).
- **Grade (required):** VERIFIED / SUPPORTED / PROVISIONAL / DISPUTED /
  UNRESOLVED. Only VERIFIED edges enter the verified-only graph. DISPUTED
  edges enter with both sides recorded.
- **Temporal scope:** `date_range` on every edge where dates are known;
  undated edges say so.

**Rules:** Never manufacture a relationship ("likely worked with" →
PROVISIONAL with exact wording, or it doesn't exist). Organizations and
their buildings are separate entities; people and their papers/collections
are separate entities. Before adding an edge, check the audit
(`duplicates.json`, `contradictions.json`) — done for this dossier, no
matches found.

## Appendix B — Evidence-File Pattern (Type 04)
