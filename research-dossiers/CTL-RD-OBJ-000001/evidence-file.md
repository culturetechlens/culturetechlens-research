# CTL-EF-OBJ-000001 — Evidence File: The Afro Pick

**Type 04 Evidence File** · companion to dossier CTL-RD-OBJ-000001
**Status:** Research · **Version:** 1.0 (2026-09-28)
**Research Question:** What can CTL responsibly establish about the afro pick — its
origins, design history, manufacture, commercial life, symbolism, and cultural
meaning — and which widely repeated claims about it survive evidence testing?

This file follows the Type 04 10-part architecture: Research Question → Claim →
Evidence For ↔ Evidence Against → Primary + Secondary Sources → Source Evaluation
→ Corroboration → Conflicts/Contradictions → Confidence Classification →
CTL Finding → What would change it. It reconciles 1:1 with the dossier's §15
claim ledger (CTL-RD-OBJ-000001): every ledger claim has a record here and vice
versa. Ledger mapping rule: REFUTED → ledger DISPUTED ("refuted — see
CTL-EF-OBJ-000001"); UNVERIFIABLE → ledger UNRESOLVED (pointer to this file).

**Method note (Object Biography pilot for CTL-DR-LIFE-001):** the chain is
manufacturer → design → period → advertising → symbolism → consumer use →
photographs → museum holdings → related people → related movements. Each link's
evidence strength is assessed in the relevant records; a chain-strength summary

## EF-001 · What the afro pick is

**Claim:** The afro pick is a hair comb with long, widely-spaced teeth and an
easily-gripped handle, suited to styling coily/afro-textured hair.

**Evidence For:**
- NMAAHC, "Strands of Inspiration: Exploring Black Identities through Hair":
  "The specialized structure of the Afro pick features wide gaps between the
  teeth of the comb and an easily-grippable handle, allowing users to
  efficiently maneuver the comb through coily hair while maintaining the
  integrity of the curl pattern."

**Evidence Against:** None found after museum-catalog and press searches.

**Primary sources:** NMAAHC object records (2014.125.2, 2014.182.99) describing
tooth spacing, handle form, materials. **Secondary:** NMAAHC interpretive essay;
History Workshop ("a wider-toothed comb is a healthier way of grooming").

**Source evaluation:** NMAAHC is the national museum of record for African
American material culture; the essay is curatorial interpretation, secondary but
institutionally strong. Object records are primary catalog descriptions.

**Corroboration:** Curatorial essay + object records + independent British
scholarship converge on the same functional description.

**Conflicts:** None.

**Classification:** VERIFIED.

**CTL Finding:** The functional description is established.

**What would change it:** A contradictory primary description of the object's
form from a manufacturer or patent record — none found.

## EF-002 · Deep African roots

**Claim:** Long-toothed hair combs have deep African roots; a ~5,500-year-old
predynastic Egyptian comb is held by the Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge.

**Evidence For:**
- Fitzwilliam Museum annual report 2012–13: exhibition "Origins of the Afro
  Comb: 6,000 years of culture, politics and identity" (2 July–3 November
  2013) traced the comb from predynastic Egypt to the present.
- UCL Culture Blog (conservator Pia Edqvist, 2013): the oldest comb on display
  was "an Ancient Egyptian comb 5,500 years old," shown side by side with the
  black fist comb; her MSc work documented "over 250 African hair combs
  originating from all over Africa spanning through 3000 years of history from
  Ancient Egypt to early 20th century."
- Fitzwilliam case study: prisoners "had noted the similarities between a
  5,500-year-old Egyptian comb from the Museum's collection and modern 'picks'
  or hair combs."

**Evidence Against:** None found; no source disputes the age or provenance of
the predynastic combs.

**Primary sources:** Fitzwilliam Museum institutional records (annual report,
exhibition case study). **Secondary:** UCL conservator's blog (first-hand
professional account).

**Source evaluation:** The museum's own annual report is a primary
institutional record. Edqvist's account is first-hand specialist testimony.

**Corroboration:** Three independent accounts (museum report, case study,
conservator) agree on the ~5,500-year figure and predynastic Egyptian origin.

**Conflicts:** None. (Popular summaries sometimes round to "6,000 years," the
exhibition's title figure; the cataloged object is described as 5,500 years
old — a labeling difference, not a factual conflict.)

**Classification:** VERIFIED.

**CTL Finding:** The comb *form* has documented African roots to predynastic
Egypt. This does not date the modern plastic pick (see EF-003).

**What would change it:** Re-dating of the Fitzwilliam predynastic comb by the
museum itself.

## EF-003 · The modern pick's emergence

**Claim:** The modern mass-produced afro pick emerged in the United States in
the 1960s–1970s alongside the Afro hairstyle and the Black Power movement.

**Evidence For:**
- NDInsider (USA Today network, 2017): Morrow's 1960s picks; Bundles/Childrey
  1969 pick; Romani 1972 fist design; Antonio's Manufacturing "king of the
  pick market" in the 1970s.
- History Workshop (2014): fist comb "coming into production at some point in
  the 1970s"; earliest patented comb of this form 1969.
- Fitzwilliam exhibition traced the *modern* designs (black fist combs) to the
  Black Power movement alongside ancient combs.

**Evidence Against:** None found.

**Primary sources:** Patent records (US Des. 238,821, granted 1976; design
1972). **Secondary:** NDInsider, History Workshop, Fitzwilliam materials.

**Source evaluation:** Convergent secondary reporting with primary patent
documents anchoring the 1970s designs.

**Corroboration:** Independent sources (US press, UK scholarship, museum)
agree on the 1960s–70s emergence window.

**Conflicts:** None.

**Classification:** VERIFIED.

**CTL Finding:** Established: the modern commercial pick is a 1960s–70s
American product built on an ancient comb form.

**What would change it:** Earlier-dated mass-manufactured pick with primary
documentation — none found.

## EF-004 · Willie Morrow and the "Afro Tease"

**Claim:** Willie Lee Morrow made and sold afro picks (wood, then plastic and
metal versions) in 1960s San Diego, calling his product the "Afro Tease,"
after being given a traditional Nigerian wooden comb by his friend Robert Bell
(c. 1962); he taught himself to mass-produce it.

**Evidence For:**
- NYT obituary (Clay Risen, July 6, 2022), via contemporary reporting: Bell
  returned from studying in Nigeria in 1962 with a wooden comb gift; Morrow was
  "enthralled."
- NPR/WCBE (July 2022): Cheryl Morrow — "he wanted to make it available in the
  Western world"; "taught himself how to make and then mass produce the comb";
  Morrow: "It was a slow turn because when you're an innovator, you know,
  you're first."
- NDInsider: "I took a look at this comb... That was the first time I saw a
  pick," Morrow said; he "started making his own picks out of wood, eventually
  adding plastic and metal versions."
- BLK ALERTS / MadameNoire: Morrow "created a tool that he called the Afro
  Tease. It later became known as the Afro pick."

**Evidence Against:** None found contradicting the account; the "inventor"
label is contested (see EF-029).

**Primary sources:** Morrow's own quoted statements (via NDInsider, NPR);
family testimony (Cheryl Morrow). **Secondary:** NYT, NPR, NDInsider obituaries.

**Source evaluation:** Obituaries are secondary but the NYT/NPR pieces quote
Morrow and family directly. BLK ALERTS/MadameNoire are weaker aggregators —
the "Afro Tease" brand name rests on them.

**Corroboration:** The core story (Nigerian comb gift → Morrow's wooden picks
→ mass production) is told independently by NYT, NPR (family), and NDInsider
(Morrow's own words). The brand name "Afro Tease" is thinly sourced.

**Conflicts:** NYT's headline frames Morrow as the pick's *popularizer*,
while Ebony's headline calls him its *inventor* (see EF-029).

**Classification:** SUPPORTED.

**CTL Finding:** Morrow made and mass-produced picks in 1960s San Diego after
the Nigerian-comb encounter; "Afro Tease" as his product name is plausible but
thinly sourced.

**What would change it:** A period advertisement or business record showing the
"Afro Tease" name would strengthen; a contradictory contemporary account would
weaken.

**Corroboration:** Two independent lines (contemporary press citation + family
account) converge.

**Conflicts:** None.

**Classification:** SUPPORTED.

**CTL Finding:** The DoD engagement is solid; reading the full 1971 NYT
article would upgrade.

**What would change it:** Retrieval of the full NYT article (upgrade);

## EF-007 · The Bundles–Childrey 1969 patent

**Claim:** Samuel H. Bundles Jr. and Henry M. Childrey (Tulloch) held one of
the first U.S. patents for an afro pick; they developed it in 1969 for Summit
Laboratories after seeing a comb in San Francisco in the late 1960s.

**Evidence For:**
- History Workshop (Ashton, 2014): "The earliest comb of this form to emerge
  was patented in 1969 by two African Americans, Samuel H. Bundles Jr., and
  Henry M. Childrey (Tulloch)."
- NDInsider: "S. Henry Bundles, Jr... held one of the first patents for an
  Afro pick, along with his business partner Henry Childrey"; "A'Lelia Bundles
  said the pick her father and Childrey developed in 1969 for Summit
  Laboratories came after they saw a comb in San Francisco in the late '60s."
- Makematic case study: "two African American inventors, Samuel H. Bundles
  Jr., and Henry M. Childrey (Tulloch)... They did invent the comb, but not
  the raised fist one."

**Evidence Against:** None found; no source denies a 1969 Bundles/Childrey
patent.

**Primary sources:** The patent document itself was not located in this
research (see EF-008). **Secondary:** three independent secondary accounts,
including direct family testimony (A'Lelia Bundles, journalist and daughter).

**Source evaluation:** A'Lelia Bundles is a strong proximate source (daughter
of the inventor). The other two accounts are independent and agree on the
1969 date.

**Corroboration:** Family testimony + two independent secondary reports
converge on existence and date; all agree it was an early pick patent, not
the fist design.

**Conflicts:** None on existence; Face2Face Africa conflates it with the fist
design (see EF-010).

**Classification:** SUPPORTED (not VERIFIED: the patent document itself was
not examined).

**CTL Finding:** A 1969 Bundles–Childrey afro pick patent for Summit
Laboratories is well attested; the patent number is a documented gap.

**What would change it:** Locating the patent document (upgrade to VERIFIED);
a USPTO negative result would require re-examination.

## EF-009 · The Romani fist-handle patent

**Claim:** The raised-fist afro comb handle was designed by Anthony R. Romani
in 1972; U.S. Design Patent Des. 238,821 ("COMB HANDLE") was filed in 1974 and
granted February 10, 1976, assigned to Antonios Manufacturing Inc., P.O. Box
1803, Altoona, PA; Antonio's Manufacturing of Cresson, PA manufactured the
pick circa 1972–1976.

**Evidence For:**
- Makematic case study, quoting the patent document verbatim: "United States
  Patent Des. 238,821 Patented Feb. 10, 1976 COMB HANDLE Anthony R. Romani,
  Hollidaysburg, Pa. Antonios Manufacturing Inc., P.0. Box 1803, Altoona, Pa."
- History Workshop (Ashton): "The original black fist comb... was designed by
  Anthony R. Romani in 1972; this much I had established by speaking to Mrs
  Romani, who still works at the company in Pennsylvania. The comb, known as a
  'styling pik' was submitted to the US Patent Office in 1974 and the patent
  was granted in 1976."
- MoMA collection record 215.2019: "Black Power Afro pick hair comb,
  1972–1976, Injection-molded plastic... Manufacturer Antonio's
  Manufacturing," crediting "Anthony Romani, Samuel H. Bundles Jr., Henry M.
  Childrey."
- NDInsider: "If you have a pick with a fist and peace sign on the handle,
  it's probably an Antonio's model based on an original design first patented
  in the early '70s"; Anthony Romani Jr. on his father, company founder.

**Evidence Against:** None found.

**Primary sources:** The patent document (quoted verbatim in Makematic);
MoMA collection record. **Secondary:** History Workshop (with direct family
testimony), NDInsider (with son's testimony).

**Source evaluation:** The patent is the decisive primary source. Family
testimony (Mrs. Romani, Romani Jr.) corroborates design authorship and date.

**Corroboration:** Patent document + two independent family accounts + museum
collection record converge.

**Conflicts:** None on Romani's authorship of the *fist handle*. (MoMA's
triple credit — Romani, Bundles, Childrey — reflects design lineage, not sole
authorship; see EF-010.)

**Classification:** VERIFIED.

**CTL Finding:** The fist-handle design is documented to Romani/Antonio's,
1972 design → 1974 filing → 1976 grant.

**What would change it:** Only a contradictory patent assignment record —
none exists.

## EF-010 · "Bundles and Childrey invented the black fist comb"

**Claim:** Samuel H. Bundles Jr. and Henry M. Childrey invented the black fist
afro comb.

**Evidence For:** Face2Face Africa: "Samuel H. Bundles, Jr. and Henry M.
Childrey (Tulloch) patented the black fist afro comb after they introduced it
in 1969." (This is the widely repeated version.)

**Evidence Against:**
- The fist-handle patent (US Des. 238,821) names Anthony R. Romani as designer
  and Antonios Manufacturing as assignee — not Bundles or Childrey.
- History Workshop: Romani designed the fist comb in 1972 (three years after
  the Bundles–Childrey patent), confirmed with Mrs. Romani.
- Makematic: "They did invent the comb, but not the raised fist one."

**Primary sources:** Patent Des. 238,821. **Secondary:** History Workshop,
Makematic (for); Face2Face Africa (against — the claim's source).

**Source evaluation:** The patent document is decisive on design authorship.
Face2Face Africa conflates two real facts (the 1969 Bundles–Childrey patent;
the fist comb's existence) into one false one.

**Corroboration:** The refutation is corroborated independently by patent
record, family testimony, and museum scholarship.

**Conflicts:** Direct: Face2Face Africa vs. patent record. The patent wins.

**Classification:** REFUTED (maps to ledger DISPUTED — "refuted — see
CTL-EF-OBJ-000001").

**CTL Finding:** Bundles and Childrey patented an early afro pick; they did
not design the fist handle. The conflation is the dossier's clearest myth
correction.

**What would change it:** A Bundles/Childrey patent document depicting a fist
handle — the located patent record points the other way.

## EF-011 · "The Black Panthers created the fist comb"

**Evidence For:** Face2Face Africa: "The Black Power salute by the Black

- Anthony Romani Jr.: his father combined fist and peace sign; asked why, he
  said, "Well, you have to fight for peace, I reckon" — a personal/marketing
  rationale, not a party commission.
- History Workshop: production "at some point in the 1970s"; no BPP
  commission documented.

**Primary sources:** None support the claim. **Secondary:** one assertion
(Face2Face Africa) without sourcing.

**Source evaluation:** Face2Face Africa offers no citation, document, or
interview for the Panther link — assertion only.

**Corroboration:** No corroboration found across patent records, family
testimony, and museum scholarship.

**Conflicts:** The documented design history contradicts a party-commission
story.

**Classification:** UNVERIFIABLE (maps to ledger UNRESOLVED — pointer to this
file). Note: this is *not* a claim that the fist lacks Black Power meaning
(see EF-012); it is a claim about *who designed it* that no evidence
substantiates.

**CTL Finding:** Undocumented. The symbolism link (EF-012) is real; the
commission story is not evidenced.

**What would change it:** A BPP document, a manufacturer record, or a
contemporary account linking the party to the design.

## EF-012 · The fist's Black Power meaning

**Claim:** The fist handle references Black Power iconography; museums
interpret it as such, while documented users' own meanings varied (Black
Power, Black pride, Mandela's release, "just a nice shape," unity, retro).

**Evidence For:**
- NMAAHC object 2014.125.2 classification: "Movement, Black is Beautiful...
  Topic, Black power, Hair, Identity."
- Fitzwilliam materials: fist combs displayed as "referencing the Black Power
  Movement."
- NMAAHC "Strands of Inspiration": "the designer of the fist comb instead
  links hair styling with Black power imagery. Symbols of Black power — such
  as the clenched fist at the top of the sleek, shiny, black comb — link Black
  hair with political meanings."
- History Workshop: fist comb "has become more than simply representative of
  an era and a political affiliation. It also symbolises Black pride and
  identity"; Ashton's 2013 exhibition interviews: "'Black Power'; 'Black
  pride'; 'Nelson Mandela's release'; 'it's just a nice shape'; and 'unity'";
  younger users: retro/'old skool', associations "nonetheless linked to
  'Black' culture and identity."

**Evidence Against:** None found; no source argues the fist is apolitical.

**Primary sources:** Museum catalog classifications (NMAAHC). **Secondary:**
Curatorial essays (NMAAHC, Ashton), exhibition interview summaries.

**Source evaluation:** Institutional interpretation is strong; Ashton's user
interviews are documented oral-history summaries (secondary, but first-hand
collection).

**Corroboration:** US and UK museum interpretation converge; user testimony
adds the nuance that meanings were plural.

**Conflicts:** None — plural meanings are recorded, not adjudicated.

**Classification:** VERIFIED.

**CTL Finding:** The Black Power symbolism is institutionally documented; the
anti-speculation discipline here is to report the *range* of documented
meanings rather than a single one.

**What would change it:** Contradictory institutional interpretation — none
exists.

## EF-018 · Pick vs. hot comb: separate technologies

**Claim:** The afro pick is a distinct technology from the hot comb/pressing
comb (heated straightening tool vs. wide-tooth styling tool) with separate
histories; the hot comb traces to Marcel Grateau's France (late 1800s), with
U.S. patents including Walter Sammons (1920) and Clara Grant (1925).

**Evidence For:**
- NMAAHC "Sizzle": "A Frenchman named Marcel Grateau... is often accredited
  with its invention in the late 1800s"; "Other individuals with patents for
  hot combs include, but are not limited to, Walter Sammons in 1920 and a St.
  Louis based woman named Clara Grant in 1925"; the hot comb is a heated
  straightening implement — functionally distinct from the pick.
- Patent record: Walter Sammons's 1920 patent for an improved straightening
  comb is consistently reported (US patent, Dec 21, 1920).

**Evidence Against:** None found.

**Primary sources:** Patent records (Sammons). **Secondary:** NMAAHC (strong).

**Source evaluation:** NMAAHC is authoritative on this distinction; the
Sammons patent is a primary document.

**Corroboration:** Multiple sources converge on Sammons 1920 and the
Grateau origin account.

**Conflicts:** Folk accounts sometimes conflate the tools; the evidence
separates them.

**Classification:** VERIFIED.

**CTL Finding:** Established: separate technologies, separate histories.

**What would change it:** Nothing short of contradictory patent evidence.

## EF-019 · "Madam C.J. Walker invented the hot comb"

**Claim:** Madam C.J. Walker invented the hot comb.

**Secondary:** the obituaries themselves, split.

cites a patent or document proving sole invention, because none exists.

**Corroboration:** N/A — the sources disagree.

**Conflicts:** Direct: "inventor" (Ebony/LA Times) vs. "popularized"
(NYT). Never adjudicated silently — recorded here.

**Classification:** DISPUTED.

**CTL Finding:** Morrow is best documented as the pick's great popularizer
and mass-producer; sole-inventor credit overstates the evidence. Corrected
wording in §27.

**What would change it:** A Morrow patent or pre-1962 manufacturing record
(upgrades "inventor"); already unlikely given his own account.
