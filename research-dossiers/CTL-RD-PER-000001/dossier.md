## 01 · Identity

**Canonical name:** Ida Bell Wells-Barnett (née Ida Bell Wells)
**Dossier ID:** CTL-RD-PER-000001
**Entity type:** Person
**One-sentence identification:** Ida B. Wells (1862–1931) was an African American
investigative journalist, newspaper owner and editor, anti-lynching investigator,
suffragist, and community organizer whose Memphis and Chicago careers built the
documentary foundation of the American anti-lynching movement.

**Disambiguation:** This dossier covers the journalist/activist Ida Bell Wells,
later Ida B. Wells-Barnett. Not to be confused with the Ida B. Wells Homes (the
Chicago Housing Authority public-housing project named in her honor;
`canon-ida-b-wells-homes`), the Ida B. Wells-Barnett House (her Chicago residence,
a National Historic Landmark), or the Ida B. Wells Women's Club (an organization
she founded). Wells is also distinct from her daughter Alfreda Barnett Duster,
who edited and published Wells's autobiography.

## 02 · Executive Profile

Ida B. Wells was born enslaved in Holly Springs, Mississippi, on July 16, 1862,
and died in Chicago on March 25, 1931. Between those dates she built three
interlocking careers that this dossier documents from the evidence: first, as a
Memphis teacher turned newspaper owner — editor and part-owner of the *Memphis
Free Speech and Headlight* — who was driven into exile in May 1892 after a white
mob destroyed her press for exposing the lie at the heart of lynching; second,
as a national and international anti-lynching investigator whose pamphlets
*Southern Horrors* (1892) and *The Red Record* (1895) pioneered data-driven
investigative journalism, drawing on white newspapers' own accounts to demolish
the claim that lynching punished assaults on white women; and third, as a Chicago
organizer — founder of the Negro Fellowship League (1910) and the Alpha Suffrage
Club (1913), a participant in the founding of the NAACP (1909 call), the National
Association of Colored Women (1896), and the Afro-American Council (1898), and a
contributor to Black newspapers including the *Chicago Defender*.

CTL establishes the Memphis arc and the Chicago arc as documented fact, the
1892 lynchings of Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and William Stewart as the
catalyst of her anti-lynching work, and her publisher's role — co-owner of the
*Free Speech* — as central, not incidental, to her power. What remains uncertain:
fine-grained dates (the exact year she took ownership of the *Free Speech*),
the full contents of lost *Free Speech* issues, the precise nature of her
early break with the NAACP's leadership, and several single-source details
flagged PROVISIONAL throughout. Nothing in this dossier is invented to fill
those gaps.

## 03 · Significance

- **CLAIM:** Wells created the first sustained, evidence-based public record of
  lynching in the United States, shifting anti-lynching argument from moral
  outrage to documented fact. **Grade: SUPPORTED** (see CLAIM-018, CLAIM-019).
- **CLAIM:** Her 1892 exile from Memphis is one of the clearest documented cases
  of the violent suppression of the Black press in the post-Reconstruction South.
  **Grade: VERIFIED** (see CLAIM-014).
- **CLAIM:** As a Black woman newspaper owner in the 1880s–1890s South, she
  occupied a publisher's role almost entirely absent from the surviving
  institutional record of Black journalism — the gap MEDIA-001's red team
  flagged. **Grade: SUPPORTED** (see CLAIM-009; gaps recorded in §28).
- **CLAIM:** Her Chicago organizing (Negro Fellowship League, Alpha Suffrage
  Club) built independent Black institutional infrastructure for Great Migration
  newcomers and Black women voters a decade before the NAACP's Chicago branch
  matured. **Grade: SUPPORTED** (see CLAIM-030, CLAIM-032).
- **CLAIM:** The body of investigative method she developed — clipping hostile
  white newspapers, tabulating victims, names, dates, and alleged causes —
  constitutes a founding practice of American data journalism.
  **Grade: SUPPORTED** (see CLAIM-018).

What would be missing without her: the statistical refutation of the
rape-myth justification of lynching at the moment it was most politically
useful; the documentary link between the Black press tradition and the modern
civil-rights movement; and the Chicago record of Black women's independent
political organizing in the 1910s.

## 07 · Biography/History

Ida Bell Wells was born July 16, 1862, on the Boling farm near Holly Springs,
Mississippi, enslaved — as were both her parents — months before the
Emancipation Proclamation took effect (CLAIM-001, CLAIM-002). Her father James
Madison Wells, a carpenter, became a trustee of Shaw University (later Rust
College) after emancipation and a committed Republican "race man"; her mother
Elizabeth "Lizzie" Warrenton Wells was known as a cook (CLAIM-003). In 1878
yellow fever killed both parents and her youngest sibling; at sixteen Wells kept
her surviving siblings together by passing the teacher's examination and teaching
(CLAIM-004). She attended Shaw University/Rust College and in the early 1880s
moved to Memphis, teaching in rural and then Memphis schools (CLAIM-005, CLAIM-006).

In May 1884 the Chesapeake, Ohio & Southwestern Railroad's conductor ordered her
out of the first-class ladies' car; she refused, was forcibly removed, sued, won
$500 in the Memphis circuit court in December 1884, and lost on appeal when the
Tennessee Supreme Court reversed the verdict in April 1887 (CLAIM-007). Already
writing as "Iola" for Black newspapers (CLAIM-008), she became editor and
part-owner of the *Memphis Free Speech and Headlight* by 1889 (CLAIM-009). When
she criticized the unequal funding of Black schools she was fired from her
teaching post in 1891 and turned to journalism full time (CLAIM-011).

On March 9, 1892, a white mob lynched her friend Thomas Moss and his partners
Calvin McDowell and William "Will" Stewart, successful Black grocers whose
People's Grocery competed with a white-owned store at the Curve in south Memphis
(CLAIM-012). The murders converted Wells from a general race journalist into an
anti-lynching investigator (CLAIM-013). Her editorials urged Black Memphians to
boycott streetcars and white businesses and to leave the city (CLAIM-015). In May
1892, while she was in the East, a committee of white citizens ran the paper's
business manager J. L. Fleming out of town and destroyed the *Free Speech*'s type
and furnishings in retaliation for her editorial demolishing the myth that
lynching punished assaults on white women; a death threat awaited any attempt to
revive the paper, and she never returned to Memphis (CLAIM-014).

In New York she joined T. Thomas Fortune's *New York Age* (CLAIM-016) and
published *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* (1892) with an
introduction by Frederick Douglass (CLAIM-017). Working from white newspapers'
own accounts — including the *Chicago Tribune*'s lynching tallies — she tabulated
victims, dates, places, and alleged causes, demonstrating that most victims were
not accused of any crime against white women and that lynching functioned as
economic and political terror (CLAIM-018). *The Red Record* (1895), subtitled
*Tabulated Statistics and Alleged Causes of Lynching in the United States*,
became the first statistical study of lynching (CLAIM-019). She carried the
campaign to Britain on speaking tours in 1893 and 1894 (CLAIM-020), and in 1893
initiated, edited, and published *The Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in
the World's Columbian Exposition* with Douglass, Irvine Garland Penn, and
Ferdinand L. Barnett (CLAIM-021), while pressuring Frances Willard and the WCTU
toward anti-lynching resolutions (CLAIM-022).

She married the Chicago attorney and *Chicago Conservator* founder Ferdinand
Lee Barnett on June 27, 1895, bought an interest in his paper, and kept writing
(CLAIM-023, CLAIM-024). They had four children; daughter Alfreda Barnett Duster
would edit her autobiography (CLAIM-025). She participated in founding the
National Association of Colored Women (1896) and the Afro-American Council
(1898), founded the Ida B. Wells Women's Club in Chicago (1893), signed the 1909
call that created the NAACP — and then distanced herself from it over strategy
and leadership (CLAIM-026, CLAIM-027, CLAIM-028). In 1898 she carried the
lynching of Black postmaster Frazier B. Baker to President William McKinley and
pressed for federal anti-lynching law (CLAIM-029).

In Chicago she built institutions for the migration era: the Negro Fellowship
League (1910), among the city's first Black settlement houses, serving southern
migrants with jobs, housing, and reading rooms and publishing the *Fellowship
Herald* (CLAIM-030); service as an adult probation officer (1913–1916)
(CLAIM-031); and the Alpha Suffrage Club (January 1913), co-founded with Belle
Squire as Chicago's first Black women's suffrage organization (CLAIM-032). On
March 3, 1913, told to march at the back of the Washington woman suffrage
procession, she refused and joined the Illinois delegation after the march began
(CLAIM-033) — months before Illinois enacted partial presidential and municipal
woman suffrage (CLAIM-034). In 1917 she reported the East St. Louis race riots
for the *Chicago Defender* (CLAIM-035); in 1921 she made her first return trip
to the South in nearly three decades to investigate the 1919 Elaine, Arkansas
massacre, publishing her report in 1922 (CLAIM-036). In the 1920s she backed the
Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, founded the Third Ward Women's Political
Club (1927), lost a 1928 Republican convention-delegate race to Oscar De Priest,
and ran as an Independent for the Illinois State Senate in 1930 (CLAIM-037,
CLAIM-038). She died in Chicago on March 25, 1931, of kidney disease, aged 68
(CLAIM-039). Her autobiography *Crusade for Justice*, written 1928–1931, was
published posthumously in 1970 (CLAIM-040). In 2020 she received a Pulitzer Prize
special citation for her lynching reporting (CLAIM-041); Chicago renamed Congress
Parkway for her in 2019 and unveiled *The Light of Truth* monument in Bronzeville
in 2021 (CLAIM-044), and the Ida B. Wells Homes had carried her name since 1941
(CLAIM-042).

## 26 · Controversies/Conflicts

- **The 1892 Memphis exile.** All sides from the evidence: Wells's editorials
  (urging boycott/exodus; demolishing the rape-myth) → white citizens'
  committee destroys the *Free Speech* (their stated trigger: the *Commercial
  Appeal*'s reading of her editorial) → Wells never returns. Graded claims
  only; no CTL adjudication beyond the record. (CLAIM-014, CLAIM-015)
- **Break with the NAACP's orbit.** Wells signed the 1909 call and is widely
  counted a founder; she distanced herself over strategy and the organization's
  white/elite leadership (Tennessee Encyclopedia; UMKC). Du Bois's role in the
  estrangement is attested as "differences with Du Bois over strategy and
  racial politics" — not elaborated beyond sources. (CLAIM-026)
- **Conflicts with Black male leadership.** Documented criticism of Booker T.
  Washington; praise of Marcus Garvey that drew U.S. Secret Service "radical"
  branding (Tennessee Encyclopedia). Presented as documented positions, not
  character judgments.
- **Conflict with Frances Willard/WCTU.** Wells's pressure campaign vs.
  Willard's initial resistance; WCTU anti-lynching resolutions through 1900;
  conflict subsided by 1896 (scalar.usc.edu). (CLAIM-022)
- **1924 NACW presidency loss to Mary McLeod Bethune.** Single-source;
  PROVISIONAL; no further detail asserted.
- **Historiographical dispute — "founder" status.** Conventional in most
  secondary accounts (Rutgers, UMKC, USC, Facing History); the formal
  conference-vs-call distinction is noted in §27. No silent winner.

## 27 · Myth vs. Evidence

- **MYTH:** "Wells was born into slavery but the Emancipation Proclamation
  freed her at birth." → **VERDICT: Exaggerated.** → EVIDENCE: Born July 16,
  1862, enslaved; the Proclamation took effect January 1, 1863. → CORRECTED:
  "Born enslaved in July 1862, months before emancipation took effect."
- **MYTH:** "A white mob burned down her newspaper office." → **VERDICT:
  Substantially true (wording corrected).** → EVIDENCE: The contemporary
  AP dispatch and her autobiography say the committee "destroyed the type and
  furnishings"; some secondaries say "burned." → CORRECTED: "A white citizens'
  committee destroyed the *Free Speech*'s type and furnishings in May 1892."
- **MYTH:** "She was a founder of the NAACP." → **VERDICT: Substantially true
  (with nuance).** → EVIDENCE: She signed the 1909 call; most institutional
  sources count her among founders; she distanced herself early over strategy
  and leadership. → CORRECTED: "She signed the 1909 call that created the
  NAACP and is widely counted among its founders; she soon distanced herself
  from the organization."
- **MYTH:** "She never set foot in the South again after 1892." → **VERDICT:
  Substantially true (scoped).** → EVIDENCE: She never returned to Memphis to
  live or work; her first investigative return to the South was the 1921
  Elaine trip. → CORRECTED: "She never returned to Memphis; her first return
  trip to the South was in 1921."
- **MYTH:** "*The Red Record* was the first statistical study of lynching."
  → **VERDICT: Substantially true (conventional).** → EVIDENCE: Described as
  such across convergent secondaries; the "first" rests on the tabulated,
  source-cited method of the 1895 pamphlet. → CORRECTED: Keep with the
  conventional qualifier; do not upgrade to an absolute "first ever" without
  a historiographical survey.
- **MYTH:** "Her husband died just days before she did." (one secondary,
  UMKC) → **VERDICT: Unverifiable.** → EVIDENCE: Single source; no
  corroboration found; conflicts with standard biographical records this
  dossier could not verify from its own sources. → CORRECTED: Do not repeat;
  see CLAIM-047 / CTL-EF-PER-000001.
- **MYTH:** "The *Free Speech* was printed on pink paper to stand out."
  → **VERDICT: Unverifiable.** → EVIDENCE: Single secondary (mahon blog); no
  surviving issue consulted. → CORRECTED: Do not assert; listed as a lead in
  gaps.md.
