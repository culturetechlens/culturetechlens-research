## 02 · Executive Profile

Washington Park is Chicago's Community Area 40, a 1.48-square-mile South Side neighborhood bounded by 51st Street, 60th Street, Cottage Grove Avenue, and the Dan Ryan Expressway. In the late 19th century it was a white working-class and affluent boulevard district; during the Great Migration it underwent one of Chicago's fastest racial transitions — 7.8% white by 1930, over 90% Black by 1940 — and has remained almost exclusively African American since. Its population peaked at 56,856 in 1950 and fell to 12,707 by 2020, driven by white flight, deindustrialization, and large-scale clearance under the city's Land Clearance Commission.

CTL establishes Washington Park as a layered Black cultural landscape: home since 1973 to the DuSable Black History Museum and Education Center; terminus and festival ground of the Bud Billiken Parade (founded 1929, the nation's largest African-American parade); site of Lorado Taft's *Fountain of Time* (1922); setting of James T. Farrell's *Studs Lonigan* and a drive-through scene in Richard Wright's *Native Son*; childhood home of filmmaker Melvin Van Peebles and Gov. Deval Patrick; and the stage of the 34-day Dyett High School hunger strike (2015) that forced Chicago Public Schools to reopen the school as a neighborhood arts high school. The dossier also corrects the record: Anthony Overton Elementary (221 E. 49th St.) is in Grand Boulevard, not Washington Park; Michelle Obama grew up in South Shore, not Washington Park; and the community area does not extend to 63rd Street. What remains uncertain: the pre-1930 institutional record of Black churches and businesses, women's organizational history, and precise block-level data on clearance-era displacement.

## 03 · Significance

- **C-013 (VERIFIED):** Washington Park was one of the principal receiving neighborhoods of the Great Migration's southward expansion in the 1920s — the Black Belt's growth edge, contested block by block through bombings and covenants. Without it, the geography of Black Chicago's expansion is incomplete.
- **C-016 (VERIFIED):** Since 1973 it has housed the DuSable Black History Museum and Education Center, the oldest independent museum of African American history in the United States — the institution's park presence makes Washington Park a national anchor of Black memory infrastructure.
- **C-017 (VERIFIED):** It is the annual terminus of the Bud Billiken Parade (1929–present), the largest African-American parade in the United States — a living cultural institution, not a historic footnote.
- **C-019 (VERIFIED):** The 2015 Dyett hunger strike made Washington Park a national symbol of the fight against school closings in Black neighborhoods (cf. Eve Ewing, *Ghosts in the Schoolyard*, 2018).
- **C-035 (VERIFIED):** The failed 2016 Olympic stadium bid made Washington Park the citywide test case for mega-event development in a historic Black park landscape — community opposition preserved the Olmsted meadow.
- **C-024/C-025 (SUPPORTED):** Its imprint on American literature (*Studs Lonigan*, *Native Son*, *Black Metropolis*) gives it a documentary footprint few Chicago neighborhoods match.

## 07 · Biography/History

**The boulevard district (1869–1910s).** What is now Washington Park began as the western division of Chicago's South Park system, authorized in 1869 and laid out by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux in 1871 — plans lost in the Great Fire and executed in the 1880s by H.W.S. Cleveland (C-006, VERIFIED). Around the park grew a split neighborhood: Irish and German railroad workers and meatpackers in working-class blocks south of Garfield Boulevard (55th St.), and affluent native-born white families in mansions and grand apartments along the wide north-south avenues, above all Grand Boulevard (now Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Drive), with cable cars and the 'L' feeding late-19th-century prosperity (C-008, SUPPORTED per Best/EoC).

**The Great Migration transition (1910s–1930s).** A turn-of-the-century apartment construction boom and the Great Migration pushed Black Chicago southward. White residents left rapidly — a transition "marked with conflicts such as the Race Riot of 1919" (C-009). White homeowners organized: the Washington Park Court Improvement Association vowed not to sell or rent to African Americans, and between 1917 and 1921 bombers attacked Black homeowners and the agents and bankers who served them (C-011, VERIFIED per City landmark report). Banker-developer Jesse Binga's 1917 purchase at 5922 S. King Dr. drew repeated bombings (C-012). By 1930 the area was 7.8% white; by 1940 over 90% Black; by 1960, 0.5% white (C-009). Brian McCammack's *Landscapes of Hope* (2017) documents how Black Chicagoans claimed Washington Park itself for leisure — baseball, fishing, boating — amid white-inflicted violence in the park during 1915–1929 (SUPPORTED).

**Mid-century: density, then clearance (1940s–1970s).** Population peaked at 56,856 in 1950 (C-010). The southern reach of the Robert Taylor Homes (51st–54th along State) put part of the nation's largest public-housing project inside Washington Park (C-015). From the 1950s the Chicago Land Clearance Commission, empowered by the 1955 amendment to the Blighted Areas Redevelopment Act, cleared land for non-residential reuse — Lake Meadows to the north is the documented exemplar — while deindustrialization and white flight hollowed the commercial base; population fell 30.6% in the 1970s and 39.2% in the 1980s (C-014, C-010).

**Cultural anchoring (1973–2000s).** In 1973 the DuSable Museum — founded 1961 by Margaret and Charles Burroughs — moved into the former South Park Commission administration building at 740 E. 56th Pl., planting the oldest independent Black history museum in America inside the park (C-016). The Bud Billiken Parade, founded 1929 by *Chicago Defender* publisher Robert S. Abbott, has terminated in Washington Park with its post-parade festival every second Saturday of August for generations (C-017). Lorado Taft's *Fountain of Time* (1922) and the 2004 National Register listing of the park (C-007, C-018) formalized the landscape's historic status.

**The Olympic bid and its aftermath (2006–2016).** Chicago's 2016 bid sited a temporary ~90,000-seat stadium and aquatics center on the park's Olmsted meadow. The Washington Park Advisory Council produced a 26-point community plan — jobs, crosswalks at 55th, lighting, cooperative housing — while Friends of the Parks, the Hyde Park Kenwood Community Conference, and the National Association for Olmsted Parks opposed building on the historic landscape (C-035). Chicago lost to Rio in October 2009; the meadow survived. Washington Park was also weighed as a site for the Obama Presidential Center before the Foundation selected Jackson Park (C-036).

**The Dyett fight and the present (2012–present).** CPS phased out Walter H. Dyett High School (the area's last open-access neighborhood high school) and closed it in spring 2015. From August 17 to September 19, 2015, a dozen-plus activists — including women strikers Cathy Dale and Jeanette Taylor-Ramann — held a 34-day hunger strike demanding a district-run Global Leadership and Green Technology academy; CPS instead reopened Dyett as an arts-focused neighborhood high school in 2016–17 (C-019, C-039). The 2020 census counted 12,707 residents, 91.9% Black — the first population increase (+8.4%) since 1970 (C-010, C-038).

## 26 · Historiography

- **Park-history consensus:** Olmsted & Vaux 1871 plans lost in the Great Fire; H.W.S. Cleveland executed the design in the 1880s; NRHP nomination (2004) is the authoritative modern record. Naming-year variance: 1880 (Graf) vs. 1881 (HPHS) — minor, documented in C-005.
- **Community-area system:** UChicago sociology (Park & Burgess tradition), 1920s; boundaries/tracts stable — treated as settled (EoC; C-003).
- **Great Migration framing:** The area is a standard case of the Black Belt's southward expansion; City of Chicago's own landmark report (chicago.gov) confirms both the violence (bombings) and the expansion. Racial-composition figures (7.8% white 1930; 0.5% white 1960) trace to the *Encyclopedia of Chicago* (Best) — the strongest secondary; Census-table re-verification remains a gap.
- **McCammack (2017)** adds the leisure/landscape dimension: Black Chicagoans claimed the park itself for nature and recreation 1915–1929 amid white violence — the park is not just a backdrop but a contested site.
- **Dyett historiography:** Ewing's *Ghosts in the Schoolyard* (2018) places the strike in the 2013 mass-closings context; contemporary activist press (Substance News) provides the day-by-day record. Causation (did the strike *cause* reopening?) is worded cautiously — CPS's decision followed the strike, and the strikers' demands were largely met in form (district-run, open-enrollment), though not in theme (arts vs. Global Leadership/Green Technology).
- **"Bronzeville" usage:** Journalistic and marketing sources stretch "Bronzeville" south into Washington Park; CTL does not adopt this as geography per the cross-project audit. Documented where it occurs (e.g., Overton coverage).

## 32 · Legacy

- **Black memory infrastructure:** The DuSable Museum (1973–present) makes Washington Park a permanent national site of African American history-keeping — the oldest independent Black history museum in the U.S. operates from the park.
- **Parade as living institution:** The Bud Billiken Parade's annual return to the park (since 1929) is a continuous thread of Black cultural production across a century.
- **Resistance template:** The 1917–1921 anti-bombing persistence (Binga), the 2007–2009 Olympic-stadium opposition, and the 2015 Dyett hunger strike form a century-long pattern: Washington Park residents defending Black place against erasure — by violence, by development, and by school closure.
- **Literary afterlife:** *Studs Lonigan*, *Native Son*, *Black Metropolis*, and McCammack's scholarship keep the neighborhood in the American canon.
- **What the record needs next:** the women's, workers', and church histories that the current celebrity-heavy record omits (see gaps.md).
