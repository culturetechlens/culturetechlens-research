# CULTURETECHLENS
## Culture, Clearly Seen. — Black Cultural Intelligence

# CTL-DR-BCI-001 — Gaps & Next Investigations

## What we know (solidly established)

- The Great Migration's Chicago arc (1916-1970, ~6M nationally; Chicago Black population 44k to 109k+ in the 1910s alone) and Bronzeville's role as the 'Black Metropolis' are multiply sourced and solid.
- Restrictive covenants, the Hansberry v. Lee case (1940), contract selling/Contract Buyers League, CHA segregation and the Gautreaux litigation, and the 1966 Chicago Freedom Movement are well documented with primary legal sources (court opinions via Justia/vLex) — track-a's strongest material.
- The founding facts of the major Black press/publishing institutions — Defender (1905, Abbott), Associated Negro Press (1919, Barnett), Johnson Publishing (1942), Ebony (1945), Jet (1951) — are consistently corroborated across independent sources.
- The Black banking lineage (Binga 1908/1921, Douglass National, Supreme Life 1919, Seaway 1965-2017) is solidly sourced, including failures — track-c's best work.
- WVON's 1963 founding by the Chess brothers is triangulated across congressional, state, and dissertation sources — the corpus's model entry for contested-founder claims.
- DuSable Museum's 1961 founding, the SSCAC's WPA origins, the Harsh Collection's 1975 transfer, and the Bronzeville demographic peaks/losses (UIC data) are well supported.

## What we think we know (provisional)

- The Defender's causal weight in driving the Great Migration (consensus credits it; historians debate how much vs. labor demand).
- Quinn Chapel's Underground Railroad role (commemorative sources agree; primary 1850s documentation is sparse, as is typical).
- Olivet Baptist's 1861 founding (Fisher 1922) vs. the church's own 1850 memory — unresolved.
- The Bronzeville name origin (1922 alderman's house vs. 1930 newspaper editor) — unresolved.
- The CBL's role in the Fair Housing Act of 1968 (advocacy claim; awareness-raising is documented, legislative causation is not).
- Self-reported superlatives treated as Verified: St. Mark's 'largest in conference,' the Harsh Collection's 'oldest/largest in the Midwest,' Provident's 'first Black-owned hospital in the US.'

## What remains unknown

- Founding dates/founders for specific Chicago Prince Hall lodges and the Chicago IBPOEW lodge (only existence + 1923 convention verified).
- Exact founding year of the Chicago NAACP branch and the Chicago Urban League (1912/1913 and 1916/1917 splits).
- ANP's end date (1964 vs 1967).
- The full roster and scale of Black Chicago's everyday institutions — block clubs, beauty schools, funeral homes, storefront churches — which the current corpus does not attempt to enumerate.
- West Side institutional history in general (no West Side cultural, religious, or economic institutions in the corpus).
- Black Muslim, Black Catholic, African-immigrant, Afro-Caribbean, and Black LGBTQ+ institutional histories in Chicago.
- Black labor history: BSCP Chicago local records, packinghouse organizing, domestic-worker networks.

## What CTL should investigate next (prioritized)

1. **Merge the 8 duplicate entity pairs and re-grade the ~15 overstated Verified claims** — Duplicate canonical entities (Bronzeville x3, Defender x2, DuSable Museum under two names) and Wikipedia-mirror-backed Verified grades are the largest integrity defects; they compound in every downstream artifact and must be fixed before public release.
2. **Black religious Chicago beyond the three historic Protestant churches: Nation of Islam/Mosque Maryam, Moorish Science Temple (both Chicago-born), Black Catholic parishes, Pilgrim Baptist (gospel music), and storefront churches** — Two Chicago-born religious movements with national impact are entirely absent; this is the most conspicuous topical hole and directly contradicts the project's 'infrastructure' framing.
3. **Black women's Chicago: Ida B. Wells/Negro Fellowship League, Lorraine Hansberry, Mamie Till-Mobley, Annie Turnbo Malone/Poro College, Vivian Harsh, Carol Moseley Braun** — Only 12.5% of person entities are women; five of the most significant figures in Black Chicago history have no entities. Poro College simultaneously fills the beauty-culture, women's-labor, and everyday-institutions gaps.
4. **West Side institutions pass: churches, Madison Street business corridor, West Side music venues, block clubs, Austin/Garfield Park community anchors** — Track-b has zero West Side entities and track-c has zero West Side economic institutions; 11% geographic coverage of the city's second Black population center is indefensible in a flagship synthesis.
5. **Black labor history: Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters (Chicago), United Packinghouse Workers, domestic workers** — Track-c covers Black capital thoroughly and Black labor not at all; porters appear only as newspaper distributors. Labor is half the economic story.
6. **Everyday-life institutions: funeral homes, barbershops/beauty salons, block-club networks, churches as social centers** — The corpus covers elite/formal associational life (Masons, Links, Elks) but none of the institutions ordinary Chicagoans actually used weekly; without them the 'infrastructure' claim is hollow.
7. **House music origins (the Warehouse, Frankie Knuckles) and Black LGBTQ+ Chicago** — A globally significant Black Chicago cultural export is missing from the music coverage; its absence also erases Black queer history.
