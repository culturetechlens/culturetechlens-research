# Track E: Red-Team Audit — CTL-DR-BMI-002

*Auditor's working file. Compiled 2026-09-28. Scope: all four research tracks (A: venues, B: labels, C: radio/house, D: lineages/stores). Method: every claim re-checked against its cited evidence as recorded in the tracks; no new factual claims manufactured. Grades referenced are the tracks' own (Verified / Provisional / Disputed / Unresolved) unless this audit recommends a change.*

## Verdict up front

The material is a **strong research base and not yet a publishable flagship**. Its greatest strength is procedural honesty: the evidence-grading discipline is unusually good, the fact-vs-lore section in Track C is exemplary, and Track D's method note (roughly 15 of 46 sources reaching Verified) tells the truth about the base. Genuinely strong primary anchors exist: the NPS Sutherland Hotel nomination, the City of Chicago Warehouse landmark report, three independent Congressional/legislative sources on WVON, Smithsonian SOVA finding aids (Roberta Martin; Martin and Morris), the City's Maxwell Street history, and a Cook County Board resolution.

Against that: at least one outright factual error (the WVON/Washington-1983 anachronism), systematic grade inflation on single-source "Verified" claims, unresolved cross-track contradictions on dates and addresses, and structural coverage gaps (West Side venues, Latinx and suburban scenes, Black classical music, informal scenes). **With the 10 corrections in §16 applied and all Disputed items quarantined to research notes, the Verified core is publishable with attribution. Without them, it is not.**

## 1. Unsupported claims (claims graded Verified that rest on thin evidence)

These are graded Verified in the tracks but should not survive contact with a public audience in their current grading. Each is a candidate for downgrade in §16.

1. **B.L.U.E.S. opened April 1979 by Rob Hecko and Bill Gillmore** (Track A, Verified). Single source: the Chicago Bar Project, a hobbyist bar-review blog. The April-1979 month precision and founder details need a second source (liquor license, Tribune/Defender listing).
2. **Kingston Mines moved to 2548 N. Halsted in 1982** (Track A, Verified). Single source: Wikipedia. The move year and the founding-year conflict (1968 vs. 1969 theatre company) make this provisional at best.
3. **Green Mill "still operating today (2026)"** (Track A, Verified). Sources: Only In Your State (listicle) and TikTok copy. A flagship should not cite TikTok for a current-operations claim; re-source to the venue or recent journalism.
4. **Delmark address history** (Track B, Verified). Source: Discogs label page — user-generated content. The addresses are plausible but Discogs is not a Verified-grade source.
5. **Chess catalog now owned by UMG, managed by Geffen/UME** (Track B, Verified). Source: Wikipedia infobox plus the auditor's own inference ("corroborated by the UMG-owns-MCA lineage"). That is one tertiary source plus reasoning, not corroboration.
6. **Vee-Jay sold ~2.6 million Beatles singles in one month (Vee-Jay + Tollie)** (Track B, Verified). Two secondary music-magazine accounts. Sales figures of this specificity need trade-press (Billboard) or company-record corroboration.
7. **Hot Mix 5 formation and founding roster** (Track C, Verified). Sources: Wikipedia plus mutually-referential fan-archivist YouTube descriptions. The roster is likely right, but "Verified" overstates the base; the archivists are not independent of each other.
8. **Lee's Unleaded Blues reopened 2024** (Track A, Verified). Sources: SomerCor (a lender's project page) and Tripadvisor reviews. Tripadvisor is not an operations-status source; confirm current status before publishing.
9. **Theresa's Lounge opening (late 1949, 4801 S. Indiana Ave.)** (Track A, Verified). "Consistent across three sources" — but all three are blues-enthusiast secondary sites. Consistency among weak sources is not corroboration; keep Verified only if one counts enthusiast consensus, otherwise Provisional.
10. **The Checkerboard 1981 Muddy Waters/Rolling Stones session details** (Track A, Verified). Sources: On Air (events blog), Eric Alper (PR blog), YouTube descriptions citing Wikipedia. The session itself is well documented on the 2012 commercial release; cite the release/Eagle Rock instead.
11. **Ewart Abner's discrimination backstory** (Track B, Verified via Smithsonian). Smithsonian is a strong single source, but it is still a single source for a biographical claim about why he could not find accounting work. Acceptable to keep with attribution ("per Smithsonian"), not as unqualified Verified.
12. **"First house compilation" — DJ International's "The House Sound of Chicago" (1986)** (Track C). Stated as fact ("released the first house compilation") on the strength of a label history and a UMC marketing campaign. No comparative discographic work was done. The "first" must come off or be downgraded.
13. **WGCI "Rap Down" / "Bad Boyz" sessions** (Track D, Provisional — correctly graded). Noted here because the temptation to upgrade will be strong: YouTube uploads are weak provenance. Keep Provisional.

Counterweight — claims graded Verified that **earn** it: the WVON launch/ownership/civil-rights core (three legislative sources + Searcy dissertation abstract); the Warehouse period of significance and namesake link (city landmark report); Sutherland Lounge integration and closure dates (NPS nomination); Maxwell Street's collective-ecosystem account (city government history); Vee-Jay's Gary founding (encyclopedia + NPR + Smithsonian + Indiana history); Curtom 1968 founding (two independent sources in each of two tracks); Chess 2120 S. Michigan occupancy (city landmark publication); Martin and Morris Music Company 1940 founding and business records (Smithsonian SOVA). The audit confirms these.

## 2. Missing communities

- **Mexican-American / Latinx Chicago.** The Warehouse is correctly noted as a Black-and-Latinx room, but Latinx communities appear nowhere as music-makers: no Pilsen/Little Village/Back of the Yards institutions, no Latin jazz or salsa rooms, no norteño/conjunto retail. For a "music metropolis" claim this is a structural hole.
- **Puerto Rican Chicago** (Humboldt Park, Division Street corridor): absent entirely.
- **Caribbean diaspora** (Jamaican, Trinidadian, Haitian communities; reggae sound systems; calypso): absent.
- **African immigrant communities** (post-1980s): absent.
- **Black suburbs**: Maywood, Robbins, Harvey, Markham, Ford Heights, South Holland — Robbins in particular has a documented historic Black music scene; Harvey had clubs. Zero entities.
- **West Side Black neighborhoods as lived places**: Twista (West Garfield Park) and Do or Die (East Garfield Park) give neighborhood origins, but no West Side *institutions* — no clubs, no stores, no churches, no schools.
- **Black classical / concert music**: Florence Price's 1933 Chicago Symphony premiere (a landmark in American music history, happened in Chicago) is absent; the National Association of Negro Musicians, Black church concert series, and Black music educators are absent. This is the single most embarrassing omission for a project calling the city a "music metropolis."
- **The Pekin Theatre** (27th & State, 1904 — the first Black-owned theater in the United States): absent. Its absence from a venues track is a serious gap.
- **Jewish Maxwell Street merchants** (the market's other half): the collective-ecosystem account names migrants, merchants, vendors, crowds — but the Jewish merchant community that co-animated Maxwell Street goes unexamined.
- **Black queer community beyond the Warehouse**: the house-music LGBTQ coverage is good for 1977–1988, but drag/ball culture, queer church musicians, and lesbian bar scenes are absent.
- **South Side neighborhoods east and far south**: South Shore, South Chicago (Lee's is in Greater Grand Crossing — good), Roseland, Pullman, Riverdale, Altgeld Gardens: absent.
- **DuSable High School and Captain Walter Dyett**: Dyett appears once as a bandleader at the Rhumboogie; the school that produced Nat King Cole, Dinah Washington, Johnny Griffin, Gene Ammons, and Redd Foxx has no entity. This belongs in §9 as well.

## 3. Geographic bias

- **South Side dominance is historically defensible but overstated by source availability.** Bronzeville (Regal, Theresa's, Gerri's, Sutherland, Roberts), Woodlawn/63rd (Checkerboard, Basin Street, Budland, Kitty Kat as leads), Hyde Park (Checkerboard relocation, Dr. Wax, Hyde Park Records). This reflects where documentation survives, not necessarily where music lived.
- **West Side: zero venue entities.** No clubs, no taverns, no theaters. The Cobra Records entry notes the "West Side Sound" but gives no West Side addresses or rooms. The West Side — home of the postwar electric-blues tavern circuit — is the biggest geographic hole in Track A.
- **Downtown/Loop: nearly absent.** Universal Recording Corporation (46 E. Walton — where a vast share of Chess, Vee-Jay, and Brunswick sessions were actually cut) appears in no track. Recording *studios* as institutions are missing everywhere; only label-owned rooms are mentioned.
- **North Side: over-covered relative to Black residency.** Kingston Mines, B.L.U.E.S., Green Mill, Gramaphone, Dr. Wax (Lincoln Park origin), Jazz Record Mart's later locations — the North Side blues-tourist corridor and DJ-retail axis get full entities while West Side equivalents get none. The tracks acknowledge the tourist-corridor dynamic (Track A on Kingston Mines/B.L.U.E.S.) but do not correct for it.
- **Suburbs: absent.** See §2.
- **Gary, Indiana**: correctly treated as origin point for Vee-Jay (Track B is explicit that the founding was in Gary, not Chicago) — this is handled well and should be the model for suburb/diaspora coverage.

## 4. Famous-person bias

The tracks are better than most music histories here — club owners (Needham, Roberts, Thurman, Oliver, the Greys/Reys), DJs, producers, and publishers get entities — but star gravity still distorts:

- **Buddy Guy is the load-bearing figure of Track A** (Theresa's first steady gig, Checkerboard co-founder, Legends). The facts support his centrality, but the venue narratives repeatedly route through him; L.C. Thurman, who actually ran the Checkerboard day-to-day for 40+ years, gets thinner treatment, and his death date was not even captured.
- **Venue performer lists are star parades** (Regal: 20+ marquee names; Checkerboard: Muddy, Clapton, SRV, Stones). Rank-and-file players — house bands (Red Saunders excepted), horn sections, the Chess session corps (Lafayette Leake, Otis Spann), backing vocalists — are background. **Malcolm Chisholm**, who engineered Chess sessions for decades, and **Ron Malo** (Universal Recording) are entirely absent: the engineers who literally shaped the recorded sound have no entities while stars have paragraphs.
- **Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, B.B. King** recur as proof-of-importance tokens across venues. The tracks use stars to validate rooms rather than letting rooms' own economics and communities validate them.
- **Frankie Knuckles vs. the scene**: Track C's fact-vs-lore section admirably resists the lone-inventor myth, but the entity structure still centers Knuckles and Hardy while the dancers, door staff, promoters (Robert Williams excepted), and second-tier DJs who constituted the scene are thin.
- **Positive note**: Vivian Carter, Theresa Needham, and Sallie Martin are treated as institutional founders rather than footnotes — the tracks' best anti-star-bias work. The gospel section (Track D) is the least star-driven: it centers publishers, choir directors, and business partnerships.

## 5. Institutional bias (big institutions vs. informal scenes)

Big, documented institutions are covered at depth (Regal, Chess, WVON, Vee-Jay, the Warehouse). The informal layer that the project's own framing ("everyday institutions") promises is mostly named, not researched:

- **House parties**: the name-origin lore mentions "house parties" as a candidate etymology, but no South Side basement/loft party scene is documented — the actual parties are absent while the club that may be named after them gets a landmark report.
- **Park jams, breaking/graffiti, school crews, 1980s battle circuits**: Track D flags this as its thinnest area. Correctly flagged; still a hole.
- **The tavern circuit**: hundreds of South and West Side taverns with weekend bands sustained the West Side Sound; the tracks name clubs but never the circuit as an economic form. **Sylvio's, the 1125 Club, Club Tay May** and their peers are the missing middle between Maxwell Street and the Checkerboard.
- **Skating rinks and bowling alleys as music venues**: Herman Roberts converted his club into a skating rink — noted in passing, never examined as a venue type. Purpose-built rinks with live-music/DJ programming are absent.
- **Social and athletic clubs**: Track A's own 1960 Bulletin listings show social-club cabarets renting the former DeLisa and Rhumboogie rooms — a genuine primary-source lead on Black associational life as venue infrastructure, left undeveloped.
- **Church basements and fellowship halls** as R&B/gospel performance spaces: the two landmark churches are covered; the hundreds of ordinary churches that hosted repasts, anniversaries, and musicals are not.
- **Rent parties**: absent.
- **Jukebox operators**: a key record-breaking mechanism in Black Chicago (operators chose what went in the boxes in Black taverns) — absent from all four tracks.
- **Pressing plants, one-stops, distributors**: Larry Sherman's plant is covered as his asset; the workers, the one-stop distributors, and the Black-owned distributors are not.
- **Record stores as the exception**: Track D genuinely treats stores as infrastructure (libraries, schools, employers, A&R pipelines) — the strongest anti-institutional-bias work in the set. The Calkins peer-reviewed citation on stores in majority-Black neighborhoods is the best single source in the retail section.

## 6. Survivorship bias

- **Documentation-survivorship, not institutional-survivorship, is the real bias.** The tracks cover many dead institutions (Regal, DeLisa, Rhumboogie, Theresa's, Sutherland, Vee-Jay, Chance, Parrot, Dakar, Chi-Sound, Twinight, Cobra, the Warehouse, the Music Box, the Power Plant, Importes, Dr. Wax, Rose Records) — credit where due. But coverage correlates with *having an English Wikipedia article or a landmark report*, which is itself a survivorship filter: white-documented, downtown-adjacent, or star-associated rooms survive in the record; Black-owned rooms that left no press trace do not.
- **Surviving institutions get living-detail depth** (Kingston Mines' motto, ownership succession; Gramaphone's current owner; Hyde Park Records' 2010 founding) while lost ones get date ranges — natural, but it means the present-day North Side corridor reads as more "real" than the demolished South Side rooms. Flagship copy should counterweight with maps, addresses, and photographs of lost rooms.
- **The "Blues District" that never happened** (Gerri's Palm Tavern, 2002 plan, outcome unknown) is a useful memento mori the tracks preserve well — keep it.
- **Lost informal scenes** (§5) suffer double survivorship bias: they neither survived nor were documented. The tracks are honest about this (Track D gap #1); the flagship must not present the documented institutional map as the whole territory.

## 8. Missing working-class history

- **Musicians' day jobs**: Jimmy Reed (stockyards) is the one developed case; Muddy Waters's factory work rests on a fan site (bluesforyou.com) graded Provisional — correctly; Twista's pre-fame jobs (factory, McDonald's, security, barber) rest on Wikipedia, graded Provisional — correctly. The *pattern* — the day-labor/night-gig economy that structured Black Chicago music-making — is asserted but never evidenced as a system. This is a flagship-level interpretive claim that needs labor-history sourcing, not anecdotes.
- **Pressing-plant workers, one-stop clerks, distributor drivers, jukebox operators**: absent (§5).
- **Record-store clerks**: covered at Importes (Wilcots, Chip E., Derrick Carter) and Gramaphone — good; Dr. Wax/Hyde Park/Rose clerks absent.
- **Radio labor beyond stars**: engineers, record librarians, salespeople (WGCI's general sales manager is quoted once — good), overnight jocks.
- **Club labor**: bartenders, bouncers/security, sound techs, coat check, kitchen staff (Gerri's was a restaurant — the food-service side unexamined).
- **Promoters beyond Robert Williams**: the South and West Side party promoters who rented halls weekly are absent; the economics of the door (cover charges, memberships, bar percentages) is touched once (Warehouse admission doubling) and dropped.
- **Audiences as economic actors**: crowds appear as atmosphere, not as the paying base whose dimes built Record Row. The Calkins finding (stores once clustered in majority-Black neighborhoods; higher 1980–1990 failure rates outside majority-white areas) is the one structural economic datum — build on it.

## 10. Weak sources

The tracks grade honestly; the weak-source inventory below is for the downgrade pass (§16):

- **Single-source "Verified" claims**: see §1 items 1–11. The recurring pattern is *one enthusiast/tertiary source → Verified*. Rule for publication: one source = Provisional, always; two independent sources or one primary/institutional source = Verified.
- **Tertiary mirrors cited as evidence**: Alchetron (Track A: Regal live-album claims; Track B: Earl Edwards songwriting, Cobra Records). Alchetron is a Wikipedia mirror — citing it alongside Wikipedia is not corroboration. Replace or drop.
- **Crowdsourced**: setlist.fm (Roberts/Sutherland dates — tracks grade Provisional/Disputed; keep quarantined), Discogs (Track B treats label-page content as Verified in places — downgrade), Wikipedia throughout (usable as a finding aid, not as the terminal citation for flagship copy).
- **Fan archivists**: YouTube aircheck biographies (Ellis B. Feaster et al.) carry much of Track C's DJ/personnel detail — graded Provisional, correctly; do not upgrade without institutional corroboration (Museum of Broadcast Communications, station records).
- **Commercial/weak**: Mystic Stamp Company (Track D: Roberta Martin Studio of Music 1939; 1998 stamp — needs USPS records), TeePublic merch copy (Track D: Rose Records — correctly graded Unresolved), Chicago Bar Project (Track A: B.L.U.E.S. — graded Verified; must be downgraded), On Air/Eric Alper blogs (Track A: Checkerboard session — re-source to the commercial release).
- **Search excerpts with no retrievable page** (Track D: Mayfield formation context, Impressions formation, hip-house notes): honestly graded Provisional, but excerpts are not citable — exclude from any public derivative until replaced.
- **Single-interview claims presented structurally**: GO BANG!/Rachael Cain interview (Trax TX-101 first release; "Fantasy" first recorded), Heiman interview (Curtom business structure) — valuable participant testimony; keep attributed, never as unqualified fact.
- **What is strong**: NPS nomination, city landmark reports (Warehouse; First Church of Deliverance; New Regal), Congressional Record ×3 + IL HR0201, Smithsonian SOVA finding aids, City of Chicago DCASE history, Cook County resolution, Loyola dissertation abstract, Calkins peer-reviewed article, contemporary press (1986 SPIN on Importes; 2008 Daily Northwestern on Dr. Wax; 2025 RA obituary for Joe Dale). The flagship's citation spine should be built from these.

## 14. Misleading causal claims

1. **WVON's talk format "powered Harold Washington's 1983 mayoral campaign"** (Track C entity text). **Temporal impossibility**: the format flip is dated 1986 (HR0201) or 1989 (WGRB history); Washington's campaign was 1983. What the sources support: WVON *as a Black music station* plus journalist Lu Palmer ("We Shall See in '83") backed the 1983 campaign; the talk format came later and served subsequent campaigns (Moseley Braun, Obama). **This is the most serious factual error in the four tracks — correct before any public use.**
2. **Rhumboogie "killed by a New Year's Eve fire"** (Track A summary). The fire (Dec 31, 1945) closed it; it reopened June 1946 and closed for good May 1947. The fire did not kill it; it "never regained its form." Correct the summary to match the body's timeline.
3. **"Success with the Beatles and Four Seasons destroyed Vee-Jay"** (Track B summary). Punchy and directionally supported (ruinous Four Seasons royalty terms are documented), but monocausal phrasing crowds out the documented co-causes: Abner's financial management, Capitol lawsuits, the 1963 LA move, the 1963 leadership shakeup. Soften to multi-causal in flagship copy; keep blame attributions (Abner's gambling) as Provisional single-source.
4. **Club DeLisa closed "after two of the DeLisa brothers died"** (Track A: Provisional, single source). Plausible; still a single-source causal claim from the African American Registry. Attribute, don't assert.
5. **Gerri's Palm Tavern shutdown → "Blues District" plan**: Track A correctly presents these as separate bullets (2001 shutdown; 2002 redevelopment plan). Flagship copy must not imply the district plan caused the shutdown — no causal link is evidenced.
6. **Kingston Mines's move "tripled revenues"** via Halsted-corridor synergy (Track A: Provisional, Chicago Bar Project — an interested party's account). One-sided business lore; attribute.
7. **"Disco died at Disco Demolition Night; house rose from its ashes"**: Track C explicitly rebuts this simplification — exemplary. Keep the rebuttal in flagship copy.
8. **Trax exploitation narrative** (no royalties, no contracts): Track C correctly frames as documented grievances from named artist interviews, not adjudicated findings — keep that framing.
9. **Maxwell Street**: the City's collective-ecosystem account (migrants + merchants + electricity + crowds + technology) is the model causal account; Track D's editorial note rightly warns against single-inventor framing. Keep.
10. **Dr. Wax closures** attributed to iTunes/downloading and **Jazz Record Mart** closure to rising rent: both are the owners' own attributed explanations (Daily Northwestern; Delmark history) — acceptable as attributed, not as structural analysis. The Calkins article is the structural complement — pair them.

## 15. Copyright / rights concerns

- **Quoted lyrics: none found.** All four tracks quote song *titles* only ("Higher and Higher," "Move Your Body," "Acid Tracks," "Your Love," "Po Pimp," "Take It EZ," etc.). Titles are not copyrightable; no lyric clearance needed for the research files as written. **Flagship rule: do not add lyric quotations without clearance.**
- **Short slogans/mottos quoted**: Kingston Mines's "Hear Blues. Drink Booze. Talk Loud. You're Among Friends." and WVON's "The Voice of the Negro" / "We Shall See in '83." Short attributed phrases; low risk, but trademark review is prudent for merchandise or title use.
- **Images**: no images are embedded in the tracks. If flagship publication uses the Russell Lee 1941 Club DeLisa photograph (via Mike Savad's colorized version), note the chain: the underlying FSA/LOC negative is likely public domain, but **Savad's colorization is a new creative work** — clear or avoid the colorized version. No other specific images are proposed in the tracks.
- **Audio**: no audio embedded. If the flagship publishes audio (e.g., "Your Love" tapes, "Acid Tracks," Checkerboard 1981 session excerpts), rights are non-trivial: the Trax catalog's chain of title is itself disputed in the research (see §11; documented artist grievances over contracts/royalties). **Do not assume Trax/Rachael Cain can clear Trax-era masters; require chain-of-title documentation.** The 1981 Checkerboard recording (2012 commercial release) likewise needs label clearance.
- **Archival text**: NPS nominations, Congressional Record, Illinois HR0201, Cook County resolution, city landmark reports, and city DCASE histories are U.S. government/municipal works — public domain or freely citable with attribution. Smithsonian finding aids: citable; reproducing archival *holdings* (letters, business records) needs repository permission. HistoryMakers oral histories and UChicago home-movie footage: licensed archival material — permission required for quotation beyond fair use.
- **Quotations used**: Cub Koda ("America's greatest blues label"), Clarence Boyer via Hymnology Archive, Ebony via the First Church landmark report — all short, attributed; fair-use low risk. Keep attributions attached in flagship copy.
- **Names, likenesses, estates**: Muddy Waters, Chess, and Vee-Jay estates; the Buddy Guy and Frankie Knuckles likenesses. Research/educational use is low risk, but commercial flagship products (merch, paid exhibits) need publicity-rights review — flag for legal, not a research blocker.
- **Bottom line**: the research files as written create no clearance emergency. The risk enters at the *production* stage (photos, audio, merchandise). Recommend a rights log be opened now listing every planned image/audio asset with its provenance — especially anything Trax-related.
