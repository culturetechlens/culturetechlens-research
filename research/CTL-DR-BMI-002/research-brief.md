# CTL-DR-BMI-002 — Research Brief

**Project:** *The Sound of Black Chicago: The Venues, Labels, Stations, Stores and People That Built a Music Metropolis*
**Research ID:** CTL-DR-BMI-002 · **Date:** 2026-09-28 · **Status:** Internal working material (not public-facing)

---

## Executive summary

This package maps the *institutions* of Black Chicago music — the venues, labels, radio stations, record stores, and gospel organizations that turned a Great Migration destination into a music metropolis. Four parallel research tracks (venues; labels; radio/DJs/house; lineages/stores) produced **81 entities, 351 evidence-graded claims, 172 timeline events, and 82 verified-only relationships**, drawn from roughly 175 track-level source consultations. An independent red-team audit applied a 15-point checklist and issued 14 binding corrections, all applied during consolidation.

**Central finding:** Black Chicago repeatedly built a *complete, self-sustaining music stack* — performance rooms, recording companies, broadcast outlets, retail counters, and sacred institutions — that generated nationally dominant forms (gospel, electric blues, soul, house) while remaining structurally under-capitalized and under-archived. The infrastructure story is the through-line; the stars are its products, not its explanation.

**Publication verdict:** a strong research base, **not yet fit for unrestricted flagship public release**. The Verified core (157 claims) is publishable with attribution once corrections are in. All Disputed and Unresolved material must remain in a clearly labeled research/lore layer, and a rights log must be opened before any audio, photo, lyric, or archival image is used.

## Research question

How did Black Chicago's venues, labels, stations, stores, and people build a music metropolis — and what does that infrastructure look like when you map the institutions rather than just the stars?

Sub-questions the tracks pursued: which rooms incubated which sounds; how records got made, financed, and distributed; how radio and DJs moved music through the city; how gospel institutions functioned as training and touring infrastructure; how retail counters shaped genres; and where women and working-class participants did load-bearing work that the standard histories under-credit.

## Methodology / evidence model

- **Four parallel tracks, independently sourced**, then consolidated with duplicate-entity merging and cross-track contradiction flags. Track working files are preserved under `working/`.
- **Evidence grading on every claim** — *Verified* (corroborated by primary sources or multiple independent secondaries), *Provisional* (single or thin source), *Disputed* (sources contradict), *Unresolved* (no usable source found). Contradictory evidence is retained, not smoothed over.
- **Knowledge-graph relationships are verified-only.** No link was manufactured to complete a narrative; informal or single-source connections are flagged as such.
- **Source hierarchy:** primary and archival sources preferred (NPS nominations, City landmark reports, Congressional/legislative records, Smithsonian finding aids, city histories, oral histories); institutional claims are attributed to the institution rather than converted into objective facts; crowdsourced sources (Discogs, setlist.fm, Wikipedia, fan archivists) are capped at Provisional.
- **Red-team audit (Track E):** every claim re-checked against its cited evidence; 14 verdicts applied during consolidation and recorded per-claim in `entities.json` under `red_team_notes`. Grade inflation was the audit's main finding — nine single-source "Verified" claims were downgraded.

## Key findings

1. **The complete stack.** Bronzeville's entertainment corridor (Regal, Savoy, Club DeLisa, Rhumboogie) → postwar blues rooms (Theresa's, Checkerboard, Sylvio's) → North Side blues clubs (Kingston Mines, B.L.U.E.S.) → the house-music club sequence (Warehouse → Music Box/Muzic Box → Power Plant). Labels ran in parallel: Chance → Vee-Jay (Gary-to-Chicago), Chess, the Brunswick/Dakar/Chi-Sound R&B operation under producer Carl Davis, Curtom, and Delmark as a documenter of blues and jazz rather than a hit factory.
2. **The Gary–Chicago pipeline.** Vee-Jay was founded in Gary, Indiana (1953) by Vivian Carter and James Bracken — a record-store-to-label story that puts Black women's entrepreneurship at the origin of one of the great Black-owned labels.
3. **House music is a club story before it is a record story.** The Warehouse (1977; owner Robert Williams, resident DJ Frankie Knuckles), Ron Hardy's Music Box, and Knuckles's Power Plant form a verified institutional sequence. The "first house record" is genuinely disputed territory ("On and On," "Your Love," Z-Factor's "Fantasy," Chip E.'s "Jack Trax"), and the name's origin is graded Provisional with three competing mechanisms rather than one asserted etymology. "First house compilation" was struck as an unsupported superlative.
4. **Gospel as infrastructure.** Thomas A. Dorsey's "father of gospel" framing follows historian Bernice Johnson Reagon: he earned the title through a campaign to install the music in Black Protestant worship, not by inventing the blues–sacred blend. Pilgrim Baptist, Ebenezer, the National Convention of Gospel Choirs and Choruses, and the Roberta Martin Singers form a training, touring, and publishing spine — and a pipeline to secular stardom.
5. **Maxwell Street and the record store as infrastructure.** The open-air market and stores like Jazz Record Mart, Imports Etc., Dr. Wax, and Rose Records functioned as A&R systems, genre-formation sites, and talent pipelines (retail counter → production). Retail displacement happened in two modes: rent pressure and digital pressure.
6. **Two factual corrections that change the story.** (a) WVON's 1983 Harold Washington coverage belonged to the station's *music era* and figures like Lu Palmer — the later all-Black talk format cannot be credited with the 1983 campaign. (b) Rhumboogie was not "killed by fire": fire on December 31, 1945 → reopened June 1946 → closed May 1947.

## Historical overview

The Great Migration built Bronzeville's "Stroll" and its entertainment corridor — the Regal and Savoy anchoring 47th Street, with Club DeLisa and Rhumboogie defining 1940s Black nightlife. Postwar, electric blues took Chess and Vee-Jay national while rooms like Theresa's and the Checkerboard held the South and West Sides. Gospel institutions ran underneath everything, training generations of singers, pianists, and choir directors.

The 1960s–70s soul era ran through Carl Davis's operations at Brunswick, Dakar, and Chi-Sound, and Curtis Mayfield's Curtom — Black creative control inside and alongside white-owned capital. As disco collapsed and major-label attention drifted, the Warehouse (1977) became the room where a new dance music was assembled; WBMX's Hot Mix 5 broadcast it; DJ International and Trax pressed it. House went from a Chicago club secret to a global form — while the clubs, stores, and labels that made it stayed fragile, under-documented, and exposed to rent and format shifts.

## The strong Verified core

Anchors a public release can stand on: the NPS Sutherland Hotel nomination; the City of Chicago Warehouse landmark designation report (2023); three independent Congressional/legislative sources on WVON; Smithsonian SOVA finding aids (Roberta Martin; Martin and Morris); the City's Maxwell Street history; a Cook County Board resolution. 157 of 351 claims hold Verified status after red-team review — concentrated in venue histories, the house-music club sequence, gospel institutions, and broadcast history.

## Limitations

- **Structural coverage gaps:** West Side venues, Black suburban scenes, Latinx/Puerto Rican/Caribbean/African community music spaces, and Black classical music are thin or absent. Informal infrastructure — house parties, park jams, taverns, skating rinks, jukebox operators, engineers, pressing-plant workers — is named but under-researched.
- **A large Provisional middle (158 claims).** Much of the date/address/scale detail rests on single sources; it is honest but not yet flagship-grade.
- **Quarantined contradictions:** Music Box address, Warehouse build/purchase year, Ron Hardy death year (1991 vs. March 2, 1992), "Your Love" demo date (1982 vs. 1984), "Move Your Body" first label (Trax vs. DJ International), Regal Theater architect, NCGCC founding year (1932 vs. 1933).
- **Rights:** no audio, photos, lyrics, archival scans, or commercial artwork cleared. Trax catalog rights are especially sensitive; label jacket copy is self-promotion, not evidence.

## What we know / think we know / remains unknown / investigate next

- **We know:** the institutional map of Black Chicago music across a century; the Gary–Chicago label pipeline; the Warehouse → Music Box → Power Plant sequence; gospel's infrastructural role; the record store as A&R system; the corrected WVON and Rhumboogie chronologies.
- **We think we know (Provisional):** many specific dates, addresses, and scales; roster and formation details for DJ collectives; the mechanics of the house-music name origin; catalog ownership chains.
- **Remains unknown:** Vee-Jay's bankruptcy month; Brunswick's present catalog ownership; NCGCC's founding year; Imports Etc.'s closing date; Rose Records' dates and scale; the exact priority order of the first house records; the full map of West Side, suburban, and non-Black-American community scenes.
- **Investigate next:** the archival priorities in `gaps.md` — Defender/Tribune listings for venue dates, liquor-license and city-directory records, FCC files, Billboard trade-press for sales and catalog claims, corporate records for bankruptcies and chains of title, and oral histories with surviving Warehouse/Music Box/Power Plant, Hot Mix 5, gospel, Maxwell Street, and early hip-hop participants before that generation ages out.
