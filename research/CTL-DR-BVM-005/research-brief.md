## 1. Methodology and evidence standard

This package follows the CTL master evidence standard:

- **Every major claim** records: the claim, the evidence, the source, primary/secondary classification, publication/date, uncertainty, and contradictory evidence where it exists.
- **Facts are graded exactly four ways:**
  - **Verified** — multiple independent credible sources agree, no material contradiction.
  - **Provisional** — a credible source asserts it, but corroboration is thin or single-sourced.
  - **Disputed** — credible sources conflict; competing versions are preserved, not averaged.
  - **Unresolved** — claimed but unverified, or contradicted without resolution.
- Track A's **CORROBORATED** grade maps to **Verified** only where two or more independent credible secondary sources agree with no material contradiction.
- **Relationships are Verified only.** Provisional and Disputed links were excluded from `relationships.json`.
- **Source preference:** census data, government/court/city records, Sanborn maps, historical newspapers, archival and university collections, institutional records, and peer-reviewed scholarship. Where this package falls short of that bar, it says so (see gaps.md).

## 2. Geographic definition — read this before the maps

Bronzeville is not one fixed place, and the artifact must not draw it as one.

- **The historic core** is the Black Belt's northern section: roughly **Douglas and the northern half of Grand Boulevard** community areas — 22nd to 51st Streets, State Street to Cottage Grove, with the densest commercial and institutional life along State Street and South Parkway (now King Drive).
- **Douglas, Grand Boulevard, Washington Park, and Woodlawn are distinct.** The research keeps them separate: the *Hansberry v. Lee* house (6140 S. Rhodes) is in **Woodlawn**; Robert Taylor Homes ran along **Grand Boulevard/Washington Park**; the "Bronzeville" of the 1920s-40s core sits in **Douglas/northern Grand Boulevard**.
- **The Stroll's boundaries are disputed and era-shifting.** No fixed boundary line is drawn without qualification.
- **Marketing "Bronzeville" has expanded** far beyond the historic core in recent decades. Mapping today's brand onto 1920s blocks is boundary presentism — a documented red-team risk.

## 3. The Rise — commerce, institutions, and the Stroll

### Black capital on State Street
The district's economic spine was **Black-owned commercial development concentrated on east State Street around 35th–39th** — the corridor of Binga Bank (private bank 1908, state charter 1921, closed June 1930; correctly framed as the first Black-owned bank in the Northern United States, not the nation's first), the Binga Arcade, the Overton Hygienic Building (commissioned 1922), and the Chicago Bee Building (commissioned 1929 by Anthony Overton, who also founded the Bee and Douglass National Bank — founding year disputed 1921/1922/1923, relationship Verified).

### The 47th Street complex — a corrective
The larger **47th/South Parkway Regal–Savoy–South Center complex was principally white-developed and financed** — the Regal opened February 4, 1928, built by the National Theater Corporation, sold to Balaban & Katz in 1931, and did not come under Black ownership until S. B. Fuller's 1961 purchase; it was demolished in 1973. The Savoy Ballroom (4733 S. Parkway, opened November 1927; closing year disputed, 1948 vs. 1954) and the South Center department store were part of the same project. This is a district pattern, not a claim that every 47th Street business was white-owned — the Palm Tavern (opened 1933) is a documented Black-owned counterexample.

### Venues and the music corridor
The Stroll's venue layer is the richest and least primary-verified part of this package: the Pekin Theatre (1904; first Black-owned stock theater), Dreamland Café (1914; address disputed, 3520 vs. 3618 S. State), the Sunset Café (opened August 3, 1921; separate from the Grand Terrace — online sources routinely conflate them), the Vendome (Louis Armstrong with Erskine Tate, 1925 — Verified), Lincoln Gardens/Royal Gardens, the Plantation Café, the Apex Club, and the Monogram and Metropolitan theaters (both lost). **Address disputes and the Savoy closing-year conflict are preserved as alternatives, not resolved.**

### Institutions that carried the neighborhood
Churches anchored it: Quinn Chapel AME (1844/1847), Olivet Baptist (1861), Pilgrim Baptist (founded September 1916; purchased the former Kehilath Anshe Ma'ariv synagogue in 1922; Thomas A. Dorsey invited as music director 1931), Ebenezer Missionary Baptist (formed 1902; Dorsey organized the first modern gospel choir there, 1931), and Liberty Baptist — grown from the women-founded Olive Leaf Club of 1917. The Wabash Avenue YMCA (completed 1913) hosted the ASNLH founding meeting (September 1915) and the organizing of Negro History Week (February 7, 1926), and was later saved by the Renaissance Collaborative. Schools: Phillips (opened September 1904; Maudelle Bousfield principal 1939–1950) and DuSable (opened February 1935; Timuel Black graduated June 1937).

### Black Metropolis as scholarship
Drake and Cayton's *Black Metropolis* (1945, with Richard Wright's Introduction) is this package's most cited interpretive frame — and is treated as scholarship, not scripture: the record includes the "ghetto story" framing critique and the exclusion of women.

## 4. Everyday and working-class life

The package deliberately counterweights venues and famous names:

- **Kitchenettes** (Wright coined the term in *12 Million Black Voices*, 1941), rent parties, overcrowding statistics (Frazier 1932; the 1922 Race Relations Commission report), packinghouse and domestic workers, beauticians and barbers, storefront churches, street-corner political forums, and queer nightlife.
- **The Pullman-porter correction:** the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters (founded August 25, 1925) was the first labor organization led by African Americans to receive an AFL charter (1935) — not "the first Black labor union." Bronzeville porter-residency claims and "porters carried the Defender south" remain unverified.
- **Women included substantively:** Vivian G. Harsh (Hall Branch), Elizabeth Lindsay Davis, Irene McCoy Gaines (whose 1931 committee work led to the Ida B. Wells Homes), Marjorie Stewart Joyner, Margaret T.G. Burroughs, Michelle Duster (Wells monument and festival), and the Olive Leaf Club founders Bertie Boone and Maggie Walker.
- **Oral-history leads are identified but not yet consulted** — CHM's *Neighborhoods: Keepers of Culture*, the Studs Terkel Radio Archive, The HistoryMakers, the Bronzeville Historical Society's video collection, and Timuel Black's *Bridges of Memory*. The flagship must not publish "everyday life" as authority before that listening is done.

## 5. Policy and multi-causal transformation

### The covenant era
The Washington Park Subdivision covenant (1927, Woodlawn) required 95% frontage assent; only 54% signed. *Burke v. Kleiman* upheld it; Carl Hansberry's purchase of 6140 S. Rhodes triggered *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32 (1940), which reversed on inadequate class representation (res judicata) — **not** on the merits of covenants generally. *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) rendered covenants unenforceable. The HOLC graded the corridor D — area D74, 35th–67th, State to Cottage Grove — with explicitly racialized reasoning.

### Clearance, expressways, and public housing
- **Lake Meadows** (1948-1954): ~100+ acres cleared for a New York Life–backed middle-income project; the oft-repeated "18,000 displaced" aggregate figure is unverified.
- **Dan Ryan Expressway:** clearance 1957–58, first segment opened December 12, 1961, full route December 15, 1962, CTA Red Line in the median 1969. Displacement counts (10,000? 12,000?) are Unresolved.
- **Ida B. Wells Homes** (constructed 1939–41; demolished 2002–11), **Robert Taylor Homes** (opened March 5, 1962; demolished 1998–2007; unit count disputed 4,321 vs. 4,415), **Madden Park Homes** (1970), and the CHA **Plan for Transformation** (2000) completed the demolition cycle.

### The house position: no single cause
CTL rejects single-cause decline stories. The evidence supports a **multi-causal account**: deindustrialization, deliberate segregation policy, demographic pressure, disinvestment, urban renewal and public-housing siting/demolition, expressway routing, and constrained-then-expanded mobility/outmigration operating together across decades. And "decline" is partly a narrative artifact — institutions persisted, and people stayed.

### The population arc (Provisional)
Douglas + Grand Boulevard combined decennial counts — 1950: 193,302 → 1960: 132,361 → 1970: 123,897 → 1980: 89,441 → 1990: 66,549 → 2000: 54,476 → 2010: 40,167 → 2020: 44,880 — derive from Rob Paral's compilation via Wikipedia and **require direct Census/NHGIS verification** before publication.

## 6. Preservation and the remaking — with competing viewpoints

### Preservation wins
Six Black Metropolis properties were individually listed on the National Register on **April 30, 1986**; the **Black Metropolis–Bronzeville Chicago Landmark District** was designated **September 9, 1998**; and the **Bronzeville–Black Metropolis National Heritage Area** was established by Public Law 117-339, signed **January 5, 2023**. Saved and reused sites include Supreme Life, the Eighth Regiment Armory, the Wabash YMCA, Overton Hygienic, the Chicago Bee Building, Rosenwald Courts, and The Forum.

### Active wounds
Pilgrim Baptist was gutted by fire on **January 6, 2006**; the National Museum of Gospel Music's construction/funding status still needs a current check. Unity Hall's condition, the Sunset Café building's post-2017 status, and the Defender Building's address and use are unresolved.

### The remaking — all viewpoints on the table
The package records **competing interpretations** of Bronzeville's 21st century: displacement concern (home prices rose ~$200K, 2018–22); Black middle-class reinvestment; selected-tract research reporting poverty reduction without measurable displacement; intentional community investment (QCDC, Urban Juncture, Emerald South); managed mixed-income growth (Rosenwald Courts, 43 Green, 4400 Grove); market-positive framing (Bronzeville Lakefront); and structural critique. **Developer job/economic-impact figures are graded as developer claims**, and Phase 1 completion status in late 2026 is unverified.

## 7. Closing assessment

### What we know
- The commercial geography's ownership pattern: Black-owned capital concentrated on State Street; the 47th/South Parkway entertainment complex principally white-developed until 1961.
- The institutional spine: churches, the Wabash YMCA, the Defender and Bee, Black banks, and the NAACP/Urban League/UL network — with dates and relationships Verified where the record supports it.
- The policy arc: covenant → *Hansberry* → *Shelley* → HOLC redlining → urban renewal → expressway → public-housing siting → HOPE VI demolition, with the legal and planning facts Verified.
- The preservation arc: 1986 NRHP listings, 1998 landmark district, 2023 National Heritage Area, and the specific saved/reused buildings.
- The multi-causal transformation framework and the naming-origin correction (Gentry/Bee 1930 → Defender 1932).

### What we think we know
- The venue-by-venue chronology of the Stroll (Lindstrom-based; Provisional where single-sourced).
- The population series (Paral compilation; needs NHGIS verification).
- The broad outlines of working-class and women's institutional life (rich in leads, thin in primary verification).
- The remaking's competing narratives (stakeholder-dependent).

### What remains unknown
- Exact displacement counts for Lake Meadows, the Dan Ryan, and aggregate urban renewal.
- Savoy closing year; Dreamland address; Regal architect; Douglass Bank founding year; *Hansberry* decision day; Robert Taylor unit count.
- Current (late-2026) status of Bronzeville Lakefront Phase 1, the Obama Presidential Center, the National Museum of Gospel Music, Unity Hall, and the Sunset Café building.
- Named working-class institutions: barbershops, beauty shops, storefront churches, fraternal lodges.
- Copyright/rights status of the archival images a flagship would need.
