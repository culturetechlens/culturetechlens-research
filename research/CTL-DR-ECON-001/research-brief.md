# CTL-DR-ECON-001
## The Black Business Ecosystem: How Black Communities Built, Financed, Sustained and Lost Economic Infrastructure

**Research brief — 2026-09-28 · Status: research complete, red-teamed**
**Grades in this package:** Verified / Provisional / Disputed / Unresolved. Disputed dates are preserved, not smoothed. Red-team corrections applied 2026-09-28 (42 findings: 7 blocking, 23 should-fix) — see `red-team.md`.

---

## 1. The question

Black Chicago's economic history is usually told as a gallery of famous entrepreneurs. This project asks a different question: **what was the economic machine?** Who supplied the capital, how did money move through the community's institutions, what held the ecosystem together, and what broke it?

Chicago-first throughout; national comparisons only where evidence supports them. This project cross-references rather than duplicates the sibling lenses: BVM-005 (Bronzeville corridors, Black-owned State Street), PLACE-001 (63rd Street), BPM-003 (newspapers as businesses and ad vehicles), and BCI-001 (institutions). ECON-001's distinctive contribution is **economic mechanics**: capital, finance, ecosystem links, reinvestment, and breakdown.

## 3. The cycle this project maps

```
CAPITAL → ENTREPRENEUR → BUSINESS → WORKERS → SUPPLIERS → CUSTOMERS
   → COMMUNITY → INSTITUTIONS → REINVESTMENT → (again)
```

Each link was researched, and each breaking point was documented as either a **documented cause** or a **plausible explanation kept distinct**.

## 4. Where the capital came from

The strongest finding in the corpus cuts against the usual story of bank lending. In Black Chicago, **insurance reserves appear to have been a larger documented channel into Black homeownership than bank lending**: Chicago Metropolitan Mutual's mortgage holdings reportedly rose from $6,000 to $281,000 between 1938 and 1945. Supreme Liberty Life's president stated in 1934 that the company functioned as a lender of mortgage capital to Black Chicagoans — a Verified claim with documentary backing.

The formal/informal boundary was porous. Founders' personal funds, church-member investments, and in at least one documented case **numbers-gambling profits** helped capitalize institutions (Binga State Bank's capitalization included informal-economy wealth). Jesse Binga's private bank (1908, state charter 1921) was the first Black-owned bank in Chicago; it closed in 1930, and Binga was convicted of embezzling $22,000 in 1933 — the ecosystem's first major institutional failure.

## 5. The anchor firms

The dossier work corrects and deepens several canonical stories:

- **S.B. Fuller / Fuller Products** — corrected the Pittsburgh Courier claim (see red-team F-001): the Courier's 1965 sale went to John H. Sengstacke, and the assertion that Fuller bought it is now carried as Disputed/contradicted. The Regal Theater purchase (1961, first Black ownership) is Verified. The decline story is documented as multi-causal: the White Citizens Councils boycott of the Nadal line, the 1963 NAM/Baptist-convention backlash, plus plausible-but-unproven integration-era competition.
- **Anthony Overton** — conglomerate builder (Hygienic Manufacturing, Victory Life, Douglass National Bank, Half-Century Magazine), Spingarn Medalist (Provisional pending medal confirmation), with birth date (1865, disputed day) and death day (July 1946) preserved as Disputed.
- **Annie Turnbo Malone / Poro College** — corrected to St. Louis-founded (not Chicago); founding year preserved as Disputed (1917 vs 1918); Chicago enters with the 1930 headquarters move.
- **Johnson Products** — exchange dispute resolved as the American Stock Exchange (BET's NYSE variant treated as erroneous); the Soul Train sponsorship superlative downgraded to Provisional.
- **Supreme Life** — founded 1919 by Frank L. Gillespie as Liberty Life; the company history is anchored in the Business History Review. An implausible $1.65 trillion "insurance in force" figure from the older study is flagged as a likely transcription error and carried as Disputed, never to render.
- **Johnson Publishing** — the Fuller "trained John H. Johnson" claim was unsupported on audit; George E. Johnson's Fuller salesmanship is the documented training link. The mentorship narrative is now carried as Provisional lore.

The ecosystem finding that matters most: **these firms were chains, not islands.** Fuller, Overton, Johnson Products, Johnson Publishing, Supreme Life and related firms shared training pipelines, employment, finance, production, and supplier relationships — a genuine ecosystem, not a set of isolated "great entrepreneur" stories.

## 6. Ordinary, lost, and overlooked firms

- **Leak & Sons Funeral Homes** (founded 1933) — the documented multigenerational counterexample to firm erasure: four generations of family leadership, still operating across three Illinois locations. The founding-capital accounts vary ($500 loan from a grandmother vs. $500 loan + $500 earned at the 1933 World's Fair); both variants are preserved.
- **Jones brothers enterprises, George's Music Room, Palm Tavern, Pershing Hotel complex, Soft Sheen, Great Northern Realty** — ordinary-firm dossiers showing how business lived at street level.
- **The largest blind spot: women-owned ordinary firms.** Beauty is documented as a sector (Poro, Joyner, beauty schools), but salon addresses, boarding-house keepers, dressmakers, and caterers remain poorly recovered from the sources consulted.
- **Joyner's patent** — researched and corrected to U.S. Patent **1,693,515**; the 1,683,530 variant is erroneous (flagged for correction in BVM-005 as well).

## 7. Geography and support infrastructure

- **State Street** — the Black-owned/financed commercial building series, anchored by the Jordan Building (ground broken 1916, commissioned by Joseph J. Jordan) and the Overton Hygienic Building.
- **Madison Street (West Side)** — documented as largely a white-owned corridor serving Black consumers; the 1965–66 pickets and the April 1968 destruction of 100+ businesses are Verified; named Black-owned firms on the corridor are largely unrecovered — an Unresolved gap.
- **Maxwell Street** — 1912 ordinance origin, 1994 demolition for UIC expansion, vendor collapse (1,200 → ~35) carried as Provisional.
- **63rd Street** — cross-referenced to PLACE-001 rather than re-researched.
- **Professional services** — Dickerson (law), Washington Wylie (accounting), Bailey and Moutoussamy (architecture), Black dentists, the Cook County Bar Association, and the Cosmopolitan Chamber (1933) form a recovered support-infrastructure layer. Several superlatives (Moutoussamy/Mies, Bailey commission, Washington Wylie's firm dates) were downgraded to Provisional on audit.
- **WVON** — Verified as the civil-rights broadcast force of the 1960s (Chess-era, MLK, Robert Kennedy); the electoral-causality claim (Harold Washington 1983 vs. the 1986 all-talk format) is now attributed and Provisional.
- **The "six-hour dollar"** — investigated and **debunked**: the figure traces to Brooke Stephens's 1996 book with no source, while the leakage campaigns it distorts (1929 Whip campaign, Double Duty Dollar, Operation Breadbasket) are documented.

## 8. What broke the cycle

The strongest breakdown finding: **"one-way economic desegregation."** White firms gained Black customers and agents while Black firms struggled to expand outward. Black-owned insurers reportedly fell from 50 (1962) to 23 (1992). The Depression killed the first-generation banks; the modern MDI contraction finished the job — Seaway's 2017 failure and Woodstock Institute's lending-gap research (28,000 fewer loans / $350M at parity, 2015–2017) document the capital infrastructure's erosion.

Succession was a structural weakness: Overton, Fuller, and Johnson Publishing did not sustain intergenerational control. Leak & Sons proves transfer was possible; the pattern is documented, the causes are not proven. Urban renewal (Maxwell Street, 1968 West Side destruction, failed rebuilding programs), the mentor-program promises of the Washington era, and the collapse of the policy economy's informal capital base each get documented treatment — with documented causes kept separate from plausible explanations throughout.

## What Survived

- **Leak & Sons Funeral Homes** — four generations of Black family ownership, still operating; the corpus's clearest case that intergenerational transfer was possible.
- **The Johnson Products brands** — Afro Sheen/Ultra Sheen returned to Black ownership in 2009 via a Black investment consortium.
- **WVON** — still Black-owned under Midway Broadcasting (Melody Spann-Cooper).
- **Leaders Network Credit Union** — Black pastors broke ground in 2023 (operating/charter status unverified; watched, not claimed).
- **The buildings** — the Jordan Building, Overton Hygienic Building, Regal Theater (landmark), and Supreme Life headquarters at 3501 S. King Drive stand as physical receipts of the ecosystem.
- **The press record** — the Defender archive, Half-Century's digitized issues, and the Business History Review study preserve what the firms themselves did not archive.

## What Disappeared

- **The banks**: Binga State Bank (1930), Douglass National (1932), and Seaway National Bank (2017) — three generations of Black banking, each ended by failure or takeover.
- **The insurers**: from ~50 Black-owned insurers in 1962 to 23 in 1992; Supreme Life itself taken over by the Illinois Department of Insurance in 1999.
- **The corridors as Black commercial centers**: Madison Street's 100+ destroyed businesses (1968); Maxwell Street demolished (1994); the Binga Block and Poro Block gone.
- **The conglomerates**: Overton's, Fuller's, Johnson Publishing's intergenerational control — all ended.
- **The policy economy's capital base** — the numbers game's informal capital flows, which once helped finance institutions, criminalized out of existence.

## What Changed

- **Capital sources flipped**: from Black-owned banks and insurance reserves to a near-total dependence on mainstream lenders whose gap the Woodstock Institute still measures in hundreds of millions.
- **Ownership form shifted**: from family conglomerates and fraternal finance to corporate acquisition (P&G), Black-led consortium buybacks, and the modern MDI/credit-union landscape.
- **The customer relationship inverted**: firms once protected by segregation now compete without its captive market — the one-way desegregation finding.
- **Memory infrastructure changed**: from company archives (mostly lost or never kept) to academic studies, digitized magazines, landmark reports, and oral-history projects doing the preservation work the firms didn't fund.

## What Remains Unknown

- Whether Chicago's mutual-aid societies ever operated susu-style rotating credit (none documented; kept Unresolved).
- The exact composition of Binga's startup capital and the precise siting of the Binga bank (3633 S. State vs. State & 36th Place).
- Named Black-owned firms on pre-1968 Madison Street.
- The fates of Supreme Life after 1962 (Puth's study ends there) and the $1.65 trillion figure's true value.
- The career sequence linking Binga's Arthur J. Wilson to Douglass National's signatory (likely the same man — unconfirmed).
- Whether Leaders Network Credit Union is operating.

## What Requires Preservation

- **The Defender's business archive and the company records that survive** — Fuller Products' papers, Poro's photographs (Western Historical Manuscript Collection), the NARA/DOCUMERICA street-vendor images: rights-checked and digitized before they scatter.
- **Supreme Life's corporate archive** — the best-documented Black insurer in America deserves a public archival home.
- **Oral histories of the ordinary firms** — the beauty schools, boarding houses, dressmakers, and caterers the written record barely names; the window is closing.
- **The buildings** — Jordan, Overton Hygienic, Regal, 3501 S. King Drive: landmarked or not, they are the ecosystem's surviving hardware.

## Limitations

This package is a web-research pass: no Defender archive access, no Sanborn/directory verification of addresses, no field verification, no oral histories, and no Abner (1962) recheck. Claims graded Provisional carry that fieldwork debt explicitly. Do not publish the Disputed or Unresolved items as fact; the package is built to prevent that.
