# Gaps — W1 Home & Family Life (CTL-DR-LIFE-001)

Domains or questions that could NOT be documented in this pass. Negative findings are recorded so future workers don't repeat the same searches.

## Birthdays (near-total gap)

- No Chicago-specific documentation of Black birthday celebrations was found: not for children's birthday parties, sweet sixteens, or milestone birthdays. Searched: "Black birthday traditions sweet sixteen African American history" — results were generic American sweet-sixteen explainers, not Chicago or Black-specific.
- **Where to look next:** Chicago Defender social pages (birthday announcements ran alongside wedding notices); Ebony/Jet (Johnson Publishing, Chicago-based) party features; church anniversary books; family photo collections at the DuSable Museum and Chicago History Museum.

## Dating and courtship (thin)

- Only two Chicago-specific items documented: Timuel Black's recollection of dating on 47th Street (Regal Theater) and the Regal as a venue. No documentation found of: how couples met (church? school? neighborhood?), parental rules, "going steady" customs, or dating across the 1940s–1980s generations.
- **Where to look next:** Defender society/personals columns; DuSable and Phillips yearbooks (dance photos, "most likely" pages); Studs Terkel interviews; oral histories at Chicago History Museum.

## Marriage (thin beyond announcements)

- Documented: Defender wedding/engagement announcements (1920s–1940s) and the jumping-the-broom tradition (national, not Chicago-specific). NOT found: Chicago-specific wedding customs (church wedding norms, reception practices, who catered, where receptions were held), elopement, or generational change in wedding scale (1940s church-basement receptions vs. 1980s hotel ballrooms).
- **Where to look next:** Defender archives (wedding write-ups with venues and caterers); Black-owned catering halls and churches; Ebony wedding features.

## Home decoration (no Chicago-specific sources)

- The "front room for company," plastic-covered furniture, doilies, and china-cabinet material is documented only in general Black-American or diasporic sources (K. Nicole Interiors 2026; McMillan 2003, UK context). Nothing Chicago-specific found: no catalogs, no advertisements for furniture stores serving Bronzeville, no photo essays of interiors.
- **Where to look next:** Sears catalogs (Chicago-based; homes and furnishings bought by Black families); Defender advertisements for furniture stores; Russell Lee's 1941 FSA-OWI photographs of Chicago interiors (Library of Congress); Ebony home features ("Ebony Showcase Home" series).

## School (yearbooks not accessed)

- DuSable's institutional history is well documented, but no school yearbook pages, student newspapers, or first-person student accounts were accessed. The 1937 "Red and Black" yearbook (Minnie Ward's copy, with inscriptions) exists in the antiquarian market — a promising primary artifact. Double-shift schooling in the 1940s rests on a single video synthesis; needs CPS records.
- **Where to look next:** DuSable and Wendell Phillips yearbooks; Chicago Public Schools archives; Defender education pages.

## Church Sunday across generations (asserted, not documented)

- The task asked for generational layering (e.g., church Sunday in the 1940s vs. 1980s). Not found: no source compared Sunday practices across decades in Chicago. Church hats and Easter dress are documented in present-day or national terms only.
- **Where to look next:** Church anniversary books (the single best source for generational comparison — they print founding-era vs. current photos and programs); oral histories with church mothers.

## Holidays (partial)

- Christmas: documented via Rollins's anthology and Great Migration homecoming accounts. Kwanzaa: documented, but the "Karamu developed in Chicago 1971" claim is single-sourced. Easter: national framing only. NOT found: Thanksgiving (no Chicago-specific Black Thanksgiving documentation), New Year's (watch-night services — a major Black church tradition — not yet researched), Juneteenth in Chicago (pre-2021 federal recognition observances not researched).
- **Where to look next:** Church bulletins for watch-night services; Defender holiday issues; Chicago Juneteenth histories.

## Funerals (institutional, not domestic)

- Documented: homegoing tradition, repast, two funeral homes. NOT found: the home-based side of mourning (sitting up with the body, funeral programs as family archives, the economics of burial insurance — though the Bronzeville video notes the ten-cent insurance collector and the "policy wheel," undocumented here).
- **Where to look next:** Funeral programs collections (often kept by families; some archived); Defender obituaries; burial insurance company records (Supreme Life, Chicago-based).

## Food/cooking (home kitchens thin)

- Documented: soul food's migration-era class politics (Poe 1999), Sunday dinner as institution. NOT found: Chicago-specific cookbooks by Black home cooks (searched briefly; none located), Defender food columns or recipes, or documentation of kitchen equipment in kitchenettes vs. later homes (icebox to refrigerator transition).
- **Where to look next:** Community/church cookbooks (fundraising cookbooks are a rich, underused source); Defender food pages; the "Date with a Dish" column in Ebony.

## Oral-history collections (not directly searched)

- Chicago History Museum, DuSable Museum of African American History, and the full Studs Terkel Radio Archive (WFMT) hold relevant interviews but were not directly searched in this pass — only web-indexed summaries were used. The Terkel–Rollins broadcast is archived at archive.org (popuparchive-1925328).

## W2 commerce & leisure
# Gaps — W2 Commerce & Leisure (CTL-DR-LIFE-001)

## Searched, found nothing usable

1. **Sears / Montgomery Ward catalogs and Black Chicago households.** Searched for catalog use in Black Chicago specifically. Found nothing beyond general knowledge that Sears and Ward were Chicago-founded mail-order houses. Whether Black Chicagoans ordered from catalogs, how mail order intersected with segregated retail, and any Black-specific catalog marketing remains undocumented in this pass. HIGH PRIORITY: Sears catalog archives are in the public domain (pre-1960) and digitized at the Internet Archive; a targeted review of whether and how the catalogs depicted Black consumers would fill this.

2. **Rent parties in Chicago.** A well-documented Harlem practice (charging admission to raise rent); searched for Chicago equivalents — found nothing Chicago-specific. Unknown whether house parties here served the same economic function. GAP.

3. **Black Chicago cruising practices, 1940s–1980s.** Searched "cruising culture King Drive 47th street Sunday cars 1960s 1970s" — results returned only one nostalgia video and the Timuel Black promenade quote. Sunday-evening drives, the "set" (e.g., 71st/79th Street scenes), and the car as status object in Black Chicago are not directly documented in what I found. The modern documented Chicago lowrider scene is heavily Latino/Chicano, so it is NOT evidence of Black Chicago car culture continuity. GAP — oral history needed.

4. **Jiffy Cab / Black-owned jitney cabs.** The ONLY source found is a TikTok creator's crowd-memory videos (2025–2026). No newspaper, municipal-license, or archival record surfaced. Kept as Provisional; the firm may be real and beloved but it is currently unverified in the documentary record. Needs: Defender archives, Chicago taxi commission records, oral history.

5. **Everyday grocery shopping across generations.** Black Metropolis (1945) documents 1930s grocery price/quality discrimination (consulted only at second hand). For the 1950s–80s — corner stores vs. chains (Jewel, A&P), Black-owned groceries specifically, the role of corner stores as credit/soda-fountain social spaces — I found only the Englewood "Corner Stores" documentary (1980s ownership transfer) and ProPublica's Madison Street memories (1960s). No Chicago-specific scholarship on the corner store as a Black social institution was found. GAP.

6. **House-party economics and nightlife outside venues/clubs.** The project brief explicitly warns against substituting venue histories for practice history. I found no Chicago-specific documentation of rent parties, social-club dances, "cabarets" (basement-party fundraisers), or the house-party-as-economic-institution. Meida McNeal's Chicago Black Social Culture Map likely documents this, but its archive was not consulted. GAP.

7. **Black Chicago fashion as everyday practice.** Found the Ebony Fashion Fair (top-down, aspirational) and the advertising scholarship. Did NOT find documentation of: "Sunday best" dressing practice, zoot suits in Chicago, tailoring (custom suits), the "steppers" dress code, or how working-class Black Chicagoans dressed for work vs. weekends. The Defender photo archives likely hold this; not consulted. GAP.

8. **Street games and children's play.** Not searched deeply; only the hydrant-sprinkling image from a nostalgia video. Chicago-specific games (e.g., 16-inch softball as the Black/white working-class game, "Cubs/Dubb" dice games, stoop culture) were not documented. GAP.

9. **Dining practices (not restaurant histories).** Documented soul-food restaurants as institutions (St. Rest 1972). Did NOT document: Sunday-dinner-at-home as a practice, church-anniversary dinners, repasts, fish fries, or where Black Chicagoans ate when traveling (Green Book lists exist but the practice of using them is thinly described). GAP.

10. **Vacation practices beyond Idlewild/Lake Ivanhoe.** The "driving down South for the summer" circuit (returning to family in the South), church-bus excursions, and day trips (e.g., Washington Park, the lakefront) are not documented here. GAP.

11. **Neighborhood sports below the high-school level.** Documented DuSable (1954) and the Chicago Public League lineage. Did NOT document: park-district leagues, 16-inch softball (a Chicago working-class staple), boxing gyms, playground culture, or girls'/women's neighborhood sports. GAP.

12. **Public transportation as daily practice.** Documented transit-as-boundary scholarship and 2024–25 rider complaints. Did NOT document: how Black Chicagoans actually used the el/buses daily in the 1940s–70s (commutes, "riding to the Loop" as a coming-of-age practice), the Green Line's role specifically, or CTA employment discrimination against Black drivers (only a passing mention in the Facing History oral history). GAP.

13. **Lake Ivanhoe corroboration.** One secondary source (Madison Times, 2025) names the three Chicago founders and the 1926 date. No second source found. Keep Provisional until corroborated.

## Middle-class bias notes (who is missing)

- The Green Book documents the Black MIDDLE class with cars and vacation money — working-class travel (buses, Greyhound, rideshares) is invisible in it.
- Ebony/Jet ads document consumers with disposable income; Ebony Fashion Fair served aspirational audiences.
- Johnson Products' story is entrepreneurial-elite history; the women using Ultra Sheen at home appear only as market segments, not voices.
- Barbershop/beauty-shop scholarship (Mills, Gill) is the best counterweight: it centers ordinary workers and their political speech.
- Nostalgia videos (South Side 1970s) capture working-class texture but without verifiable sourcing — used sparingly and flagged.

## Nostalgia-bias cautions logged

- Timuel Black's 47th Street memories are affectionate; the same era included the grocery exploitation Drake & Cayton documented.
- Idlewild's "Black Eden" framing must not erase that it declined when integration gave families options — its necessity was a product of exclusion.
- DuSable's 1954 team is remembered heroically; the same school later became a symbol of gang-violence crises — neither frame should be collapsed into the other.

## Contradictions (Disputed links)

### Afro pick invention/patent attribution — DISPUTED
- Account A: A'Lelia Bundles reported that Samuel H. Bundles Jr. and Henry M. Childrey developed a pick in 1969 for Summit Laboratories (via ND Insider, 2017-02-24).
- Account B: Anthony Romani's fist/peace-sign design was reportedly patented in the early 1970s (same reporting); MoMA's record for the 1972–1976 Antonio's Manufacturing pick lists Romani, Bundles, and Childrey together as designers.
- The two accounts cannot be collapsed into one inventor. **Needed:** retrieval of the actual patent record(s) from the USPTO.

### Hot comb inventor — DISPUTED
- The widespread conventional account credits Madam C.J. Walker (sometimes with a 1905 patent).
- NMAAHC's "Sizzle" essay explicitly states it is a misconception that Walker invented the hot comb, notes the exact origin is difficult to determine, documents U.S. commercial-catalog sales by 1886, and cites a Walter Sammons patent (1920) and a Clara Grant patent (1925).
- The conventional account is treated as a **refuted misattribution** (see relationships.json). **Needed:** the underlying Sammons and Grant patent documents; this is the batch's warning example against "first Black-owned" brand mythology.
