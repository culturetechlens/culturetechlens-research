## Missing-evidence register (track 2 — non-celebrity infrastructure)

### 1. Chess-era blues session personnel
- **What Is Missing:** Complete, session-level credits for the blues side of Chess (drummers, bassists, pianists on individual 1950s-60s dates); names like Fred Below and Lafayette Leake are canonical but were not verified against session logs in this research pass.
- **Why It Matters:** Blues session players are the most-cited yet least-documented Chicago music workers; without session logs their contributions remain anecdotal.
- **Where To Look Next:** Chess session discographies (e.g., Ruppli/Leadbitter logs), AFM union contracts, Blues Heaven Foundation archives, liner notes of the Chess 50th-anniversary reissues.

### 2. Women session musicians in the Chicago soul era (1960s-70s)
- **What Is Missing:** No named women instrumentalists were found in sources on Chicago soul sessions at Chess, Curtom, or One-derful!.
- **Why It Matters:** Deliberate emphasis of this track; the absence may reflect erasure rather than absence.
- **Where To Look Next:** Curtom session contracts, AFM Local 10-208 records, oral histories in the Chicago House Music Oral History Project and HistoryMakers.

### 3. Background singers on Chicago soul/blues sessions
- **What Is Missing:** Systematic credits for background vocalists on Chess/Curtom/One-derful! recordings; only isolated names (Shawn Christopher, Ricky Dillard) surfaced.
- **Why It Matters:** Background singing was a key paid role, disproportionately held by Black women, and is nearly invisible in Chicago session histories.
- **Where To Look Next:** Liner notes of Curtom and Brunswick reissues, union session sheets, gospel-to-pop crossover interviews.

### 4. Club workers: doormen, bartenders, coat check, security at the Warehouse, Music Box, and South/West Side clubs
- **What Is Missing:** No named individuals found for any non-DJ, non-owner club staff role in Chicago's Black music venues.
- **Why It Matters:** These workers shaped the door culture, safety, and social codes of the scenes; their absence is one of the starkest gaps.
- **Where To Look Next:** Oral histories (Chicago House Music Oral History Project), Chicago Defender nightlife columns, Robert Williams' planned memoir (5 Magazine project).

### 5. In-house sound technicians and lighting designers (beyond Richard Long)
- **What Is Missing:** Richard Long designed the Warehouse system, but the technicians who ran and maintained club sound and lighting night-to-night are unnamed in all sources consulted.
- **Why It Matters:** Sound and light were central to the Warehouse experience per the landmark report; the labor behind them is uncredited.
- **Where To Look Next:** Robert Williams memoir project, Richard Long & Associates business records, DJ oral histories.

### 6. Imports Etc. staff beyond the owner
- **What Is Missing:** Only owner Paul Weisburg and employee Chip E. are named; other clerks, buyers, and the staff who curated the 'As Heard At The Warehouse' bins are undocumented.
- **Why It Matters:** Record-store clerks were tastemakers who connected DJs to imports; their curation labor is invisible.
- **Where To Look Next:** Chicago house DJ oral histories, 1980s Chicago Reader/Defender ads, 'Beyond Heaven' flyer book acknowledgments.

### 7. Loop Records (Chicago) ownership and staff
- **What Is Missing:** Marshall Jefferson named Loop Records as a favorite 1980s-90s Chicago shop; no owner, staff, or address details were found.
- **Why It Matters:** A second key dance-music retail node besides Imports Etc. with no documented history.
- **Where To Look Next:** Marshall Jefferson interviews, Chicago DJ oral histories, 1980s-90s city directories.

### 8. Neighborhood record shops of the South and West Sides
- **What Is Missing:** A personal memoir names Joe's Records (35th & Giles) and Metro Music (87th & Harper) as formative Black-neighborhood shops; owners and staff are undocumented, and these are only two of likely dozens.
- **Why It Matters:** Neighborhood shops were the primary music access points for working-class Black Chicagoans; their history is almost entirely unrecorded.
- **Where To Look Next:** Chicago Defender advertisements, city business directories, HistoryMakers retail interviews, community memoir projects.

### 9. House party flyer/poster artists (1983-1989)
- **What Is Missing:** The book 'Beyond Heaven: Chicago House Party Flyers From 1983-1989' documents the visual archive, but individual designers of Chicago house flyers are unnamed in sources consulted.
- **Why It Matters:** Flyer art was the scene's visual infrastructure; the artists are uncredited.
- **Where To Look Next:** The 'Beyond Heaven' book credits/acknowledgments, Printed Matter archive, promoter collections (Robert Williams, Chosen Few).

### 10. Women promoters in early Chicago house
- **What Is Missing:** No women promoters were identified in sources on the 1977-1990 house scene; all named promoters (Williams, Risqué, Davis) are men.
- **Why It Matters:** Deliberate emphasis of this track; women's promotional and door labor likely existed but is undocumented.
- **Where To Look Next:** Chicago House Music Oral History Project (women's interviews), Defender social pages, Chosen Few picnic organizing history.

### 11. Women in ghetto house/juke/footwork production
- **What Is Missing:** No women producers or DJs were identified in the ghetto house/juke/footwork lineage covered (all named figures are men).
- **Why It Matters:** The scene's documented history is overwhelmingly male; women's participation is likely under-recorded.
- **Where To Look Next:** Teklife and Era Footwork Crew interviews, Chicago dance-battle video archives, WKKC/Kennedy-King radio archives.

### 12. Pressing-plant workers (historic)
- **What Is Missing:** The Chess pressing plant (320 E. 21st St., from 1968) is documented as a facility, but no press operators, foremen, or plant workers are named.
- **Why It Matters:** Working-class Black labor literally manufactured the records; the workforce is unnamed.
- **Where To Look Next:** AFM/union records, Chess payroll archives (if extant), former employee oral histories via Blues Heaven Foundation.

### 13. Black-owned independent distributors beyond United and Barney's
- **What Is Missing:** Only the Leaners' United Record Distributors and the Barney family operation were documented; the full roster of Black-owned Chicago distributors is incomplete.
- **Why It Matters:** Distribution was the bottleneck that determined which records reached stores and jukeboxes.
- **Where To Look Next:** Billboard 'soul' distributor listings 1960s-80s, Chicago Defender business pages, NARM archives.

### 14. AFM Chicago Black local: number and merger date
- **What Is Missing:** The Black local's number (reported elsewhere as Local 208) and exact merger date with the white local were not confirmed in sources consulted; Absher's book covers 1963-1967 desegregation but the finding aid level detail was not retrieved.
- **Why It Matters:** The union structured who could work which gigs; its segregated history is foundational labor context.
- **Where To Look Next:** AFM Local 10-208 archives, Absher 'The Black Musician and the White City' ch. 5, Chicago Defender labor coverage 1963-1967.

### 15. Church keyboardists who did secular session work
- **What Is Missing:** Beyond gospel stars, the church organists/pianists who crossed into R&B/soul session work are unnamed; the pipeline is asserted in general histories but not person-documented.
- **Why It Matters:** The church-to-studio pipeline is central to Chicago's sound; its workers are anonymous.
- **Where To Look Next:** Curtom and Brunswick session sheets, church musician union records, gospel oral histories (HistoryMakers clergy/musician interviews).

### 16. Steppers set participants and quarter-party hosts (1940s-1970s)
- **What Is Missing:** The basement/quarter-party hosts, social-club organizers, and instructors who transmitted steppin' before its commercial era are largely unnamed beyond Sam Chatman and Darrel Davis.
- **Why It Matters:** The dance survived through informal, unpaid community labor that histories compress into a few names.
- **Where To Look Next:** ilovesteppin.com community histories, World's Largest Steppers Competition organizers, Chicago Defender social pages.

### 17. South/West Side blues and soul club staff (bartenders, doormen, band bookers)
- **What Is Missing:** Venues like the Checkmate and High Chaparral appear in a 1968 account as live rooms, but no club workers or bookers are named.
- **Why It Matters:** The club circuit was the employment base for hundreds of musicians; its support staff is undocumented.
- **Where To Look Next:** Chicago Defender entertainment columns 1960s-70s, musician oral histories (e.g., John Primer interviews), Chicago Blues Archive.

### 18. Recording engineers beyond the famous few
- **What Is Missing:** Only Malcolm Chisholm and Stu Black were documented; engineers like Ron Malo (Chess) and Curtom's staff engineers (e.g., Roger Anfinsen, credited on 'Curtis in Chicago') were not researched to the verification standard.
- **Why It Matters:** Engineers shaped the Chicago sound as much as producers; most remain uncredited in histories.
- **Where To Look Next:** Album liner-note credits (Discogs), EARS (Engineering and Recording Society of Chicago) obituaries, Columbia College audio alumni records.

### 19. Payola-era economics for Black DJs
- **What Is Missing:** Absher documents Chess paying Al Benson ~$900/week, but the broader system of payments, record-shop kickbacks, and DJ-owned labels (Richard Pegue's Penny/Nickel, Richard Stamz's Fox/Foxy/Sign/Dawn) is only fragmentarily documented.
- **Why It Matters:** Payola narratives usually center white DJs (Alan Freed); the Black Chicago economy of it is under-studied.
- **Where To Look Next:** Absher ch. on payola, congressional payola hearing transcripts (1959-60), soul-source.co.uk DJ-label threads cross-checked against 45 label scans.

### 20. AIDS-era losses among Black queer club workers and DJs
- **What Is Missing:** Sources note queer DJs and promoters 'disappearing' in the late 1980s (iHeart 'But We Loved' podcast), but names, numbers, and the impact on scene infrastructure are not systematically documented.
- **Why It Matters:** The epidemic decimated the very community that built house music; the loss is acknowledged but not recorded.
- **Where To Look Next:** Chicago LGBTQ archives (Gerber/Hart), 'But We Loved' podcast transcripts, Chicago House Music Oral History Project.
