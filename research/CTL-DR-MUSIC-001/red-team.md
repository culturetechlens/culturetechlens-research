# Red Team — CTL-DR-MUSIC-001

Stress tests, mythology audit, contradictions, and rights guidance.

## Red-team findings (bias & integrity)

### 1. Celebrity bias: the Chess 'genius founder' narrative over-credits label owners and star soloists, under-credits the studio ecosystem
- **Detail:** Chess Records histories center Leonard Chess and a handful of stars (Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, Chuck Berry, Bo Diddley), while the sidemen, arrangers, producers, and house bands who made the records possible — Willie Dixon as songwriter/talent scout/bassist, Little Walter, Jimmy Rogers, Otis Spann, Hubert Sumlin, Fred Below — appear as supporting cast. Willie Dixon is routinely called 'the songwriter' for Chess hits, yet Leonard Chess was regularly accused of exploitative contracts and withheld royalties; Dixon, Waters, and Wolf all had to sue the Chess publishing arm (Arc Music) in the 1970s for royalties.
- **Example:** Willie Dixon wrote 'Hoochie Coochie Man,' 'I Just Want to Make Love to You,' 'Little Red Rooster,' 'Spoonful' — songs that became standards — but received little money until his manager audited Arc Music in the 1970s; Dixon then had to sue Arc and separately sue Led Zeppelin (settled 1987) for 'Whole Lotta Love'/'Bring It on Home' appropriation.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://www.bandmine.com/williedixon', 'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willie_Dixon', 'https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/chess-records-a-brief-history-of-chicagos-legendary-blues-label/', 'http://www.donaldclarkemusicbox.com/encyclopedia/detail.php?s=981']
- **Recommendation:** Rebalance the constellation: credit Dixon's full producer/scout/arranger role, name sidemen on landmark records, and frame Chess as a contested workplace — not a benevolent family. Note the 1977 Dixon/Waters suit against Arc Music and Dixon's Blues Heaven Foundation advocacy.

### 2. Missing women: Black women are narrated as vocalists, not as founders, executives, producers, or theorists of the music
- **Detail:** The standard Chicago story spotlights Mahalia Jackson (gospel) and Etta James / Koko Taylor (blues) as singular 'queens,' which flattens a dense field: Vivian Carter co-founded Vee-Jay (one of the most successful Black-owned labels in the US, Beatles' first American label) and her role is routinely eclipsed by Chess's story; gospel architects Albertina Walker, Sallie Martin, and the Caravans get footnotes; house's women DJs/producers (Lady D / Darlene Jackson, DJ Heather, Lori Branch, Colette) are largely absent from the Knuckles/Ron Hardy founder myth; no published history centers Chicago's Black women recording engineers.
- **Example:** Vee-Jay was founded in July 1953 by Vivian Carter and James Bracken with a $500 pawnbroker loan; at its peak it was bigger than Chess and for a time bigger than Motown, yet flagship Chicago narratives routinely treat Chess as the only label that matters.
- **Severity:** Critical
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://www.wcmu.org/music/2007-08-21/the-success-and-undoing-of-vee-jay-records', 'https://theartsdesk.com/node/72934', 'https://kansaspublicradio.org/npr-news/npr-news/2023-03-01/from-the-warehouse-to-the-world-chicago-and-the-birth-of-house-music']
- **Recommendation:** Give Vee-Jay/Vivian Carter a full node equal to Chess; build dedicated nodes for gospel women (Walker, Martin, Caravans), house women (Lady D, DJ Heather, Lori Branch), and mark the absence of documented women engineers as an explicit gap, not a settled fact.

### 3. Missing working-class and everyday history: the myth of sudden 'discovery' erases the labor economy that sustained the music
- **Detail:** Narratives frame artists as 'discovered' by labels (Muddy Waters 'found' at Stovall by Lomax; Chess 'discovering' bluesmen) while skipping the working-class infrastructure: rent parties, house parties, Maxwell Street busking economy, day jobs (Waters drove a truck; Koko Taylor cleaned houses for a decade before 'Wang Dang Doodle'). The Lomax/Library of Congress recordings at Stovall Plantation (1941-42) were field recordings of a working musician, not a 'discovery' of an unknown.
- **Example:** Koko Taylor moved to Chicago in 1953, worked as a domestic/housekeeper, and sang club gigs on the side for over a decade before Willie Dixon got her a Chess contract in 1964.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://dailyegyptian.com/51092/archives/koko-taylorstill-got-the-blues/', 'https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40222.pdf', 'https://producelikeapro.com/blog/muddy-waters/']
- **Recommendation:** Add an 'economy of the music' layer: rent parties, day jobs, the Maxwell Street informal economy, club circuits as workplaces. Retire 'discovery' language in favor of migration + labor.

### 4. Geographic bias: the South Side/Downtown Chess story eclipses the West Side Sound
- **Detail:** The constellation's gravitational center is usually the South Side (Chess at 2120 S. Michigan) and Maxwell Street, but a distinct, younger, louder electric blues movement grew on the West Side in the late 1950s around Cobra Records (1956-59) — Otis Rush, Magic Sam, Buddy Guy — the 'West Side Sound' that replaced horn sections with amplified lead guitar and became a building block of rock. Willie Dixon defected from Chess to Cobra as its artistic vision. Cobra folded in 1959 and its catalog was sold off, so it left a thinner archival trail.
- **Example:** Otis Rush's 'I Can't Quit You Baby' (1956, Cobra) spent six weeks on the Billboard R&B chart (peaked #6); Magic Sam's 'All Your Love' (1957) helped define the West Side Sound. Buddy Guy's first singles were on Cobra's Artistic subsidiary (1958).
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobra_Records', 'https://www.avclub.com/great-vintage-blues-19-magic-sam-1798214973', 'https://www.kwit.org/arts/2018-09-30/otis-rush-chicago-blues-legend-dies-at-84', 'https://inreviewonline.com/2015/05/01/west-side-soul/']
- **Recommendation:** Give Cobra Records and the West Side Sound first-class status — a full node, not a footnote. Map West Side clubs and address why Cobra's catalog fragmentation made it less remembered than Chess's.

### 5. Genre bias: jazz, soul, and R&B are flattened into a blues-to-rock pipeline
- **Detail:** The dominant 'constellation' frame runs blues -> rock, which sidelines: Chicago jazz (the AACM founded 1965; Nat King Cole Trio's Chicago years; Sun Ra's 1946-1961 Chicago period as arranger/pianist), Chicago soul (Vee-Jay, Curtis Mayfield and the Impressions, Jerry Butler, the Chi-Lites, Gene Chandler's 'Duke of Earl'), and gospel as a living tradition rather than a pre-history of soul. The Impressions came from Chicago's North Side (Uptown) — a geography the South Side frame misses entirely.
- **Example:** Sun Ra lived and worked in Chicago 1946-1961 (arranging for Fletcher Henderson, leading the Arkestra's earliest form) before Philadelphia/New York; he is almost never included in Chicago Black music histories despite being one of the city's most consequential musical exports.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Provisional
- **Sources:** ['https://www.wcmu.org/music/2007-08-21/the-success-and-undoing-of-vee-jay-records', 'https://digitalcommons.colum.edu/cbmr/']
- **Recommendation:** Build parallel genre tracks (jazz/AACM, soul/R&B, gospel) with their own origin logic rather than nesting everything under the blues. Verify Sun Ra's Chicago dates and the Impressions' North Side origins with dedicated sourcing before publication.

### 6. Survivorship bias: Chess's archive survived; its rivals' did not — and the archive shapes the canon
- **Detail:** What we 'know' about Chicago Black music is skewed toward labels whose masters survived. The Chess catalog survived largely intact (despite hundreds of thousands of records being destroyed in the 1975 dismantling of Chess, the master tapes survived). Vee-Jay's assets were seized, liened, and sold off after its 1966 bankruptcy; Cobra's catalog was bought by Stan Lewis and scattered across reissue labels. Independent gospel, juke-party, and club recordings from the 1940s-60s mostly vanished.
- **Example:** A review of an Etta James Chess collection notes gratitude 'that these recordings survived the destruction of hundreds of thousands of records during the dismantling of Chess Records in 1975. The master tapes survived.' No equivalent luck protected Vee-Jay's masters, which were leased piecemeal to Buddah and Springboard in the late 1960s-70s.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://washburnreview.org/21752/uncategorized/chess-recordings-of-etta-james-contain-her-best/', 'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vee-Jay_Records', 'https://alchetron.com/Vee-Jay-Records']
- **Recommendation:** State the survivorship bias explicitly on the page: 'the canon reflects what survived.' Treat thinly-documented labels and scenes as data gaps, not as evidence of lesser importance.

### 7. Disputed attribution inside the music: the 'Jimmy Reed sound' was Eddie Taylor's architecture
- **Detail:** Even within blues hagiography, credit is misallocated: the Delta-born guitarist Eddie Taylor — who learned from Charley Patton and Robert Johnson in Mississippi and arrived in Chicago around 1949 — described the so-called 'Jimmy Reed sound' as fundamentally his Mississippi Delta conception ('I learned to play guitar in Mississippi. I started in 1933'). Reed's name is on the records; Taylor's architecture is inside them.
- **Example:** Smithsonian Folkways liner notes quote Taylor: 'to me both [the Memphis sound and the Chicago sound] sound alike,' adding 'Muddy Waters still plays the same way he did down in Mississippi 20 years ago' — collapsing the clean Delta-to-Chicago transformation story.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40222.pdf']
- **Recommendation:** When naming signature sounds, name the uncredited architects (Taylor, Dixon as arranger, the Chess house band). Use Taylor's testimony to complicate the Delta-to-Chicago transformation narrative.

### 8. Misleading causal claim: 'Chicago invented the electric blues'
- **Detail:** The standard line — Muddy Waters plugged in and Chicago blues was born — is half true. Smithsonian Folkways scholarship shows the core components (including the electric band format and electric slide stylings like Elmore James's) were created, electrified, and recorded in Mississippi before arriving in Chicago; Muddy's 1947-48 Chess sides are 'basically identical' to his 1942 Lomax/Fisk Library of Congress recordings 'except for electricity.' What Chicago added was amplification scale, band format, club economy, and recording infrastructure — not the invention of the sound from nothing.
- **Example:** Folkways: 'If you compare the recordings Muddy Waters cut for Chess in Chicago in 1947-1948 with those he made for Alan Lomax and the Fisk University team in 1942, they are basically identical, except for electricity.'
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40222.pdf', 'https://www.classicalite.com/articles/1725284/20260331/chicago-blues-vs-delta-blues-raw-roots-electric-fire-whats-real-difference.htm']
- **Recommendation:** Reframe: 'Chicago electrified and industrialized the blues' rather than 'invented.' Credit the Mississippi electric experiments and name Elmore James's pre-Chicago electrification.

### 9. Misleading causal claim: 'Frankie Knuckles invented house music at the Warehouse'
- **Detail:** The clean origin myth — Knuckles at the Warehouse (1977-82), kids ask record stores for 'that Warehouse music,' genre named — conflates three different things: (1) a club culture and its name, (2) a DJ practice (disco/soul/funk/gospel re-edits), and (3) a recorded genre that did not exist until mid-1980s producers (Jesse Saunders, Vince Lawrence, Jamie Principle, Marshall Jefferson, Mr. Fingers) made tracks on drum machines. In the Warehouse's early years there was no such thing as a 'house record.' Multiple originators (Ron Hardy at the Music Box, Robert Williams as promoter, the Chosen Few DJs, South Side high-school party crews) are compressed into one hero.
- **Example:** A 2026 video essay notes: 'The Warehouse might be the birthplace of a culture and a name — but it was not, in its first years, the birthplace of a recorded genre. Those are two different crime scenes.' Chicago's own landmark report (209 S. Jefferson) is the strongest municipal evidence for the club's role.
- **Severity:** Critical
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/zlup/Historic_Preservation/Publications/The_Warehouse_Report.pdf', 'https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=976amYdW_l0', 'https://kansaspublicradio.org/npr-news/npr-news/2023-03-01/from-the-warehouse-to-the-world-chicago-and-the-birth-of-house-music']
- **Recommendation:** Separate the three births: the club/culture (Warehouse, 1977-82), the DJ practice (Knuckles, Hardy, Levan lineage), the recorded genre (1983-86, multiple producers). Name Robert Williams, Ron Hardy, Jesse Saunders, Vince Lawrence, Jamie Principle alongside Knuckles. Flag that Knuckles himself said he first saw the term 'house music' in a South Side record-store window circa 1980-81.

### 10. Misleading causal claim: the 'birthplace of gospel music' single-origin story
- **Detail:** Pilgrim Baptist Church (Bronzeville) is widely called 'the birthplace of gospel music' because Thomas A. Dorsey, hired as music director in the early 1930s, fused blues/jazz harmony with church song. But NPR Illinois/WTTW reporting gives a competing claim: Ebenezer Missionary Baptist Church (also Bronzeville) says Dorsey introduced his blend there and started the first gospel choir there. Gospel also has deep roots in southern spirituals, Holiness/Pentecostal churches, and quartet culture that predate Dorsey's 1930s work. The NMAAHC itself hedges: Pilgrim 'became known as the birthplace of gospel music' — a reputation, not a settled fact.
- **Example:** NPR Illinois (2023): 'The Ebenezer Missionary Baptist Church in Chicago's Bronzeville neighborhood... points out Thomas Dorsey, the father of gospel music, introduced his blend of Christian praise and blues at Ebenezer and started the first gospel choir there.'
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://www.nprillinois.org/illinois/2023-06-14/statewide-the-birthplace-of-gospel-music', 'https://nmaahc.si.edu/object/nmaahc_2015.134.1a-g', 'https://illinoisentertainer.com/2006/01/birthplace-of-gospel-music-destroyed/']
- **Recommendation:** Present both claims with attribution ('Pilgrim is widely called...; Ebenezer also claims...') and place Dorsey in a longer lineage (spirituals, quartets, Pentecostal music). Note the 2006 fire destroyed Pilgrim's building and likely Dorsey's original sheet music — the archive loss itself is part of the story.

### 11. Misleading causal claim: the blues were 'born in the Mississippi Delta'
- **Detail:** The popular Delta-birth story (Delta -> Chicago -> rock) is challenged by scholarship: Abbott and Seroff's 'The Original Blues' locates the blues' emergence in Black vaudeville and traveling tent-show circuits, not solely in Delta sharecropper isolation. Treating the Delta as the single womb erases the commercial, urban, theatrical circuits — many routed through Chicago — that shaped the music.
- **Example:** Summary of Abbott & Seroff: 'For a long time, that origin story was the prevailing popular notion about the birth of the blues. But it's all wrong, as recent scholarship has shown.'
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://gdestefano.ag-sites.net/attachments/Contrary_to_Popular_Belief.pdf', 'https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40222.pdf']
- **Recommendation:** Present the Delta account as the popular account and the vaudeville/tent-show account as the scholarly revision. Avoid 'birthplace' language; use 'formative region' / 'formative circuits.'

### 12. Anachronistic genre labels: 'house music' applied to 1977-81 Warehouse sets; 'rock and roll' applied retroactively to 1950s Chess
- **Detail:** Histories routinely describe Knuckles's late-1970s Warehouse sets as 'house music,' but the term and the recorded genre postdate the club's early years — Knuckles was playing disco, imports, and reworked soul/funk. Similarly, calling 1951-55 Chess sides 'rock and roll' projects a later commercial category backward; Chuck Berry and Bo Diddley were making Black R&B/race records that the industry later re-labeled. Anachronism makes contingent history look inevitable.
- **Example:** House: 'In its early years, the Warehouse wasn't playing house records. There was no such thing yet. It was playing disco, imports, reworked soul and funk cut up and extended for the dance floor' (2026 video essay). The landmark report confirms the club's 1977-82 dates.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=976amYdW_l0', 'https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/zlup/Historic_Preservation/Publications/The_Warehouse_Report.pdf']
- **Recommendation:** Date-label discipline: describe what was actually played/recorded at the time, and mark when the genre name appears (e.g., 'house' in record-store signage c. 1980-81). Avoid 'rock and roll' for pre-1955 Chess; use period terms (R&B, race records) with a note on re-labeling.

### 13. The British Invasion as the validation frame: Chicago blues narrated through white rock reception
- **Detail:** A common arc — Chess bluesmen languish, then the Rolling Stones / Yardbirds / Led Zeppelin / Cream 'discover' them and confer legitimacy — centers white reception as the moment the music becomes historically important. It also launders appropriation: Led Zeppelin's uncredited use of Willie Dixon's 'You Need Love' ('Whole Lotta Love') and 'Bring It on Home' required lawsuits (1972 Arc Music suit; Dixon's 1985 suit, settled 1987) before credit was granted.
- **Example:** Muddy Waters's famous 1981 quote — '[The Stones] didn't leave me standing out in the rain. They passed a quarter to me; gave me credit, you know?' — is routinely cited as absolution; Dixon's simultaneous litigation tells a different story.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://opentext.uoregon.edu/payforplay/chapter/chapter-36-landmark-musical-work-copyright-infringement-cases/', 'https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/chess-records-a-brief-history-of-chicagos-legendary-blues-label/', 'http://ledzepnews.com/2025/04/12/the-previously-untold-history-of-willie-dixons-legal-battle-with-led-zeppelin-over-whole-lotta-love/']
- **Recommendation:** Decenter the validation frame: lead with the Black audience and Black press reception (Chicago Defender, WVON), treat the British reception as a second chapter, and name the plagiarism suits as part of the constellation's legal history.

### 14. Maxwell Street romanticism: 'birthplace of Chicago blues' vs. a demolished working market
- **Detail:** Maxwell Street is mythologized as the blues' open-air cradle (Muddy Waters busking, Koko Taylor watching Sunday jam sessions). The romantic version obscures: (a) the market was a multi-ethnic immigrant commercial district first; (b) its destruction was a policy choice — declared 'blighted' in 1966, eminent domain for UIC expansion, market moved 1994, final buildings demolished 2002, replaced by University Village; (c) the 'blues cradle' framing was partly constructed after the fact by preservation campaigns. Koko Taylor's own testimony ('When they tore down Maxwell Street I felt sad. All of the history was lost and erased') is grief, not a birth certificate.
- **Example:** City Cast Chicago (2026): Dan Ryan Expressway (1957) bisected Maxwell Street; UIC opened 1965; the street was deemed 'a slum and blighted' in 1966; UIC got eminent-domain permission in 1967; historic-district nominations were rejected twice; final buildings fell in 2002.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://chicago.citycast.fm/explainers/maxwell-street-decline', 'https://www.britannica.com/topic/Maxwell-Street-Market', 'https://dailyegyptian.com/51092/archives/koko-taylorstill-got-the-blues/']
- **Recommendation:** Tell Maxwell Street as an urban-renewal story with music inside it, not a music shrine story. Name the displacement (14,000 residents displaced per community accounts; verify against UIC/city records) and the failed preservation bids.

### 15. Disco Demolition Night (1979) as a false rupture: 'disco died, house was born'
- **Detail:** The tidy causality — the Comiskey Park 'Disco Demolition' riot killed disco, and house rose from its ashes — is chronologically and culturally wrong. Disco continued in Black and gay clubs; Knuckles called house 'disco's revenge,' i.e., continuity, not replacement. The demolition narrative centers a white rock-radio stunt as the hinge of Black dance-music history.
- **Example:** DJ Lady D: 'The roots of House come from disco... Frankie Knuckles actually popularized that statement, that House music is disco's revenge.'
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://getpodcast.com/podcast/the-rundown-chicago-news/why-the-roots-of-house-come-from-disco-dj-lady-d-on-40-years-of-the-ge_5505cda044', 'https://kansaspublicradio.org/npr-news/npr-news/2023-03-01/from-the-warehouse-to-the-world-chicago-and-the-birth-of-house-music']
- **Recommendation:** Frame Disco Demolition as a racist/industry event that house artists routed around, not as house's cause. Use 'disco's revenge' as the continuity frame.

### 16. Cadillac Records (2008) and similar dramatizations are treated as history
- **Detail:** The film 'Cadillac Records' — and much popular Chess lore — takes documented creative liberties (composite characters, invented scenes, Beyonce-as-Etta James framing) that then circulate as fact in casual histories and even some journalism. The 'Cadillac as reward' anecdote is real in outline but the film's race-and-business simplifications mislead.
- **Example:** A 2026 commentary notes the film 'takes creative liberties with history to tell a more compelling story about race, culture, and the cost of ambition' — liberties that then get repeated as the Chess story.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nu34ySB0Naw']
- **Recommendation:** Never source facts to the film. Where the page references 'Cadillac Records,' label it explicitly as dramatization and cross-check each claim against label discographies and court records.

### 17. The 'first house record' dispute is unsettled and politically loaded
- **Detail:** Jesse Saunders's 'On and On' (Jes Say, Jan 1984) is 'sometimes cited as the first house record,' but J.M. Silk's 'Music Is the Key' (1985), Z-Factor's 'Fantasy' (recorded 1983, released March 1984 on Mitchbal), and Jamie Principle's cassette-circulated 'Your Love'/'Waiting on My Angel' are rival candidates. Saunders has publicly disputed rival claims (e.g., against Farley Jackmaster Funk's 2021 account); his own brother Wayne Williams (Chosen Few DJs) has publicly questioned Saunders's version. 'First' depends on definitions: first pressed to vinyl vs. first produced vs. first circulated on cassette in clubs.
- **Example:** Insomniac: 'Even in 1986, Saunders was something of a legend... responsible for the record many consider to be the first house cut ever committed to vinyl: 1984's On and On. (Plenty of other house tracks had already been produced — Jamie Principle's Your Love, for instance — but they had circulated through the clubs via cassette tapes rather than vinyl.)'
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://www.insomniac.com/?p=85397', 'https://www.energyflashrecords.co.uk/catalogue/title.phtml?product_id=138108', 'http://music.hyperreal.org/library/history_of_house.html', 'https://earstothehouse.com/2021/11/04/on-and-on-indeed-part-two-after-jesse-saunders-takes-issue-with-claim-farley-jackmaster-funk-was-the-first-to-play-early-house-records-now-chosen-few-djs-founder-wayne-williams-has-a-few-things-to/']
- **Recommendation:** Do not crown a 'first.' Present the candidate records, the criteria (vinyl vs. cassette vs. club play), and the family dispute as the story — the contest itself is historically informative about how canons form.

### 18. Footwork/juke is cut out of the Chicago electronic lineage
- **Detail:** Chicago's Black electronic story is usually told as house (1980s) -> EDM (2010s), skipping footwork/juke — the Black South/West Side battle-dance and production culture pioneered by RP Boo (credited creator of footwork), DJ Rashad, DJ Spinn, and the Teklife crew in the 1990s-2000s. Footwork is arguably Chicago's most globally influential 21st-century Black music export, yet it rarely appears in 'Chicago music history.'
- **Example:** The constellation's house chapter typically ends with 1990s commercialization; footwork's 2000s-2010s arc (Teklife, Planet Mu releases, 'Double Cup' era) is absent from most civic narratives.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Provisional
- **Recommendation:** Add a footwork node with RP Boo as credited originator; verify dates and the Battle Groundz/battle-culture documentation before publication. Treat footwork as the continuation of the house lineage, not a separate genre.

### 19. The 'Godfather of House' title compresses a collective, queer Black and Latino scene into one man
- **Detail:** Frankie Knuckles is universally titled 'Godfather of House,' which is deserved as honorific but misleading as history: the Warehouse was built by promoter Robert Williams (who first offered the residency to Larry Levan), sustained by Black gay men and Latino dancers, sonically shaped by the Levan-Knuckles partnership forged at the Continental Baths, and continued after Knuckles by Ron Hardy at the Music Box. The landmark report itself documents Williams's role and Levan's refusal. Queer-of-color congregation — the scene's actual social form, per Salkind's oral histories — disappears when the story becomes one man's genius.
- **Example:** Chicago landmark report: Williams sought Levan first; Levan recommended Knuckles; Knuckles arrived March 1977. The club's crowd was 'Black, Latino, largely gay.'
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/zlup/Historic_Preservation/Publications/The_Warehouse_Report.pdf', 'https://digitalcommons.colum.edu/house/index.12.html']
- **Recommendation:** Keep the honorific but distribute the credit: Williams (founder/promoter), Levan (the road not taken / sonic co-parent), Hardy (successor), the dancers (the social form). Cite Salkind's 'Do You Remember House?' oral histories for the queer-of-color frame.

### 20. Chicago Defender and Black press infrastructure under-credited as music-historical actors
- **Detail:** The Chicago Defender (and later WVON radio, DJs like Herb Kent 'The Cool Gent,' Don Cornelius pre-Soul Train) were not just observers but infrastructure: the Defender's Great Migration editorials pulled musicians north; its entertainment pages made or broke club acts; WVON ('The Voice of the Negro') was the national R&B tastemaker. Music histories treat press/radio as background.
- **Example:** WVON launched in 1963 as a Black-oriented station and became a national R&B hitmaking force; its DJs (Herb Kent, Lucky Lockett) shaped the steppers/soul canon — yet standard Chicago music timelines jump from Chess to house with no radio chapter.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Provisional
- **Recommendation:** Add a media-infrastructure node: Defender, WVON, key DJs (Kent, Cornelius, E. Rodney Jones). Verify WVON launch details and DJ tenures before publication.

## Mythology audit (track 4)

### 1. Frankie Knuckles single-handedly invented house music.
- **Repeated By:** Popular obituaries and shorthand histories; the City of Chicago Warehouse landmark report's broad 'Knuckles created house' framing.
- **Evidence Shows:** House emerged collectively: Knuckles's Warehouse residency (1977–82) and Power Plant (1983), Ron Hardy's Music Box (1983), Robert Williams's promotion, the WBMX Hot Mix 5's radio diffusion, and producers Saunders/Lawrence, Principle, Jefferson, Heard, and Phuture. Even the City of Chicago's Warehouse landmark report — a strong source — lapses into the singular 'Knuckles created house' shorthand, which is precisely the oversimplification to avoid. Resident Advisor describes house origins as 'steeped in mythology and confusion.'
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** City of Chicago Warehouse landmark report; Resident Advisor (ra.co/news/10915); Red Bull Music Academy (2015-05).

### 2. 'On and On' (Jesse Saunders, 1984) was the first house record, full stop.
- **Repeated By:** Many house histories and listicles; Resident Advisor's 'generally accepted' formulation is careful, but it is routinely flattened into an unqualified first.
- **Evidence Shows:** The defensible claim is narrower: 'On and On' is generally accepted as the first house track committed to vinyl (early 1984). But Jamie Principle's 'Your Love' circulated on cassette in Chicago clubs before its April 1986 vinyl release — i.e., it was heard as house music before 'On and On' was pressed. 'On and On' itself was based partly on a Mach 'On & On' bootleg megamix. 'First' depends on whether one means first created, first circulated on tape, first club-tested, or first pressed to vinyl.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** Resident Advisor; Wikipedia 'On & On (Jesse Saunders song)'; Wikipedia 'Your Love (Jamie Principle song)'.

### 3. Phuture's 'Acid Tracks' was the first acid house record.
- **Repeated By:** Popular acid-house histories and anniversary coverage.
- **Evidence Shows:** 'Acid Tracks' was created and club-tested by Ron Hardy before its 1987 pressing, but Sleezy D/Adonis/Marshall Jefferson's 'I've Lost Control' appeared on vinyl in 1986 — a year earlier. Whether 'first created/tested' or 'first released on vinyl' is meant changes the answer; the unqualified claim fails on the vinyl criterion.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** MusicRadar (acid house history); Georgia Entertainment (2017-02).

### 4. Vee-Jay Records was America's first Black-owned record label.
- **Repeated By:** Some label profiles and casual histories of Vee-Jay.
- **Evidence Shows:** Vee-Jay was founded in Chicago in 1953 by Vivian Carter and Jimmy Bracken — a major Black-owned label, but not the first. Harry Pace's Black Swan Records (Pace Phonographic Corporation) dates to 1921, and Chicago's Black Patti label dates to 1927. Any 'first' claim must specify scope (e.g., first with sustained national distribution, which itself needs separate sourcing).
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** signaturesoundsonline.com (Vee-Jay history); harlemworldmagazine.com (Black Swan history).

### 5. Thomas A. Dorsey invented gospel music.
- **Repeated By:** Obituaries, textbooks, and popular summaries that compress 'father of gospel music' into invention.
- **Evidence Shows:** Dorsey is best credited — per the New Georgia Encyclopedia — with developing, publishing, and promoting gospel blues, and with institutionalizing it (Pilgrim Baptist music director 1932–late 1970s; NCGCC co-founder 1933). Black sacred music traditions long predate him. 'Father of gospel music' is an honorific for his gospel-blues role, not a literal invention claim.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** New Georgia Encyclopedia; Michael W. Harris, 'The Rise of Gospel Blues'.

### 6. Led Zeppelin were found guilty in court of stealing 'Whole Lotta Love' from Willie Dixon.
- **Repeated By:** Casual retellings of the Zeppelin–Dixon dispute.
- **Evidence Shows:** Dixon sued in 1985 over 'Whole Lotta Love' (derived from his 'You Need Love,' recorded by Muddy Waters in 1962). The case settled out of court on February 5, 1987; it never reached a final hearing, so no court ever adjudicated plagiarism. A settlement is not a guilty verdict.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** Vaidhyanathan, Penn State legal history (PDF); ledzepnews.com (2025-04-12).

### 7. Disco Demolition Night (July 12, 1979, Comiskey Park) killed disco.
- **Repeated By:** Popular memory and anniversary coverage of 'the night disco died.'
- **Evidence Shows:** The Comiskey Park event — records destroyed, field invasion, forfeited second game — was a real and symbolic backlash, but Britannica notes disco went underground and was absorbed into pop rather than literally dying. Retrospective commentary links the backlash to disco's Black, Latino, and LGBTQ constituencies, which Dahl and Veeck dispute — the meaning of the night is itself contested.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** Chicago Magazine (Dec 2020); Britannica ('Disco Demolition Night').

### 8. Disco Demolition directly caused the creation of house music.
- **Repeated By:** Some retrospective features drawing a straight line from Comiskey Park to the Warehouse.
- **Evidence Shows:** House was already developing at the Warehouse (1977–82) before July 1979, so a single 1979 event cannot be its direct cause. The subtler claim — that the disco backlash pushed dance music underground and created conditions house exploited — is plausible but not the same as direct causation, and the evidence supports context, not a causal chain from one night.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** City of Chicago Warehouse report (1977–82 timeline); Britannica.

### 9. Acid house got its name from LSD.
- **Repeated By:** Folk etymology in casual histories and some press coverage.
- **Evidence Shows:** The name attached to the TB-303's squelching sound and to track titling ('Acid Tracks'), but the LSD etymology is folk etymology without solid contemporary documentation located in this research. The drug association reads as retrospective projection onto the sound's psychedelic quality. Stronger primary sourcing (contemporary interviews on the naming) is still needed.
- **Verdict:** unresolved
- **Sources:** Georgia Entertainment (2017-02); MusicRadar — both document the TB-303 sound origin, neither substantiates the LSD naming claim.

### 10. Trax Records was a legitimate, artist-friendly independent label.
- **Repeated By:** Label nostalgia and uncritical histories of the Chicago house canon.
- **Evidence Shows:** In 2022 Vince Lawrence, Marshall Jefferson, Adonis, Maurice Joshua and others sued Trax alleging unpaid royalties, forged signatures, bounced checks, and poor accounting; DJ Mag's history notes longstanding complaints of missing contracts and poor vinyl quality. These are allegations (outcome not established in sources reviewed), but they contradict any uncritical 'artist-friendly' framing.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** DJ Disc Jockey (2022); DJ Mag (2020); GO BANG Magazine (2020-04-09).

### 11. DJ International Records was founded solely by Rocky Jones.
- **Repeated By:** Some histories crediting Jones alone.
- **Evidence Shows:** DJ International was co-founded in 1985 by Rocky Jones and Benji Espinoza; Jones's own quoted account describes the label's start as a record pool linking working DJs and labels. Single-founder accounts erase Espinoza.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** DJ Mag (Espinoza obituary; Jones account).

### 12. Katrin Quinol sang Black Box's 'Ride on Time.'
- **Repeated By:** Viewers of televised/mimed performances; casual credits.
- **Evidence Shows:** 'Ride on Time' was built on an uncleared sample of Loleatta Holloway's 'Love Sensation'; Holloway sued. Quinol, a model, mimed the vocal in public performances, creating a false public credit. Treating her as the singer — or the sample as an authorized feature — is contradicted by the record.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** NME (30-year 'Ride on Time' history).

### 13. 'Your Love' was originally a Frankie Knuckles record.
- **Repeated By:** The 1987 Trax Records credit and histories repeating it uncritically.
- **Evidence Shows:** 'Your Love' is Jamie Principle's song; it circulated on cassette in clubs before any vinyl release, and the 1986 Persona pressing was Principle's. The 1987 Trax version credited to Knuckles is the contested artifact — Principle alleged in 2014 that Trax 'literally just stole my stuff.' The Knuckles credit reflects label practice, not authorship.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** Wikipedia 'Your Love (Jamie Principle song)'.

### 14. House music was named after the Warehouse because the music was invented there.
- **Repeated By:** Shorthand histories compressing the naming story into an origin story.
- **Evidence Shows:** The name does derive from the Warehouse — that part is sound. But the claim smuggles in sole invention at one address: the sound developed across the Music Box, the Power Plant, WBMX radio, and producers' studios, and the Warehouse's own address history (206 S. Jefferson) is distinct from the Music Box sites. Naming and sole invention are different claims; only the first is supported.
- **Verdict:** disputed
- **Sources:** City of Chicago Warehouse report; Red Bull Music Academy (2015-05).

### 15. Mahalia Jackson's shout — 'Tell them about the dream, Martin!' — single-handedly created the 'I Have a Dream' speech.
- **Repeated By:** Popular retellings of the March on Washington.
- **Evidence Shows:** Jackson did call out the line during King's August 28, 1963 speech (WFMT; All About Jazz), and King's adviser Clarence B. Jones affirmed the celebrated passages were delivered extemporaneously. But King had used 'dream' language in earlier sermons, and multiple factors shaped the speech; the single-cause anecdote outruns the evidence. The shout is well-attested; sole causation is not.
- **Verdict:** provisional
- **Sources:** WFMT (2017-01-11); All About Jazz; mahalia-jackson.com.

## Contradictions register

### 1. When did Aristocrat become Chess Records — 1948 or 1950?
- **Detail:** EBSCO's Muddy Waters research starter says 'Aristocrat changed its name to Chess Records in 1948, and shortly thereafter released Waters's signature hit Rollin' Stone (1950).' AllMusic's 1948-1950 compilation notes say the sessions run 'up through February 1950, when Aristocrat was renamed Chess Records.' Both cannot be right; the AllMusic/February-1950 dating is the discographically standard one.
- **Example:** EBSCO: renamed 1948. AllMusic: renamed February 1950. The 'Rollin' Stone' Chess 1426 release sits at the hinge, dated 1950 by both.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/muddy-waters', 'https://www.allmusic.com/album/1948-1950-mw0000660715']
- **Recommendation:** Use 'Aristocrat became Chess in 1950 (some sources say 1948; discographical consensus is February 1950).' Do not present either date as settled without the note.

### 2. Was 'Rollin' Stone' the first Chess release or the second?
- **Detail:** AllMusic calls 'Rollin' Stone' (Chess 1426, recorded February 1950) 'Muddy's first Chess recording.' A fan discography (loungecore) calls it 'the second release for new label' after the Aristocrat-to-Chess transition. The discrepancy is definitional: first recording session under the Chess name vs. first catalog number issued.
- **Example:** AllMusic: 'Muddy's first Chess recording was a powerful solo performance entitled Rollin' Stone. Issued as Chess 1426 with Walkin' Blues on the flip side.'
- **Severity:** Low
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://www.allmusic.com/album/1948-1950-mw0000660715', 'http://www.loungecore.com/blog/muddy-waters-rollin-stone']
- **Recommendation:** Say 'among the first Chess releases (Chess 1426, 1950)' and avoid the ordinal unless citing a discography.

### 3. Muddy Waters's birth year: 1913 vs. 1915
- **Detail:** Most biographical sources give April 4, 1913 (Issaquena County, Mississippi); some sources — including the search-result corpus here — give 1915. The 1915 date appears in older reference works and some journalism; 1913 is the current scholarly consensus.
- **Example:** Music Connection's Chess reissue coverage gives 'Born McKinley Morganfield in Mississippi in 1915'; standard references give 1913.
- **Severity:** Low
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://www.musicconnection.com/chess-records-ume-releases-muddy-waters-the-best-of-muddy-waters-and-howlin-wolfs-moanin-in-the-moonlight/']
- **Recommendation:** Use 1913 with a note ('some older sources give 1915').

### 4. 'Birthplace of gospel': Pilgrim Baptist vs. Ebenezer Baptist
- **Detail:** The NMAAHC and most civic sources call Pilgrim Baptist 'known as the birthplace of gospel music' via Dorsey's 1930s tenure. NPR Illinois (2023), reporting on Ebenezer Missionary Baptist Church, says Ebenezer claims Dorsey 'introduced his blend of Christian praise and blues at Ebenezer and started the first gospel choir there.' Both churches are in Bronzeville; Dorsey worked across churches.
- **Example:** NMAAHC: 'With Dorsey as its music director, the church became known as the birthplace of gospel music.' NPR Illinois: Ebenezer 'points out Thomas Dorsey... started the first gospel choir there.'
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://nmaahc.si.edu/object/nmaahc_2015.134.1a-g', 'https://www.nprillinois.org/illinois/2023-06-14/statewide-the-birthplace-of-gospel-music']
- **Recommendation:** Present both claims with attribution; do not let the page pick a winner. Note Dorsey's peripatetic church work as the likely reason both claims exist.

### 5. 'First house record': 'On and On' (1984) vs. 'Fantasy' (1983/84) vs. 'Music Is the Key' (1985) vs. cassette-only tracks
- **Detail:** Jesse Saunders's 'On and On' (Jes Say, January 1984) is 'sometimes cited as the first house record'; Energy Flash notes J.M. Silk's 'Music Is the Key' (1985) is also cited; the Hyperreal history argues Z-Factor's 'Fantasy' (recorded 1983, Mitchbal, released March 1984) is probably first to vinyl; Jamie Principle's 'Your Love' existed earlier but circulated on cassette, not vinyl. Saunders himself has publicly contested rivals' accounts, and his brother Wayne Williams has publicly questioned Saunders's.
- **Example:** Four credible 'firsts' depending on whether the criterion is first produced, first pressed to vinyl, or first heard in clubs.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Disputed
- **Sources:** ['https://www.energyflashrecords.co.uk/catalogue/title.phtml?product_id=138108', 'http://music.hyperreal.org/library/history_of_house.html', 'https://www.insomniac.com/?p=85397', 'https://earstothehouse.com/2021/11/04/on-and-on-indeed-part-two-after-jesse-saunders-takes-issue-with-claim-farley-jackmaster-funk-was-the-first-to-play-early-house-records-now-chosen-few-djs-founder-wayne-williams-has-a-few-things-to/']
- **Recommendation:** Present the candidates and the criteria; make the dispute itself a page feature ('The fight over the first record is part of the history').

### 6. Origin of the term 'house music': the Warehouse vs. Leonard 'Remix' Rroy's tavern sign
- **Detail:** The consensus account (supported by Chicago's landmark report) derives 'house' from the Warehouse. ### 7. Vee-Jay's bankruptcy date: May vs. August 1966

## Rights & copyright guidance (risk-aware, not legal advice)

### 1. GREEN — Facts, dates, names, and ideas are free to use
- **Detail:** Copyright does not protect facts, dates, names, titles, slogans, or short phrases (U.S. Copyright Office Circular 1). The flagship page can freely state that Muddy Waters recorded 'Rollin' Stone' for Chess in 1950, that the Warehouse stood at 206 S. Jefferson, or that Vee-Jay was founded in 1953 — no permission needed. What must be avoided is copying another author's expressive presentation (their sentences, their narrative structure) of those facts.
- **Example:** Safe: 'Cobra Records operated on Chicago's West Side from 1956 to 1959 and launched Otis Rush, Magic Sam, and Buddy Guy.' Not safe: lifting a paragraph of a published history's prose about Cobra.
- **Severity:** Low
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Wikilegal/Fair_use_protections_for_posting_music_lyric_excerpts_and_translations']
- **Recommendation:** Write all narrative prose originally; cite sources for facts but never paste their sentences. This is the single largest risk-reducer for the page build.

### 2. GREEN — Short attributed quotations for criticism/commentary (fair use, 17 U.S.C. 107)
- **Detail:** Brief quotations used for purposes of criticism, comment, scholarship, or review can be fair use under the four-factor test (purpose, nature, amount, market effect). One or two lines of lyrics or a sentence from an interview, clearly attributed and directly discussed, is the classic low-risk pattern — especially where the use is transformative (analysis, not decoration). There are no bright-line word counts; fair use is case-by-case.
- **Example:** Quoting Eddie Taylor's 'I learned to play guitar in Mississippi. I started in 1933' to analyze the Delta-to-Chicago narrative is textbook commentary fair use. Quoting a full verse of 'Hoochie Coochie Man' as a chapter epigraph is not.
- **Severity:** Low
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Wikilegal/Fair_use_protections_for_posting_music_lyric_excerpts_and_translations', 'https://www.writersdigest.com/business-legal-matters/fair-use-legal-lingo-you-can-understand']
- **Recommendation:** Rule of thumb for the build: quote only what you directly analyze; attribute every quote; keep lyric quotes to a line or two. When in doubt, paraphrase the idea and cite.

### 3. YELLOW — Song lyrics beyond fair use need licensing
- **Detail:** Lyrics are independently copyrighted as literary works (ABKCO Music v. Stellar Records, 2d Cir. 1996). Songs are short creative works, so even a line or two can be a 'generous portion' under fair-use factors two and three, and the music industry actively licenses lyric reprints (fees commonly cited $25-$250+ per use via publishers). A lyrics-heavy flagship page (full verses, choruses as decoration) would need permission from publishers — for Chess material, largely the Universal Music Group / Arc Music successor catalogs.
- **Example:** Printing the full lyrics of 'Mannish Boy' or 'Wang Dang Doodle' on the page = license required. Quoting 'everything gonna be alright this mornin'' inside a paragraph analyzing Muddy Waters's vocal phrasing = likely fair use.
- **Severity:** High
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Wikilegal/Fair_use_protections_for_posting_music_lyric_excerpts_and_translations', 'https://www.writersdigest.com/business-legal-matters/fair-use-legal-lingo-you-can-understand']
- **Recommendation:** Design the page to be lyric-light: paraphrase and analyze; quote at most 1-2 lines per song with attribution. Budget for a licensing line-item if the design requires more. Never reproduce full lyrics.

### 4. RED — Audio recordings: do not host audio files; embed from licensed services
- **Detail:** Sound recordings carry their own copyright, separate from the composition. Pre-1972 U.S. recordings were under state law until the 2018 Music Modernization Act's CLASSICS Act, which federalized them with a staggered public-domain schedule (pre-1923 recordings entered the public domain in 2022; 1923-1946 recordings follow on a rolling schedule through 2067). Nearly all Chicago blues/soul/house master recordings (Chess, Vee-Jay, Trax, DJ International) remain under copyright. Hosting MP3s or clips on CTL infrastructure = direct infringement exposure.
- **Example:** A 30-second clip of 'I Can't Quit You Baby' (Cobra, 1956) hosted on the CTL site would infringe the sound-recording copyright (now administered through the Jewel/Paula successor catalog) regardless of any fair-use argument about the composition.
- **Severity:** Critical
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Recommendation:** Never host audio. Use embeds from licensed platforms (YouTube, Spotify, Apple Music embeds) which carry the platform's licenses. For pre-1923 recordings only, public-domain audio may be usable — verify each recording's date and consult counsel.

### 5. RED — Photographs with unknown provenance must not be published
- **Detail:** Every photograph has a copyright holder (usually the photographer or their estate/employer), independent of who owns a print. 'Found on the internet' is not a license. The Chicago Defender photo morgue, club flyers, and fan-taken club photos are the highest-risk categories: orphaned works with no clear owner. Note that lack of a copyright notice on older photos does not automatically mean public domain — it depends on publication date, registration, and renewal under the pre-1978 regime.
- **Example:** A widely-circulated photo of the Warehouse dance floor with no credit line cannot be safely published; the photographer (or their heir) could surface. By contrast, the Raeburn Flerlage archive (45,000+ images) is held by the Chicago History Museum with a clear licensing desk.
- **Severity:** Critical
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://www.beachwoodreporter.com/music/chicago_history_museum_makes_t/']
- **Recommendation:** Build the page's visual program from licensable sources only: Chicago History Museum (Flerlage archive and others), NMAAHC/Smithsonian Open Access, Library of Congress, Wikimedia Commons images with verified PD/CC licenses. For each image, record: source, rights statement, credit line. Reject any image whose provenance cannot be documented.

### 6. YELLOW — Thumbnails and reduced-size images for commentary
- **Detail:** Using reduced-size, low-resolution images specifically to illustrate critical commentary can weigh toward fair use (cf. the Ninth Circuit's Kelly v. Arriba Soft on thumbnails), but this is a balancing test, not a safe harbor — and it does not cover decorative hero images or galleries. Rights holders for music photography (estates, Getty, Corbis successors) are commercially active licensors.
- **Example:** A small Flerlage photo of Howlin' Wolf at Pepper's Lounge, directly discussed in a paragraph about West Side club culture, is a stronger fair-use posture than the same photo as a page banner.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Provisional
- **Recommendation:** Prefer licensed images over fair-use gambles. If relying on thumbnail fair use anywhere, keep images small, directly discussed in adjacent text, and log the four-factor reasoning per image.

### 7. YELLOW — Album covers and label logos are trademarked as well as copyrighted
- **Detail:** Album artwork is copyrighted (to the label or artist), and label logos (the Chess Records logo, Vee-Jay's logo) are trademarks. Nominative fair use allows naming labels in text, but reproducing logos as design elements implies endorsement/sponsorship. Cover art shown for purposes of commentary (e.g., analyzing a Vee-Jay sleeve design) is the standard defensible use; using covers as decorative thumbnails is weaker.
- **Example:** Discussing the design of the 'Introducing... The Beatles' Vee-Jay sleeve with the cover shown small = commentary. Using the Chess logo as the page's section icon = trademark risk.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Provisional
- **Recommendation:** Do not use label logos as graphic design elements. Show cover art only where directly discussed, at reduced size, with label credit. Clear any merch or logo-adjacent uses with counsel.

### 8. YELLOW — Right of publicity: Illinois protects deceased personalities for 50 years
- **Detail:** Illinois's Right of Publicity Act (765 ILCS 1075) gives a deceased individual's identity (name, likeness, voice) 50 years of post-mortem protection, enforceable by heirs. Editorial/informational uses (a history page about Muddy Waters) are generally protected expression, but commercial uses (merchandise, advertising, sponsored content trading on a likeness) are not. Several constellation figures died within the last 50 years (e.g., Koko Taylor d. 2009; Otis Rush d. 2018; Frankie Knuckles d. 2014).
- **Example:** A biographical page about Koko Taylor with a licensed photo = editorial use, low risk. A CTL t-shirt with her likeness = needs heir permission.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Recommendation:** Keep the flagship page strictly editorial/informational. Any future merch, ads, or sponsored content using artists' names/likenesses needs heir clearance — build that into the marketing-system track's planning now.

### 9. YELLOW — Oral histories and interviews: get permission; don't assume the interviewer owns the words
- **Detail:** Interviewees generally hold copyright in their own spoken words; the interviewer/recorder holds rights in the recording. Publishing quote excerpts from the Salkind house oral histories, radio interviews, or documentary footage requires checking each collection's access/use terms. The CBMR's Salkind collection notes audio is available online with transcripts pending — availability to listen is not permission to republish.
- **Example:** Quoting a Salkind interviewee's two sentences with attribution for commentary = likely fair use. Transcribing and publishing whole interviews = needs the collection's permission.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Provisional
- **Sources:** ['https://digitalcommons.colum.edu/house/index.12.html']
- **Recommendation:** For any substantial interview quotation, contact the holding institution (CBMR, Smithsonian, etc.) for use terms. For new CTL-commissioned oral histories, use written release forms assigning CTL the needed rights at the time of recording.

### 10. GREEN — Public-domain and openly-licensed image pools to prefer
- **Detail:** Usable without licensing friction: (1) U.S. federal works (NMAAHC/Smithsonian Open Access — check each object's terms; many are CC0); (2) Library of Congress prints/photographs with 'no known restrictions' statements; (3) pre-1930 published photographs whose copyrights were not renewed (verify via the Catalog of Copyright Entries — do not assume); (4) Wikimedia Commons files with verified license tags and source chains. Note: 'public domain' claims on aggregator sites are frequently wrong — verify at the holding institution.
- **Example:** The NMAAHC's Dorsey rehearsal piano object page is a Smithsonian holding — object photography terms are stated per-object and must be checked, not assumed CC0.
- **Severity:** Low
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Sources:** ['https://nmaahc.si.edu/object/nmaahc_2015.134.1a-g']
- **Recommendation:** Maintain a rights log (spreadsheet) for every visual asset: file, source URL, rights statement, credit line, verification date. Re-verify before publication; institutions change terms.

### 11. YELLOW — Sheet music and published arrangements (gospel) have their own copyrights
- **Detail:** Dorsey's published gospel sheet music (e.g., 'Take My Hand, Precious Lord,' 1932, via his own publishing) is under copyright for the arrangement/engraving as well as the composition; displaying full sheet-music images on the page = reproduction of a copyrighted work. Short excerpts for analysis of his blues-to-gospel harmonic language are the defensible pattern; full-page displays need the publisher's permission.
- **Example:** Showing two bars of a Dorsey arrangement to illustrate the blues inflection in gospel harmony, with commentary = fair-use-shaped. Scanning and posting the full octavo = infringement.
- **Severity:** Medium
- **Grade:** Provisional
- **Recommendation:** Treat sheet music like lyrics: excerpt minimally, analyze directly, attribute. The 2006 Pilgrim fire makes surviving Dorsey manuscripts especially sensitive — do not reproduce manuscript images found online without verifying the holder's terms.

### 12. Process recommendation — build a pre-publication rights checklist into the flagship workflow
