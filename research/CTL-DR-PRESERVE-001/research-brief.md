# The Black Memory Emergency — Research Brief

> **CULTURETECHLENS** · *Culture, Clearly Seen.* · **Black Cultural Intelligence**

**Project:** CTL-DR-PRESERVE-001 · **Date:** 2026-09-28
**Corpus:** 84 canonical collections · 119 entities · 70 timeline events · 78 relationships (77 Verified · 1 Provisional) · 304 sources

---

## Executive summary

Black cultural memory in the United States is not primarily threatened by a lack of collecting — it is threatened by everything that happens *after* collecting. This synthesis of six research tracks finds that the memory chain breaks hardest at **cataloging** (uncataloged backlogs make saved collections unfindable and undefendable), that **ownership changes** — bankruptcy sales, corporate strategy pivots — are the fastest-acting destroyer of Black archives, and that the **private collector's mortality** is the single most time-sensitive risk class: estates disperse, garages burn, and hard drives die — most often with no succession plan and no institutional relationship in place. Family and community succession is the norm; external custody is one option among others, never the default.

The good news is structural: when the chain works — the Johnson Publishing Company rescue ($30M consortium, 2019), the burial-records rescue (140,000+ records, 2022), the Separate Cinema acquisition (2025) — it works because someone acted *before* the worst stage. The emergency is therefore not mysterious. It is a triage problem, and this project delivers the triage framework.

**Headline numbers:**

- **84 canonical collections** mapped across six tracks (deduplicated from 91 track-level records — 32 institutional + 29 community + 21 digital + 5 framework + 4 risk; 6 cross-track merges absorbing 13 records into 6).
- **35 documented risk cases** across 14 risk types; 12 types have documented cases. Two types — *building demolition destroying records* and *domain expiration* — returned **no documented case**: recorded here as explicit negative findings, not as safety.
- **10-stage memory chain** audited: CATALOGED is the worst-supported stage by hard evidence; DISCOVERABLE the thinnest-evidence stage overall; CONNECTED the second-thinnest — which is this project's rationale.
- **8 worked examples** through the new priority framework, including two intervention successes (DJ Screw, Mother Bethel) that illustrate tier movement retrospectively.

## Central question

*Where does Black cultural memory actually break — and what should be saved first?*

---

## Methodology and evidence model

Six tracks — institutional repositories, community-held and private collections, digital collections and platforms, a 35-case risk register, a prioritization framework, and a 10-stage memory-chain analysis — researched independently on the public web (2026-09-27 to 2026-09-28), then synthesized with mandatory cross-track deduplication.

**Evidence grades** (applied to every substantive claim): **Verified** (primary or corroborated reporting) · **Provisional** (single-source or inferential) · **Disputed** (sources conflict; both preserved) · **Unresolved** (not knowable from public sources; recorded as a gap, not an assumption). Where tracks disagreed on a grade, the weaker grade was kept. Where sources conflicted on point values (JPC's 4M vs. 4.5M images; Harsh's 70,000 vs. 80,000+ books; Moorland-Spingarn's 17,000 vs. 18,000+ linear feet), the documented range is preserved and the conflict noted — never averaged away.

**Privacy constraint (absolute):** no private street addresses, no precise home/storage locations of private collections, no sensitive personal information. All locations normalized to city/institution level. Every record was re-audited; the build's automated privacy scan returned zero flags.

**What this is not:** not a census of all Black archives (the corpus is Chicago-anchored by design, national where documented); not a substitute for on-the-ground archival survey; not a claim about materials the tracks could not see — which is most of them. Coverage skew disclosure: roughly 62% of the 84 mapped collections are held by universities, major museums, public libraries, or federal institutions — a method-induced skew of web-visible sources, stated here and in `gaps.md`; community-controlled holdings are the known thin edge of this corpus, not the thin edge of Black memory itself.

## Key findings (ranked)

### 1. Cataloging is where the chain breaks worst.
The AFRO-American Newspapers chain archive — ~3 million photos, 130 years of a family-owned Black press — sits in inaccessible state storage with ~5% processed since October 2022. The BMRC's 2009–2011 survey found Chicago's Black collections systematically unprocessed or underprocessed. The Baltimore Collection arrived as 53 "found objects" with no photographers, sitters, dates, or provenance. Uncataloged collections cannot be found, used, cited in grant applications, or defended in budget fights. **Cataloging is the highest-leverage intervention point in the entire system.** (Verified; AFRO current-state portion Provisional per 2026-09-28 re-audit)

### 2. Ownership change is the fastest destroyer.
The Johnson Publishing Company archive reached a bankruptcy auction in July 2019 and was saved only by a four-foundation consortium paying just under $30M. BET Digital permanently deleted its entire written news archive in September 2026 as a "video-first pivot" under Paramount — no warning, writers losing their only copies. MTV News's archive was wiped in 2024. Vee-Jay Records' masters passed through a 1966 bankruptcy into undocumented custody. **Corporate-held Black memory is revocable memory.** (Verified)

### 3. The collector's mortality is the ticking clock.
John Simmons's life's-work negatives burned in a friend's garage during a move (2025). Teenie Harris's ~80,000 negatives survived only because Carnegie Museum of Art bought them from the family in 2001, three years after his death. Octogenarian photojournalists Ovie Carter and Bob Black hold personal negative archives with no documented succession. John Duke Kisch's 37,000-item Separate Cinema Archive worked — acquired by the Lucas Museum in 2025 — but it is the exception that proves the rule. **Every private collection without a succession plan is a countdown.** (Verified; Simmons fire and Carter/Black disposition Provisional)

### 4. Platform memory is not memory.
MySpace lost all 2003–2015 uploads (~50M songs). Vine shut down in 2017. Tumblr purged in 2018. Twitter's 2023 API paywall broke the dominant research infrastructure for Black digital expression, and Pew measured the decay: 18% of 2020–2022 #BlackLivesMatter tweets were already gone by 2023. Black Archives Co — a 13-year archive of Black life — lives on Instagram with no documented institutional backup. The Internet Archive is the only systemic backstop, and it is a nonprofit. (Verified; follower figure Provisional)

### 5. Rescue happens at the decision point — usually by communities, not frameworks.
JPC (2019, ~$30M via a four-foundation consortium), the Illinois burial records (2022, 140,000+ records via a 10-person community team), King and King funeral-home records (2024, recovered before demolition), the DJ Screw Collection (family donation from 2011, now cataloged and partially digitized), Mother Bethel AME records (2013 move to Presbyterian Historical Society; digitized 2021). **Every rescue in this corpus happened because someone acted at the decision point, not after the loss — and the actors were communities, families, and coalitions, not triage frameworks.** The priority framework exists to manufacture more such decision points; it cannot claim credit for rescues that predate it. (Verified)

### 7. Rights uncertainty is the silent blocker.
Thirty-three of the 84 mapped collections carry undocumented rights status, and rights uncertainty blocks digitization and reuse even where materials are physically safe: *Eyes on the Prize* vanished from circulation for roughly a decade over expired music licenses, and Duke University Libraries' 2013 Copyright Office filing names a Jim Crow-era Black-life image collection as an orphan-works case. Rights is arguably the top practical barrier to making Black collections accessible — it is documented at record level throughout this corpus and stated here as a synthesis-level finding, with a dedicated investigation queued in `gaps.md`. (Verified)

### 8. Two risks show no documented case — and that is a finding, not comfort.
*Building demolition destroying records* and *domain expiration* returned no documented case in this pass. Both are recorded as **explicit negative findings**: uncataloged materials destroyed in demolitions leave no paper trail, and the exposure surface for domain loss (small archives, personal sites, no IT staff) is real. Absence of evidence here is evidence of invisibility. (Unresolved by design)

## The memory chain: stage-by-stage verdict

| Stage | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1. MEMORY CREATED | Strongest | The memory exists in abundance |
| 2. RECORDED | Second-strongest | Broad documentation activity |
| 3. COLLECTED | Strong | Many collections in private/institutional hands |
| 4. CATALOGED | **Worst-supported (hard evidence)** | AFRO ~5% processed; BMRC underprocessing; Baltimore orphans |
| 5. PRESERVED | Mixed | Major rescues alongside active loss (fires, mold, vinegar syndrome) |
| 6. DIGITIZED | Patchy | Harsh in-person-only until 2024; HistoryMakers conversion incomplete |
| 7. CONNECTED | Second-thinnest | The project's rationale |
| 8. DISCOVERABLE | **Thinnest overall** | Portal coverage partial; most holdings invisible to search |
| 9. USED | Strong where discoverable | Shorefront → reparations; Freedmen's Bureau → genealogy |
| 10. TRANSMITTED | Strong | Exhibitions, curricula, creator reuse |

The chain's shape is the project's thesis: **abundance at the start, breakage in the middle, this project aimed at stages 7–8.**

## Priority framework and top-tier examples

The framework scores **Risk × Significance × Uniqueness**, gated by **Feasibility**, into four tiers: **Tier 1 Emergency** (act within 12 months) · **Tier 2 Urgent** (1–3 years) · **Tier 3 Planned** (3–5 year pipeline) · **Tier 4 Monitor** (ongoing). Provisional assessments are allowed but must be labeled and revisited. (Full framework: `priority-framework.md`.)

**Tier 1 (Emergency):** surviving Oscar Micheaux film elements — single/partial nitrate prints, active decomposition, scattered holdings (EX-07, Verified). Retrospective Tier 1: the JPC archive at its 2019 auction — now Tier 4 after rescue.

**Tier 2 (Urgent):** the Obsidian/Defender digitization backlog; BlackPast.org (key-person risk after its founder's 2025 death); Knoxville College records (Provisional — records-specific threat inferential); retrospective Tier 2: Mother Bethel at its 2013 move-or-lose point — now Tier 4.

**Tier 3 (Planned):** the Black Archives of Mid-America's 18-box Muhammad Speaks run (Provisional — fragile newsprint, completeness unverified).

**Tier 4 (Monitor — interventions that worked):** the DJ Screw Collection and Mother Bethel records, both moved down from Tier 2 by documented action. **The retrospective examples illustrate how tier movement works; they predate the framework and cannot validate it — validation requires prospective use.**

## What we know / think we know / don't know / next

**We know:** the chain breaks at cataloging; ownership change destroys fastest; collector mortality is the clock; platform memory is not memory; rescue happens when someone acts at the decision point.

**We think we know:** the scale of the private-collector universe (WVON airchecks, Carter/Black negatives, church archives) dwarfs the institutional one, but it is mostly unmapped — Provisional.

**We don't know:** the current status of the Obsidian Collection Archives' digitization totals and preservation-grade backups; the custodianship of the Muhammad Speaks morgue, the Chicago Whip morgue, Binga/Supreme Life corporate records, Vee-Jay masters, and above all the **A.A. Rayner & Sons records** — the single highest-value unresolved lead in the corpus (see `gaps.md`).

**Next investigations:** A.A. Rayner records (privacy-constrained inquiry); Obsidian current-status verification; Vee-Jay masters custody; the smaller BMRC repositories the survey couldn't reach; church and funeral-home archives as a class; domain-expiration exposure among small Black archives.
