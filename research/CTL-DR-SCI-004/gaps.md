# CTL-DR-SCI-004 — Gaps and Unknowns

Consolidated from all five research tracks plus the red-team audit.
These are research leads, not findings.

## Red-team overall assessment

The five tracks are diligent, unusually honest about evidence grades, and strong on the famous-pulpit and civic-leadership stories (tracks b and c are the best-sourced). As a corpus they are publishable as a research base but NOT as settled public history without a harmonization pass: the same facts carry different verdicts in different tracks (Quinn/Provident, Fellowship founding, Pilgrim dates, Wright start year), and a handful of claims are graded Verified on thin or single-source evidence. Structurally, the project reproduces the archive's famous-pulpit bias (~85-90% of named-congregation coverage goes to prominent churches; the storefront tradition that was ~72% of Black churches in 1930 gets aggregate statistics and two or three names), under-covers women as institutional actors beyond four leaders, and is overwhelmingly South Side (1 West Side congregation, 0 North Side, 0 suburbs), overwhelmingly Baptist (10 of 20 named congregations), and entirely survivor-based (zero defunct Black congregations profiled). None of these flaws is fatal — several are already named in the tracks' own gaps sections — but the synthesis must present them as findings, not footnotes, and must not publish Disputed dates or single-source superlatives as settled fact.

## Structural gaps (red-team findings)

### 4. Famous-pulpit bias
- ~20 unique congregations receive dedicated entries across the five tracks; ~17-18 of them are prominent flagship institutions (the 12 'landmark' churches, plus New Friendship, Warren Ave., Greater Mount Hope, Apostolic Church of God, Vernon Park, Greater Salem, Morning Star).
- The storefront tradition — 72% of Black Chicago churches in the 1930 Mays & Nicholson survey — is covered almost entirely as aggregate statistics (Boyd, Noreen, Stronks) with only two or three named storefronts (Traveling Souls Spiritual Church, Healing Water Spiritual Church, from name-lists). No small-congregation pastor gets a biographical entry; no oral histories.
- Coverage ratio is roughly inverted relative to the historical landscape: ~85-90% of named-congregation attention goes to institutions that were, at most, a small minority of Black churches.
- Mitigating factors: track-e exists and is explicit that 'the record is thinnest precisely where the track most wants it'; several gaps sections name the problem. The bias is documented, not hidden.
- Recommendation: In the flagship output, state the inversion quantitatively (72% storefronts vs ~90% flagship coverage) as a headline finding about the archive, not a footnote.

### 5. Missing women
- Track-c is the strongest: 3 of 11 leaders are women (Addie Wyatt, Willie Barrow, Mother Consuella York) — genuinely good coverage of women as civic power-holders.
- Everywhere else women are thin: track-a's 12 congregations name zero women pastors as profiled leaders (Rev. Jennifer Tinsley appears only in passing as a current pastor; Bishop Anne Henning Byfield as a guest preacher). Founding women appear as wives and club members (Bertie Boone, Maggie Walker, Juanita Finnie).
- No Chicago church mother is named anywhere — the Church Mother is covered as an abstract 'practice' (track-e) via Gilkes, with zero named Chicago instances. No deaconess, missionary-society president, or usher-board leader is named.
- Mother Consuella York ('first Black woman ordained into Baptist clergy in Chicago') rests on the ordaining church's site and one community-press account, with the ordination year (1954) single-sourced and her subsequent church unidentified — the 'first' claim is high-stakes and under-evidenced.
- Recommendation: Name the gap explicitly: 'no named church mothers' should be a stated finding, with a research plan (Defender obituaries, church anniversary books, oral histories).

### 6. Missing working-class / everyday-life history
- The project's named leaders are overwhelmingly professional-class: elected officials, presidential appointees, denominational presidents, megachurch pastors, recording artists. The working-class congregant — the domestic worker, packinghouse worker, Pullman porter in the pew — appears only as an aggregate ('migrants,' 'church members').
- Lay auxiliaries (usher boards, nurses guilds, missionary societies, deaconess boards) are described functionally (track-e) but no lay leader is named; no oral-history or memoir source is used in any track.
- Class tension within Black religious life is noted analytically (Boyd's 'dicty old-line churches' vs. storefronts; Best on Pentecostals) but never grounded in a Chicago congregation-level story.
- Economic life beyond flagship development projects (Imani Village, Woodlawn Central, Trinity Square) is thin: no burial societies by name, no church credit-union story (track-b honestly reports none was found), no rent-party or church-anniversary economy quantified — though track-d's 'choir anniversary economy' concept is a promising start.
- Recommendation: Add a working-class layer via Defender 'church news' columns, anniversary-book ads (which list members' occupations), and oral histories (e.g., Harsh Collection holdings like the Wyatt papers).

### 7. Missing communities
- Entire Christian traditions absent: Presbyterian (Black Presbyterian churches), Lutheran, Episcopal, Christian Methodist Episcopal (CME), African Methodist Episcopal Zion, Seventh-day Adventist, Jehovah's Witnesses (a significant Black Chicago presence).
- Pentecostalism is covered only through All Nations (1920s) and the Apostolic Church of God; the Church of God in Christ — the largest Black Pentecostal denomination, 8% of 1965 storefront names — gets only an aggregate entity, no named congregation.
- Catholic coverage is one priest (Fr. Clements) and one parish (Holy Angels); no other Black Catholic parishes or institutions.
- Beyond Christianity: only the Nation of Islam (Temple No. 2 / Mosque Maryam). No coverage of Black Hebrew Israelites, African traditional religions, Bahá'í, or Black-Jewish institutional relations beyond recycled synagogue buildings.
- Recommendation: Add at minimum: one named COGIC congregation, one Black Catholic parish beyond Holy Angels, and one mainline (Presbyterian or Episcopal) congregation to test whether the civic-infrastructure thesis holds outside the Baptist/AME core.

### 8. Denominational balance
- Rough count of the 20 uniquely named congregations: Baptist 10 (Olivet, Liberty, Pilgrim, Fellowship, Ebenezer, Greater Harvest, New Friendship, Greater Mount Hope, Morning Star, Greater Salem), AME 2 (Quinn, Bethel), UMC 1 (St. Mark), UCC 1 (Trinity), Apostolic 2 (Metropolitan Apostolic, Apostolic Church of God), Pentecostal 1 (All Nations), Spiritualist/MSCC 1 (Deliverance), Church of God (Anderson) 1 (Vernon Park), Congregational 1 (Warren Avenue). Plus NOI 1.
- Baptists are 50% of named congregations — roughly proportionate to their historical share of Black Chicago churches, but the Baptist entries are all large flagships; small Baptist storefronts (the majority Baptist experience per Noreen's 69% figure) get no entries.
- AME is under-weighted relative to its historic institutional weight (oldest congregation, denominational newspaper, bishops as civic leaders). Methodists overall (AME+UMC = 3) trail Baptists 10-to-3.
- Track-c's leader set is more balanced (AME, Baptist, UCC, Pentecostal, Catholic, Church of God), which partially offsets the congregational skew.
- Recommendation: When selecting additional congregations, weight toward AME, COGIC/Pentecostal, and Catholic to rebalance.

### 9. Geographic bias
- Of ~20 named congregations, ~18 are South Side: Bronzeville/Grand Boulevard/Douglas corridor dominates (Quinn, Olivet, Pilgrim, Deliverance, Metropolitan, Fellowship, Ebenezer, Bethel, Greater Harvest, All Nations), plus Englewood (New Friendship, Greater Mount Hope), Roseland (Trinity), Chatham (St. Mark), Woodlawn (Apostolic Church of God), South Shore (Mosque Maryam), Pullman (Imani Village).
- West Side: exactly one congregation (Warren Avenue Congregational, E. Garfield Park) — and its building now houses a different church, i.e., even the one West Side entry is a closed-congregation story. No coverage of Black churches in Austin, North Lawndale, or West Garfield Park, where much of Black Chicago has lived since the 1960s.
- North Side: zero. Suburbs (south suburban Black church growth): zero.
- The geography reproduces the Black Metropolis / Great Migration map and freezes it there; the post-1960s westward and southward movement of Black Chicago is institutionally invisible.
- Recommendation: Add a West Side module: at minimum 2-3 congregations in Austin/North Lawndale/Garfield Park, where the storefront tradition continued and megachurches later grew.

### 10. Survivorship bias
- Zero defunct Black congregations are profiled. Every named congregation is presented as a going concern (or, in Pilgrim's case, a congregation continuing across the street from its ruin).
- The only 'lost' institutions are buildings, not congregations: Pilgrim's burned 1891 building, and the two synagogue predecessors (Kehilath Anshe Ma'ariv, Isaiah Temple).
- Track-a's gaps section is honest that 'post-peak histories (decline/stabilization eras, 1980s-present) are thin,' but no track investigates a closure.
- This matters analytically: churches lost to the Dan Ryan Expressway construction, CHA/urban-renewal demolition, arson, and membership migration south and west are part of the civic-infrastructure story — infrastructure is also demolished.
- Recommendation: Investigate as named cases: (a) Black congregations displaced by Dan Ryan Expressway construction and Bronzeville urban renewal (1950s-60s); (b) Pilgrim Baptist's congregational status and membership since the 2006 fire; (c) congregations that followed members to Chatham, Auburn Gresham, South Shore, and the south suburbs — and those that did not survive the move; (d) the Metropolitan Community Church's trajectory after the 2003 sale (does the predecessor congregation still exist?); (e) First Church of Deliverance's post-Cobbs era.
