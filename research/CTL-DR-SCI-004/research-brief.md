## Executive summary

Black churches in Chicago were never only houses of worship. Across 180 years — from Quinn Chapel's 1840s prayer meetings to Imani Village's 2025 senior residence — congregations functioned as migration reception centers, movement headquarters, voter-mobilization machines, property owners, broadcast studios, record labels, and mutual-aid societies. This research package assembles the evidence for that claim: **62 entities, 207 evidence-graded claims, 168 timeline events (1844–2026), 28 verified relationships, and 116 sources**, organized across five tracks and subjected to a full red-team audit.

The corpus is unusually honest about what it can and cannot prove. Founding-date disputes are systemic (Olivet 1850/1861, Pilgrim 1916/1922, Fellowship 1950/1958, Greater Harvest 1912/1920, Liberty 1918/1919, NCGCC 1932/1933, Wright's Trinity start 1971/1972) — a finding in itself about how congregational memory works. Where tracks disagreed, the merged package takes the conservative grade; fourteen red-team corrections were applied during harmonization.

## Key findings

**1. The church as migration infrastructure is the best-documented civic function.** Olivet Baptist's Great Migration reception work, the 1918 $80,000 purchase of the 3101 S. King Drive building, and Quinn Chapel's abolition-era organizing (HABS-documented) form a continuous line: the church as the institution migrants encountered first.

**2. Gospel music was a church product before it was an industry.** Dorsey's 1932 Pilgrim appointment, the Dorsey–Frye–Martin network at Ebenezer, the 1932 Dorsey House of Music, First Church of Deliverance's 1935 gospel radio broadcasts, and Fellowship's radio→LP→label pipeline show congregations as training grounds, publishers, broadcasters, and distributors. The "birthplace of gospel" primacy dispute (Pilgrim vs. Ebenezer, 1931) remains genuinely unresolved.

**3. The pulpit-to-politics pipeline ran through verifiable public offices.** Carey (elected/appointed office), J.H. Jackson (National Baptist Convention presidency), Evans (PUSH founding board chair), Barrow, Brazier, Wyatt (labor + church), and the CCCO/TWO/Operation Breadbasket nexus are documented in public records — not inferred influence.

**4. The everyday church is under-documented by design.** Storefront congregations were an estimated 72% of Black churches in 1930 (Mays & Nicholson — itself unverified since), yet the scholarly record is phenomenon-level, not congregation-level. Church mothers, missionary societies, usher boards, and deaconesses ran the institutions; almost none are named in the record. This absence is structural, not accidental.

**5. Property was power.** From Quinn Chapel's 19th-century buildings to Imani Village's $13.9M city-financed development (2025), church real estate anchored neighborhoods through disinvestment — but development figures vary across sources and most rest on institutional communications, not city records.

## Methodology

Five parallel research tracks (landmark congregations; civic infrastructure; pastoral civic leadership; gospel music/church side; storefronts and everyday religious life), each bound to the CTL evidence standard: every major claim records claim, evidence, source, primary/secondary status, publication date, uncertainty, and contradictory evidence, graded Verified / Provisional / Disputed / Unresolved. A sixth pass (red team) audited all tracks against a 14-point checklist, found 12 duplicate entities and 10 date disputes, and issued 14 harmonization corrections applied in this package. Sensitivity rule throughout: public institutional history only; no doctrinal judgments; no private details about living clergy beyond public roles.

## What we know

- The institutional continuity: a small set of congregations (Quinn, Olivet, Pilgrim, Fellowship, Trinity, Deliverance, Ebenezer) anchor 180 years of Black Chicago's civic life, with documented building, membership, and leadership histories.
- Churches served as documented infrastructure for migration reception, civil-rights organizing (CCCO, SCLC Chicago campaign connections where verified), voter mobilization (1982–83, 1992 drives), and gospel music production and distribution.
- Women — Wyatt, Barrow, Consuella York (ordination claim thin), church mothers as a class — carried institutional weight disproportionate to their archival footprint.

## What we think we know

- Founding dates for most landmark congregations (nearly all rest on anniversary histories; treat as institutional memory, not settled fact).
- Membership superlatives (Olivet's 20,000; Deliverance's 9,000; "first Black church to televise" 1953) — widely repeated, thinly sourced.
- The scale of the storefront-church world (the 72% figure is 95 years old and unreplicated).

## What remains unknown

See gaps.md: Defender-archives mining not done; denominational minutes not consulted; NCGCC incorporation records not located; NOI transaction dates uncorroborated; zero defunct congregations profiled; no named church mothers recovered; West Side and suburban coverage thin; no documented Black church-founded credit union found.

## What CTL should investigate next

1. **Chicago Defender archives (1910–1970):** church announcements, choir programs, and broadcast schedules — the primary-source backbone for everything currently Provisional.
2. **Denominational minutes and church anniversary books** (AME, National Baptist, UCC, COGIC) to arbitrate the founding-date disputes.
3. **DuSable Museum and Chicago History Museum** congregational collections for the storefront and church-mother record.
4. **NCGCC incorporation records** and the Dorsey papers for the 1932/1933 dispute and choir-primacy question.
5. **Oral histories** with church mothers, usher boards, and missionary societies — the record will not build itself.
6. **West Side congregations and defunct churches** — the two largest geographic and survivorship gaps in the corpus.
